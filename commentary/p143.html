Introduction — Crossing the Boundaries of Prejudice

At the end of June, A.D. 27, as the opposition of the religious leaders grew fiercer, Jesus and the twelve left Jerusalem and went north toward Samaria. They taught both Jews and Samaritans at Bethel, Gophna, Ephraim, Arimathea, and Thamna. With the exception of Judas, the apostles succeeded in overcoming much of their prejudice against the Samaritans. (143:0.1, 143:0.2)

The settings of this paper are the Greek cities of Archelais and Phasaelis, and Sychar and Mount Gerizim in Samaria. For the first time the apostles worked among gentiles alone, and then they went in among the Samaritans, with whom the Jews had been at odds for more than six hundred years. The paper shows how faith grows sturdy among strangers. (143:1.1, 143:4.1)

I. Preaching at Archelais — The Gospel of Courage

The Greeks and Romans dismissed the apostles’ message as a teaching fit only for weaklings and slaves. Pagan religion, they said, was better because it built a strong and aggressive character. Philip brought this objection straight to the Master. (143:1.2)

Jesus began by declaring that his mission was to do the Father’s will and to reveal the Father’s loving character. He added that even divine love has its severe disciplines, and that the Father rules the universe of universes by the compelling power of his love. (143:1.4)

Truth is a liberating revelation, but love is the supreme relationship. (143:1.4)

Jesus told them to give up any idea that service in the kingdom would be dull or easy. The Paradise ascent is the supreme adventure of all time, and to give one’s life alone for truth takes a deeper courage than to die beside one’s comrades. He taught that courage, too, has its levels. (143:1.6, 143:1.7)

The text notes that this was one of the rare addresses in which Jesus showed marked emotion. From that day the apostles’ message took on a new note of courageous dominance, and they preached less of negative virtues and passive injunctions. Against the view that nonviolence is weakness, this teaching reminds us that gentleness can be the highest kind of courage. (143:1.8, 143:1.9)

II. Lesson on Self-Mastery — From Self-Denial to Self-Mastery

Jesus was a perfect example of self-control: when reviled he did not revile, and when suffering he did not threaten. When Andrew asked how John’s self-denial differed from Jesus’ self-control, Jesus drew a clear contrast between the two ways. (143:2.1, 143:2.2)

Self-mastery is the measure of man’s moral nature and the indicator of his spiritual development. (143:2.3)

Jesus pointed out that people blame their wrongdoing on the influence of the evil one when in fact they have been led astray by their own natural tendencies. Salvation comes not by works of the flesh but by the regeneration of the spirit, and cleansing oneself from evil becomes not a duty but an exalted privilege. The secret of self-mastery lies in faith in the indwelling spirit, which works by love. (143:2.5, 143:2.6, 143:2.7)

the fruits of the spirit are the essence of the highest type of enjoyable and ennobling self-control, even the heights of terrestrial mortal attainment—true self-mastery. (143:2.8)

III. Diversion and Relaxation — Leaving Problems to Solve Them

By this time the apostles were under great nervous and emotional strain from living together, from friction with John’s disciples, and from contact with gentiles. When Andrew came to him almost beside himself, Jesus declined to intervene, saying that such tangled problems could not be solved by talk. Instead he took the twelve up Mount Sartaba for three days of rest and asked that their problems not be mentioned. (143:3.1, 143:3.2)

On the second day Jesus gave each of them a nonreligious topic to talk about, and at lunch he even omitted the spoken blessing. Coming down on the third day, they found that many of their pressing problems had been the product of exaggerated fears and magnified anxieties. After this holiday their relations with John’s followers also greatly improved. The lesson: sometimes what a weary community needs is not more meetings but rest. (143:3.4, 143:3.6, 143:3.7)

IV. The Jews and the Samaritans — Six Hundred Years of Hostility

The ill feeling between the two peoples dated from about 700 B.C., when the Assyrian king Sargon carried off the people of the northern kingdom and settled other peoples in their place. Religious hostility began after the return from exile, when the Samaritans hindered the rebuilding of Jerusalem; their temple on Mount Gerizim, built with Alexander’s permission, was later destroyed by John Hyrcanus. (143:4.1, 143:4.2)

The apostles had not objected to preaching in gentile cities, but the words “Let us go into Samaria” put their loyalty to a severe test. Yet the personal loyalty built through more than a year with Jesus proved stronger than old prejudice. Prejudice sometimes gives way first not to logic but to loyalty toward someone we love. (143:4.3)

V. The Woman of Sychar — Living Water at the Well

At Jacob’s well the weary Jesus asked Nalda, a woman of Sychar who had come to draw water, for a drink. In those days it was thought improper for a man to speak to a woman in public, and still more for a Jew to speak to a Samaritan. Jesus answered that if she understood, she would rather ask him for living water. (143:5.1, 143:5.2)

Everyone who drinks of this water will thirst again, but whosoever drinks of the water of the living spirit shall never thirst. (143:5.3)

Nalda mistook his kindness for familiarity, and she came to her senses when he said, “Go, call your husband.” The text explains that she had been unjustly cast aside by her husband and was in desperate circumstances. She repented, but quickly turned the conversation to the dispute over where to worship. The text sees in this how many people use theological discussion to sidestep the question of their own salvation. (143:5.4, 143:5.5)

Your salvation comes not from knowing how others should worship or where but by receiving into your own heart this living water which I am offering you even now. (143:5.6)

When Nalda spoke of the coming Deliverer, Jesus said, “I who speak to you am he.” The text stresses that this was his first open declaration of his divinity and sonship, and that it was given to a Samaritan woman of questionable morals in the eyes of men. In the divine eyes she was more sinned against than sinning, a soul longing for salvation. (143:5.7, 143:5.8)

The text adds that Jesus never told her she had had five husbands; under his look and manner her whole past life came before her mind of itself. The narrator then observes that Christianity has been built more around the death of Jesus than around his life, and that the world should take more interest in his happy, God-revealing life. (143:5.11, 143:5.13)

VI. The Samaritan Revival — Fields White for the Harvest

When Nalda brought the people of Sychar out to see him, the hungry apostles urged Jesus to eat first. He answered that his food was to do the will of the One who sent him, and that the fields were already white for the harvest. His saying that they would reap where others had labored referred to the preaching of John the Baptist. (143:6.1)

My meat is to do the will of Him who sent me and to accomplish His work. (143:6.1)

On the first night at Mount Gerizim, Jesus did not rebuke the apostles about the scene at the well but spoke on the central realities of the kingdom. The narrator warns that in religion fact easily usurps the place of truth, and says that the fact of the cross is not the central truth of the religion that may be drawn from Jesus’ life and teachings. Jesus wanted people to see God as a Father-friend, and he repeatedly stressed that love is the greatest relationship in the universe. (143:6.3, 143:6.4)

They stayed at Mount Gerizim until the end of August, spending the days carrying the gospel to the cities of Samaria. This harvest prepared the way for the remarkable work Philip would later do in the region after the persecutions scattered the believers. (143:6.6)

VII. Teachings about Prayer and Worship — Looking to the One to Serve the Many

At the evening conferences on Mount Gerizim, Jesus taught that true religion is the act of an individual soul in its self-conscious relations with the Creator; organized religion is man’s attempt to socialize that worship. He also said that life is healthy when opposites alternate and balance each other. (143:7.2, 143:7.3)

Its definition of worship as effortless attention and true soul rest invites readers worn out by busy religious lives to see worship anew as rest. (143:7.7, 143:7.9)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 143 is a paper of crossing boundaries. Faced with gentile ridicule, the apostles learned the gospel of courage; instead of self-denial they learned the liberty of self-mastery; and they overcame their prejudice against Samaritans. And Jesus, disregarding social propriety, reached out to one woman’s soul and showed that the way of salvation lies not in the place of worship but in a heart that receives living water. (143:1.9, 143:5.6)
