Introduction — The Long Age Between Fear and Love

Paper 90 asks who conducted the primitive observances described in the three preceding papers. Its author, a Melchizedek of Nebadon, explains that religious observance progressed from placation, avoidance, exorcism, and coercion to sacrifice, atonement, and redemption, and that as ritual grew more complex it was inevitably dominated by medicine men, shamans, and priests. (90:0.1)

In time the spirit world came to be regarded as unresponsive to the ordinary mortal. Once people believed that only exceptional men and women could catch the ear of the gods, religion entered a secondhand phase. The author adds that most of the organized religious systems on Urantia today are still passing through this level. (90:0.2)

always does a medicine man, a shaman, or a priest intervene between the religionist and the object of worship. (90:0.2)

The paper’s whole frame is set out in the last paragraph of its introduction. Evolutionary religion is born of a simple, all-powerful fear that surges through the mind before the unknown, and it eventually reaches the realization of an all-powerful love—the limitless affection of the Universal Father for the sons of the universe. Between that beginning and that consummation lie the long ages of the shamans, who presumed to stand between man and God as intermediaries. (90:0.3)

Evolutionary religion is born of a simple and all-powerful fear (90:0.3)

I. The First Shamans — The Medicine Men

The shaman was the ranking medicine man, the ceremonial fetishman, and the focus personality for all the practices of evolutionary religion. In many groups he outranked the war chief, marking the beginning of the church’s domination of the state. Often the office was hereditary. (90:1.1)

Because anything abnormal was ascribed to spirit possession, any striking mental or physical abnormality qualified a person to become a medicine man. Many of the men were epileptic and many of the women hysteric, and the author holds that these two types account for a good deal of ancient inspiration and possession. Most shamans sincerely believed they were possessed by spirits, but many were shrewd and able tricksters. (90:1.2, 90:1.3, 90:1.4)

In the end a shaman’s reputation rested on apparent achievement. If he failed and could not offer a plausible alibi, he was demoted or killed. The result was a paradox. (90:1.5)

Thus the honest shamans early perished; only the shrewd actors survived. (90:1.5)

Shamanism took the direction of tribal affairs out of the hands of the old and the strong and lodged it in the hands of the shrewd, the clever, and the farsighted. This shift of power from strength to wit was itself a kind of change. (90:1.6)

II. Shamanistic Practices — Seekers of Revelation and Their Price

Spirit conjuring was a precise and highly complicated procedure, comparable to church rituals conducted in an ancient tongue. Mankind sought superhuman help—revelation—very early, and people believed the shaman actually received it. Shamans relied heavily on the power of suggestion, but almost always negative suggestion; positive suggestion has been used only in very recent times. Their chief function was not so much healing disease as knowing and controlling the hazards of living. (90:2.1)

The same art was called white art when practiced by priests or shamans and black art when practiced by others. Later the witch became associated with the devil, setting the stage for comparatively recent outbreaks of religious intolerance. Shamans believed that chance revealed the will of the spirits and so cast lots, a habit that survives in children’s “counting-out” rhymes. (90:2.2, 90:2.3, 90:2.4)

That which was serious business to primitive man has survived as a diversion of the modern child. (90:2.4)

Astrology, dream interpreting, and the rain makers or weather shamans all come down from ancient times. The Caesars banished the astrologers again and again, yet popular belief always brought them back, and thousands still believe in lucky and unlucky stars. In every age, though, true prophets and teachers arose to expose shamanism—among them the Shawnee Tenskwatawa, who predicted the eclipse of 1806 and denounced the vices of the white man. (90:2.5, 90:2.6, 90:2.7, 90:2.9)

Shamanism was not confined to any one region. The Greeks believed in oracles, the Chinese used magic against demons, and shamanism flourished in India; across much of the world it was abandoned only recently. The paper invites us to read it not as the failing of one people but as a stage the whole human race has passed through. (90:2.8)

The shamans built their reputations as voices of God by sprinkling and naming the newborn and presiding over burials. Through fees they grew wealthy, became the original aristocracy exempt from tribal restrictions, and suppressed rivals by branding them witches. Even so, the author fairly adds that the veneration of shamanism shows the premium mankind placed on wisdom. (90:2.10, 90:2.11, 90:2.12, 90:2.13)

Primitive man regarded the shaman as a necessary evil; he feared him but did not love him. (90:2.13)

III. The Shamanic Theory of Disease and Death — From Ghosts to Natural Causes

Ancient man believed that he and his material surroundings responded directly to the whims of ghosts and spirits, so his religion was almost wholly concerned with material affairs. Since all disease and death were regarded as spirit phenomena, it was inevitable that the shaman, besides being priest, should also labor as doctor and surgeon. (90:3.1, 90:3.2)

The primitive mind may be handicapped by lack of facts, but it is for all that logical. (90:3.3)

These theories bore directly on human lives. Some primitive peoples still kill someone whenever a nonviolent death occurs, the medicine man naming the guilty party; obscure deaths were laid to witchcraft and a supposed witch executed. The note that ancient coroner’s inquests, which dissected the body to settle the cause of death, saved many such lives shows that the search for facts is itself a way of saving people. (90:3.6, 90:3.7)

This list is a ladder of thought by which mankind has tried to explain suffering. The observation that the fourth theory—disease as punishment for personal or racial sin—still prevails among reputedly civilized groups invites us to examine how we look upon a sick neighbor. An understanding of old age and contagion is gradually erasing the fear of ghosts and gods as the authors of human misery. (90:3.8, 90:3.9)

Evolution unerringly achieves its end (90:3.10)

The author explains that evolution instills a superstitious fear of the unknown as the scaffolding for the God concept, and then, once revelation has brought a better comprehension of Deity, sets in motion forces of thought that inexorably remove the scaffolding. The decline of superstition is not a crisis of faith but the taking down of a scaffold that has served its purpose. (90:3.10)

IV. Medicine under the Shamans — Faith and Secrecy

The entire life of ancient men was prophylactic, and their religion was in no small measure a technique of disease prevention. They had unbounded faith in their remedies, and that faith was itself a powerful remedy. The author observes that the faith needed to recover under an ancient shaman was not much different from the faith needed to experience healing at the hands of some of his later-day, nonscientific successors. (90:4.1, 90:4.2)

It was a great advance in humanitarianism when the evolution of shamancraft produced priests and medicine men who consented to treat disease. (90:4.3)

For long ages primitive tribes feared the sick and shamefully neglected them. Later the whole clan would crowd into the sickroom to help the shaman howl the disease ghosts away, and sometimes a woman diagnosed while a man treated. Through mistaken theories—such as the belief that every animal-caused disease had its beneficent plant remedy—mankind slowly groped its way toward real medicines. (90:4.3, 90:4.7)

The Greeks were the first to evolve truly rational methods of treating the sick, and both Greeks and Egyptians received their medical knowledge from the Euphrates valley. Many secret remedies lost their power once they became known. (90:4.9)

secrecy has always been essential to the successful practice of fraud and superstition. (90:4.9)

V. Priests and Rituals — A Millstone, and Leaders Who Pointed the Way

For the savage, the essence of ritual was the perfection of its performance. Done correctly, it compelled the spirits; done wrongly, it angered the gods. So the early shamans inevitably evolved into a priesthood trained to direct meticulous ritual, and for tens of thousands of years endless rituals were an intolerable burden on every act of life. (90:5.1)

Ritual is the technique of sanctifying custom (90:5.2)

Priests specialized according to their natural talents, and when religion became institutionalized they claimed to “hold the keys of heaven.” This claim shows the secondhand religion described in the introduction at its height: the authority of the intermediary who stands between man and God. (90:5.5)

Words like amen and selah, pilgrimages, purification rites, and prayer, song, and responsive reading all belong to this lineage of ritual. Interestingly, the habit of swearing is explained as a prostitution of the old ritual repetition of holy names. Priests sought to awe the people with an ancient tongue and magical passes in order to enhance their own authority. (90:5.3, 90:5.6)

The great danger in all this is that the ritual tends to become a substitute for religion. (90:5.6)

The author’s judgment is balanced. The priesthoods delayed scientific development and hindered spiritual progress, yet they contributed to the stabilization of civilization and to certain kinds of culture. (90:5.7)

It is not denied that the priests have been a millstone about the neck of the races, but the true religious leaders have been invaluable in pointing the way to higher and better realities. (90:5.8)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 90 shows that on religion’s road from fear to love there was a long age when intermediaries stood in the way. Shamans and priests laid burdens on people through trickery and monopoly, but they also left a tradition of caring for the sick and honoring wisdom. For today’s reader the paper asks whether anyone’s special mediation is really needed to reach God, and whether ritual has quietly taken the place of religion in our own faith.
