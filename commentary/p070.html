Introduction — Government Is an Unconscious Development

Following the paper on primitive institutions, Paper 70 examines the evolution of human government. The author, a Melchizedek of Nebadon, observes that no sooner had man partly solved the problem of making a living than he faced the task of regulating human contacts. Industry demanded law and order, and private property made government necessary. (70:0.1, 70:12.21)

On an evolutionary world antagonisms are natural, and peace is secured only by some system of social regulation. No one designed government; it grew as practices that proved their survival value became tradition. Anarchy added to misery, and so law and order slowly emerged. (70:0.2, 70:0.3)

Government is an unconscious development; it evolves by trial and error. (70:0.3)

I–II. The Genesis of War; The Social Value of War — Scaffolding That Needs a Substitute

War is the natural state and heritage of evolving man, an animalistic reaction to misunderstanding and irritation, while peace measures the progress of civilization. As social groups formed, individual irritations were submerged in group feeling, and peace within the tribe was bought at the expense of peace between tribes. Those who learned the golden rule early, like the Andonites, settled disputes by beating a tree with a stick or by a public contest of ridicule. (70:1.1, 70:1.2, 70:1.3, 70:1.5)

War is the natural state and heritage of evolving man; peace is the social yardstick measuring civilization’s advancement. (70:1.1)

The author does not hide the atrocities of ancient times committed in the name of God, but he states clearly that such things are not instigated by Deity. War was gradually refined: captives were taken, women and noncombatants were exempted, wars were declared, and sanctuary and the right of asylum were recognized. (70:1.16, 70:1.20, 70:1.21)

In the past war had social value: it imposed discipline, enforced co-operation, and fostered courage. But like slavery, it is scaffolding that must one day be abandoned. As bloodletting gave way to better remedies, so must war, and for that to happen peaceful substitutes must be found for what war once accomplished. (70:2.9, 70:2.10, 70:2.19)

Do not make the mistake of glorifying war; (70:2.19)

Industrialism, which is to replace militarism, has its own dangers. The author lists materialism and spiritual blindness, the worship of wealth-power, luxury, indolence, biologic deterioration, and standardized industrial slavery, warning that labor ennobles but drudgery benumbs. Future leaders must be found not in war but in industry, science, and social achievement. (70:2.11, 70:2.17, 70:2.21)

III–IV. Early Human Associations; Clans and Tribes — Widening the Peace Group

Real government appears only when groups larger than the family form. Families united by blood into clans, and clans evolved into tribes, which were territorial communities. War and outside pressure forced tribal organization, but it was commerce and trade that kept peace within. The author holds that peace will be promoted far more by international trade organizations than by sentimental peace planning. (70:3.2, 70:3.3, 70:3.4)

The gain motive is a mighty civilizer when augmented by the desire to serve. (70:3.5)

From an age in which strangers were killed, mankind moved to adoption, “guest friendship,” and peace treaties sealed by blood bonds, and the circle of relationship slowly widened. The peace group grew from family to clan to tribe to nation, and the author finds it most encouraging that today it reaches beyond blood ties to embrace nations. The clans served local self-government well but delayed the growth of large nations. (70:3.6, 70:3.9, 70:4.1, 70:4.10)

V–VI. The Beginnings of Government; Monarchial Government — From Council to King

The first real governmental body was the council of the elders, which held the potential of all three functions: executive, legislative, and judicial. But as people learned that neither peace nor war can be run by a debating society, power gathered in the war chiefs. War has always been a kingmaker. (70:5.2, 70:5.3, 70:5.5)

Man discovered that effective government comes from conferring power on a personality, not on an idea. Hereditary kingship prevented the havoc of succession struggles, but it also bred the superstition of the king’s divine origin. Yet even the most despotic tyrant was restrained by fear of assassination, by priests and aristocracy, and by people who would rise up against him. (70:6.1, 70:6.3, 70:6.4, 70:6.6)

VII–VIII. Primitive Clubs and Secret Societies; Social Classes

After blood kinship and intermarriage, the next step was religious cults and political clubs. Appearing first as secret societies, they trained adolescent youths and taught sex control, and they also served as spies, secret police, and tax collectors. Yet they became the first schools, the first political parties, and the first charitable organizations, and finally the forerunners of churches and the first international fraternities. (70:7.1, 70:7.14, 70:7.17, 70:7.19)

The mental and physical inequality of human beings ensures that social classes will appear. The author lists ten reasons classes form: natural, personal, chance, economic, geographic, social, vocational, religious, racial, and age. Strikingly, the only worlds without social strata are the most primitive and the most advanced, and a world settled in light and life has largely effaced these divisions. (70:8.1, 70:8.2)

Flexible, shifting classes are indispensable to an evolving civilization, but when class hardens into caste, stability is bought at the cost of personal initiative and social co-operation. The author foresees that classes will be dissolved over millenniums through biologic improvement, education, and a religious quickening of the sense of brotherhood. (70:8.13, 70:8.14, 70:8.18)

Religion is the mighty lever that lifts civilization from chaos, but it is powerless apart from the fulcrum of sound and normal mind resting securely on sound and normal heredity. (70:8.18)

IX–X. Human Rights; Evolution of Justice — The Rules of the Game

Nature confers no rights on man, only life and a world in which to live it. Society’s prime gift is security. The author lists ten rights that society has come to assure, the last being the guarantee of the freedom of religious practice so that all other social activities may be exalted by spiritual motivation. (70:9.1, 70:9.2, 70:9.12)

But human rights are not really natural; they are entirely social. (70:9.13)

Rights are relative and ever-changing rules of the game. Society cannot offer equal rights to all, but it can promise to administer the varying rights of each with fairness and equity. Its duty is to give everyone a fair opportunity for self-maintenance, self-perpetuation, and some measure of self-gratification, and the author sums up the three together as human happiness. (70:9.13, 70:9.17)

Justice, too, is a product of evolution. Primitive man asked not what killed someone but who, and ignored motive. Through ordeals of poison and fire, blood vengeance, and blood money, true justice began only when revenge was taken from private and kin groups and lodged with the state. Of the ordeal that survives in scripture, the author insists that no divine being ever gave such unfair instructions. (70:10.3, 70:10.5, 70:10.8, 70:10.13)

it was learned that the severity of the punishment was not so valuable a deterrent to crime as was its certainty and swiftness. (70:10.15)

XI–XII. Laws and Courts; Allocation of Civil Authority — Toward Representative Government

Mores are laws in the making, and law is a codified record of long human experience. Law begins as negative and prohibitive, like “you shall not kill,” but becomes more positive and directive as civilization advances. The first courts were regulated fistic encounters, yet the status of any civilization can be accurately judged by the thoroughness and equity of its courts and the integrity of its judges. (70:11.1, 70:11.2, 70:11.6, 70:11.14)

In early society crime was an assault on the tribal mores, and sin the transgression of taboos sanctioned by ghosts. The failure to separate the two caused long confusion, but self-interest, social tradition, and religious sanction together made human life more safe and sacred. The author judges that superstition was the moral and social police force of the long evolutionary ages. (70:11.4, 70:11.5)

The great struggle in the evolution of government has concerned the concentration of power. Universe administrators have learned by experience that evolutionary peoples are best governed by representative government with a proper balance among executive, legislative, and judicial branches. The author says that Urantia mortals are entitled to liberty, and should adopt their own constitutions and choose competent and worthy leaders. (70:12.1, 70:12.2, 70:12.5)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 70 shows that the peace, rights, and courts we enjoy were not handed down from heaven ready-made but refined little by little through bloodshed and trial and error. Its conclusion—that even the ideal government is not revealed but must be discovered—returns responsibility to every citizen. Peace does not come merely by wishing for it; it comes when we provide wise substitutes for what war once did.
