Introduction — Serving Best by Standing Apart

Paper 99 brings the history of religion traced in the two preceding papers to bear on present-day problems. Its author, a Melchizedek of Nebadon, states the principle of the whole paper in its first sentence: religion renders its highest social service when it is least entangled with the secular institutions of society. (99:0.1, 99:7.6)

RELIGION achieves its highest social ministry when it has least connection with the secular institutions of society. (99:0.1)

In past ages social reform was largely confined to the moral realm, so religion could devote itself to replacing evil with good within the existing order. In the twentieth century, however, religion is called on to adjust to extensive and continuing social reconstruction. True religion opposes violence as a means of social evolution, but it does not oppose society’s intelligent efforts to adapt its institutions to new conditions. (99:0.1, 99:0.2, 99:0.3)

I. Religion and Social Reconstruction — A Compass on the High Seas

Mechanical inventions and the spread of knowledge are reshaping civilization, and the coming social order will not settle down for a millennium. Mankind must accept a procession of changes, adjustments, and readjustments on its march toward an unrevealed planetary destiny. Religion has no new duties in such an age, but it is urgently needed as a wise guide and experienced counselor. (99:1.1, 99:1.4)

The social ship has steamed out of the sheltered bays of established tradition and has begun its cruise upon the high seas of evolutionary destiny (99:1.3)

Society is becoming more mechanical, more compact, and more interdependent. Religion must act as the cosmic salt that keeps these new relationships from becoming mutually destructive. A godless humanitarianism is a noble gesture, the paper says, but true religion is the only power that can lastingly make one group responsive to the sufferings of another. (99:1.4, 99:1.5)

The spirit of religion is eternal, but the form of its expression must be restated every time the dictionary of human language is revised. (99:1.6)

II. Weakness of Institutional Religion — Caught in a Vicious Circle

Institutional religion cannot lead the coming age of reconstruction, because it has become an organic part of the very social order that must be rebuilt. It cannot reconstruct society without first reconstructing itself, and it cannot reconstruct itself until society has been reconstructed. Only the real religion of personal spiritual experience can break out of this deadlock. (99:2.1, 99:2.2)

For this reason religionists must work in society, industry, and politics as individuals, not as groups or parties. The moment a religious group acts collectively in nonreligious affairs, it becomes a political party or an economic organization. Nor are religious people automatically better reformers; they are of more value only insofar as their religion has given them the social wisdom born of the desire to love God and every man as a brother. (99:2.3, 99:2.4)

An ideal social order is that in which every man loves his neighbor as he loves himself. (99:2.4)

III. Religion and the Religionist — Influence through Individuals

Early Christianity was entirely free from civil entanglements and economic alliances. The kingdom of heaven is neither a social nor an economic order; it is a spiritual brotherhood of God-knowing individuals. Such a brotherhood is nevertheless an amazing social phenomenon in itself, with far-reaching political and economic repercussions. (99:3.1, 99:3.2)

The religionist is not indifferent to social suffering or injustice. Religion influences civilization directly by spiritualizing the individual citizen, and indirectly as those citizens become influential members of many groups. A high civilization first requires the ideal type of citizen. Many reformers who reject institutional religion are themselves driven by unrecognized religious motives, but since they are not open to religious criticism, they find it hard to correct themselves. (99:3.3, 99:3.4, 99:3.6, 99:3.7)

It is a fact that religion does not grow unless it is disciplined by constructive criticism, amplified by philosophy, purified by science, and nourished by loyal fellowship. (99:3.7)

Religion is always in danger of distortion, as when in wartime it is turned into military propaganda, and loveless zeal always harms it. The paper names five ways to keep religion free from unholy secular alliances. (99:3.8, 99:3.9)

In sum, religionists as a group must concern themselves with nothing but religion, yet any one of them, as an individual citizen, may become an outstanding leader of a social, economic, or political reform movement. The business of religion is to create, sustain, and inspire in such citizens a cosmic loyalty that carries them through the hard work of social service. (99:3.15, 99:3.16)

IV. Transition Difficulties — Between the Religion of Fear and the Religion of Love

Genuine religion makes the religionist socially fragrant and lends new meaning to family, school, play, and humor. A living and growing faith, together with children, is the great unifier of family life. But if religion is to build character it must not be standardized; if it is to act as a lure of values it must not be stereotyped; and if it is to inspire supreme loyalties it must not be formalized. (99:4.1, 99:4.2, 99:4.3)

After all, it is what one believes rather than what one knows that determines conduct and dominates personal performances. (99:4.5)

What, then, makes religion genuine? The paper’s test is simple. Whatever upheavals civilization undergoes, a religion is genuine and worth while if it fosters in the individual an experience in which truth, beauty, and goodness are sovereign. Through love and worship this becomes fellowship with man and sonship with God. Religion becoming more and more a private matter is no danger, so long as it does not lose its motivation for unselfish service. (99:4.4, 99:4.7)

Amid the upheavals of the scientific age, thousands are anxious, restless, and fearful, and they need the consolation of sound religion as never before. The greatest spiritual danger, the author says, is partial progress: forsaking the evolutionary religion of fear without yet grasping the revelatory religion of love. Modern science and psychology have weakened only those religions that rest on fear and superstition. (99:4.6, 99:4.8)

V. Social Aspects of Religion — Goals, Not Creeds

Religion is the personal experience of knowing God as a Father, but that experience necessarily leads to knowing man as a brother, and so it acquires a social side. Religion begins as inner adjustment and grows into social service. As long as people like to gather together, religious groups will come into existence. (99:5.1)

True religion is to know God as your Father and man as your brother. (99:5.2)

The paper foresees a day when religionists will cooperate on the basis of unity of ideals and purposes rather than theological opinion. Let “faith” mean the individual’s relation to God, not a creed a group has agreed upon. Jesus did not ask his followers to recite a form of words; he asked them to gather and share the supper of remembrance of his life. (99:5.7, 99:5.10)

Goals rather than creeds should unify religionists. (99:5.7)

The author also speaks of how to regard other traditions. It is a great mistake, while presenting Christ as the supreme spiritual leader, to require people to reject the God-knowing leaders who brought light to their own peoples in past ages. He adds that the doctrine of the total depravity of man undermined religion’s uplifting power, and that Jesus sought to restore human dignity by declaring all men the children of God. (99:5.5, 99:5.11)

Primitive man danced out his religion rather than putting it into words; modern man has thought out many creeds and tests of faith. The paper says the religionists of the future must live out their religion and dedicate themselves to the service of the brotherhood of man, with an experience so personal and sublime that it lies too deep for words. (99:5.9)

VI–VII. Institutional Religion; Religion’s Contribution

It is far better to have a religion without a church than a church without religion. (99:6.1)

The author does not read the religious turmoil of the twentieth century as spiritual decline. Confusion may come before destruction, but it also comes before growth. His diagnosis—that sectarianism is a disease of institutional religion and dogmatism an enslavement of the spiritual nature—reads as a warning meant to steer that confusion toward growth. (99:6.1)

This does not mean the paper rejects religious groups. Group activity dramatizes the loyalties of religion, fosters unselfish fellowship, family life, and religious education, and makes group worship possible. But the more religion is institutionalized, the more its power for good is curtailed and its possibilities for evil multiplied. The paper lists these dangers at length. (99:6.2, 99:6.3)

The last section sums up religion’s distinctive contribution. Religion should stand aloof from secular activities yet do nothing to hinder the coordination of social institutions. In all reconstruction it provides a stabilizing loyalty to a transcendent object, a goal beyond the immediate one. Without meditating in the presence of God’s sovereignty, no one can wisely decide temporal issues or rise above self-interest. (99:7.1, 99:7.2, 99:7.4)

Religion inspires man to live courageously and joyfully on the face of the earth (99:7.3)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

The conclusion of Paper 99 is balance. Science sobers the dreamer, economic necessity ties him to reality, and personal religious experience brings him face to face with the eternal realities of cosmic citizenship. Religion’s task is not to design society directly but to build a cosmic loyalty in the people who will lead it. (99:7.5, 99:3.16)
