Introduction — What the Last Paper Asks

The last paper of The Urantia Book is concerned less with what Jesus did than with how he believed. Jesus enjoyed a sublime and wholehearted faith in God. He passed through all the ordinary ups and downs of mortal life, yet he never religiously doubted God’s watchcare. His faith was neither inherited from tradition nor an intellectual conclusion; it was a personal, spiritual insight born of the activity of the indwelling Adjuster. (196:0.1)

He saw God not only as holy, just, and great but also as true, beautiful, and good, and he unified all these attributes as “the will of the Father in heaven.” The idea of calling God Father did not originate with him, but he exalted it into a sublime experience and proclaimed every mortal a child of that Father. (196:0.2)

The text says that Jesus’ great contribution was not that he revealed many new ideas about God, but that he so magnificently and humanly demonstrated a new and higher type of living faith in God. (196:0.3)

Never on all the worlds of this universe, in the life of any one mortal, did God ever become such a living reality as in the human experience of Jesus of Nazareth. (196:0.3)

Faith in Daily Life — Faith Without Fanaticism, a Life of Prayer

Strong spiritual faith in religious geniuses often slides into fanaticism and exaggerated ego. But Jesus’ faith never overrode the judgments of practical life. He respected personal honor, family love, religious obligation, social duty, and economic necessity, and he harmonized them all with his faith. Even in his intense mission there was never the rage of a fanatic or the bluster of a religious egotist. (196:0.6, 196:0.7, 196:0.8)

He dedicated his own will to the service of doing the divine will and interpreted religion wholly in terms of the Father’s will. He never prayed as a religious duty. The secret of his unparalleled religious life was his consciousness of the presence of God, which he attained not by visions or extraordinary practices but by intelligent prayer and sincere worship. (196:0.10)

When you study the career of the Master, as concerns prayer or any other feature of the religious life, look not so much for what he taught as for what he did. (196:0.10)

Jesus trusted God as a child trusts a parent, and believed in the fundamental goodness of the universe as a child believes in the security of home. Yet his faith was neither immature nor credulous. He joined a child’s trusting optimism with the intelligent courage of a mature adult, and his faith grew so high that it was without fear. (196:0.11, 196:0.12)

Notwithstanding that Jesus’ faith was childlike, it was in no sense childish. (196:0.12)
Jesus does not require his disciples to believe in him but rather to believe with him (196:0.13)

Not only to believe what he believed but to believe as he believed: this is the full meaning of “Follow me.” The text adds that believing in this way takes a strong will and steadfast trust. Jesus’ faith was full of trust yet far from presumption, and it faced disappointment, difficulty, and the demands of duty with courage. (196:0.13, 196:0.14)

I. Jesus — The Man

However great the sovereignty of Michael, the human Jesus must not be taken away from men. He ascended on high as a man as well as God. The text says that debates about the humanity or divinity of Christ must not obscure the saving truth that he was a religious man who, by faith, knew and did the will of God. (196:1.1)

he was the most truly religious man who has ever lived on Urantia. (196:1.1)

The text says the time has come for a figurative resurrection of the human Jesus from the burial tomb of nineteen centuries of theological tradition. To follow Jesus is not to imitate his outward life literally but to share his faith: to trust God as he trusted God, and to believe in people as he believed in people. (196:1.2, 196:1.3, 196:1.5)

the world’s greatest religious teacher was indeed a layman. (196:1.4)

As human beings advance from human consciousness to the realization of divinity, so Jesus ascended from human nature to the consciousness of divine nature. That ascent was the joint achievement of mortal faith and the work of the indwelling Adjuster, and it is marked by seven stages. (196:1.6)

II. The Religion of Jesus — Beyond a Religion About Jesus

You may preach a religion about Jesus, but, perforce, you must live the religion of Jesus. (196:2.1)

Here the thread traced through the last papers of Part IV is drawn together. Peter at Pentecost unintentionally inaugurated a new religion of the risen and glorified Christ, and Paul transformed it into Christianity, founded on his own experience. The text judges that the New Testament is a superb Christian document, but only meagerly portrays Jesus’ own religious life. (196:2.1)

The text calls it a great mistake to regard Jesus as wholly human or as only divine. He is still truly human and divine. And because his ascent from the human to the divine was wholly a mortal achievement, he became the new and living way by which every mortal may make the same ascent. (196:2.2, 196:2.3, 196:2.4)

Jesus led people to feel at home in the world. He taught that the world is not fundamentally evil, and he attained an ideal religious life in the very midst of the real world. He did not view humankind negatively; the high value he placed on people is why he poured himself out in ceaseless service. (196:2.9, 196:2.10)

He saw most men as weak rather than wicked, more distraught than depraved. (196:2.9)

III. The Supremacy of Religion — Fact, Meaning, and Value

Personal, spiritual religious experience is an efficient solvent for most mortal difficulties. Religion does not remove problems, but it illuminates and transcends them. And it bridges the deep gulf between the intellectual logic that knows a First Cause of the universe and the soul’s affirmation that he is the Father in heaven of Jesus’ gospel. (196:3.1)

The full summation of human life is the knowledge that man is educated by fact, ennobled by wisdom, and saved—justified—by religious faith. (196:3.4)

The human mind is not wholly material, for within it is a spirit nucleus, the Adjuster. The text gives three evidences of this spirit indwelling. The mind does not create values; it discovers, interprets, and chooses them, and moral values become ours through three choices: self-judgment, social judgment, and God-judgment. (196:3.6, 196:3.10, 196:3.11)

The text compares several religions to show Jesus’ place. The Hebrews achieved a religion of moral sublimity, the Greeks a religion of beauty, and Paul and his associates a religion of faith, hope, and charity. Jesus revealed and exemplified a religion of love: security in the Father’s love and the joy of sharing that love with one’s brothers. Yet love without truth, beauty, and goodness is only sentiment; love must be redefined on each level of progress. (196:3.19, 196:3.29)

Religion stands above science, art, philosophy, and morals, yet it is not separate from them. God is not the invention of human idealism but the personality of love, the source of all that is true, beautiful, and good. And the great challenge to modern people is to achieve better communication with the divine Monitor who dwells within the mind. (196:3.24, 196:3.28, 196:3.34)

Be not discouraged; human evolution is still in progress, and the revelation of God to the world, in and through Jesus, shall not fail. (196:3.33)

Conclusion — Living This Paper, and This Book

Paper 196 invites us to move from believing in Jesus to believing with Jesus. His faith trusted like a child yet was brave like an adult, burned warm without fanaticism, and was renewed daily in prayer and worship. And the paper’s last paragraph ends with a promise: the realities born of love and nurtured in service do not perish. (196:0.13, 196:3.35)

That last sentence brings the whole book back to its beginning. In Paper 1, before anything else, The Urantia Book introduced the Universal Father. After the long journey through the central and superuniverses, the local universe, the history of Urantia, and the life and teachings of Jesus, the book returns to the Father and comes to rest. (1:0.1, 196:3.35)

THE Universal Father is the God of all creation, the First Source and Center of all things and beings. (1:0.1)
When all is said and done, the Father idea is still the highest human concept of God. (196:3.35)

With this, the paper-by-paper commentary on The Urantia Book comes to its close. (196:2.1, 196:1.5)
