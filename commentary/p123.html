Introduction — From Alexandria to Nazareth

Paper 123 begins with the family’s refuge in Egypt and follows Jesus until he is eight years old. In Alexandria Joseph soon found work and was promoted to foreman of a large group of workmen on a public building, which gave him the idea of becoming a contractor and builder. That experience would later change the life of the Nazareth family. (123:0.1)

At first Mary wanted to keep the child always at her side, but Joseph and their kinsfolk persuaded her that this would deprive him of the helpful experience of learning to get along with children his own age. Mary came to see that undue sheltering might make him self-conscious and self-centered, and she agreed to let the child of promise grow up like any other child. It was the moment when love turned from holding the child close to letting him grow. (123:0.2)

Only an affectionate mother can know the burden that Mary carried in her heart for the safety of her son during these years of his infancy and early childhood. (123:0.2)

The believers in Alexandria urged the family to stay, insisting that the child of destiny would exert a far greater world influence there, and on parting they gave him a complete copy of the Hebrew scriptures in Greek. After they returned to Bethlehem in 4 B.C., Mary still wanted to raise him in the City of David, but Joseph spent three weeks persuading her that Galilee was better for the child’s safety and education. (123:0.3, 123:0.5, 123:0.6)

I. Back in Nazareth — A Promise of Silence Kept

On the fourth day of the journey the family reached Nazareth, and for the first time since Jesus’ birth Mary settled down to enjoy life in her own home. Jesus was about three years and two months old. On the way, Joseph and Mary had agreed not to tell anyone in Galilee that Jesus was a child of promise, and they kept that promise faithfully. (123:1.1, 123:1.2, 123:1.3)

In his fourth year Jesus formed a lifelong friendship with a neighbor boy named Jacob. Joseph built a small workshop near the village spring and the caravan tarrying lot, and Jesus grew up listening to the talk of travelers from the four corners of the earth. The family grew as follows. (123:1.4, 123:1.6)

II. The Fifth Year (2 B.C.) — The Adjuster Arrives, and a Boy Full of Questions

On February 11, 2 B.C., when Jesus reached the age of his first personal and wholehearted moral decision, a Thought Adjuster came to dwell with him. This Adjuster had served earlier with Machiventa Melchizedek and so had experience in connection with an incarnation. From that day the direct supervision of the Universe Rulers ended, and Jesus’ guardianship rested with the indwelling Adjuster and the seraphic guardians. (123:2.1, 123:2.2)

Yet Jesus, like millions of other children, did not notice that moment. The Adjuster came to him quietly, just as it comes to every normal child. (123:2.1)

Jesus was no more aware of the coming of the divine Monitor than are the millions upon millions of other children (123:2.1)

The most valuable part of Jesus’ early education came from his parents’ faithful answers to his questions. Joseph and Mary could not always answer them, but they never failed to help him work toward a satisfying solution of his own. (123:2.3)

From the time Jesus was five years old until he was ten, he was one continuous question mark. (123:2.3)

The Galilean Jews had a systematic program for rearing their children, dividing a child’s life into seven stages. On August 21, 2 B.C., following custom, Mary formally turned Jesus over to Joseph for further instruction. (123:2.5, 123:2.13)

Even afterward Mary taught him to care for the vines and flowers of the garden, and in shallow boxes of sand on the roof he practiced writing Aramaic, Greek, and later Hebrew. Had you been there, says the narrator, you would only have seen a normal, healthy, and exceedingly inquisitive child. (123:2.14, 123:2.16)

III. Events of the Sixth Year (1 B.C.) — A First Disillusionment and Talks with the Father

Joseph taught Jesus Greek using the scriptures they had received in Egypt. It was one of only two complete copies in Greek in all Nazareth, so a steady stream of truth seekers came to the house. Before he was six, Jesus asked his father the cause of an earthquake, and Joseph honestly answered that he did not know. (123:3.1, 123:3.2)

Thus began that long and disconcerting disillusionment in the course of which Jesus found out that his earthly parents were not all-wise and all-knowing. (123:3.2)

Joseph avoided the easy answer of blaming God for the earthquake. Jesus long accepted the doctrine of good and evil spirits as a possible explanation of mental and spiritual phenomena, but very early he doubted that unseen influences caused the physical happenings of nature. An honest “I do not know” opened the child’s thinking in the right direction. (123:3.3)

After a visit from John’s family, Jesus took a keen interest in the history of Israel and its feasts, and Joseph explained the meaning of each in turn. (123:3.4, 123:3.5)

This year Jesus puzzled his parents by insisting on talking to his heavenly Father much as he talked to his earthly father. This intimate conversation, added after his formal prayers, foreshadows the relationship with the Father he would one day proclaim. (123:3.6)

he would say his prayers just as he had been taught, after which he insisted on having “just a little talk with my Father in heaven.” (123:3.6)

Joseph became a builder, and the family income more than trebled; Jesus traveled with his father to the surrounding towns and diligently stored up knowledge about how people lived. Mary was a fairly strict disciplinarian, but Joseph would sit down and explain the reasons behind family rules, and when Jesus understood, he always cooperated willingly. (123:3.7, 123:3.8, 123:3.9)

IV. The Seventh Year (A.D. 1) — An Accident That Could Not Be Avoided

The seventh year was eventful. In January came one of the deepest snowfalls at Nazareth in a hundred years. Jewish children of that time mostly played at the serious things their elders did, such as weddings and funerals, and Jesus played with wood blocks in the corner of the carpenter shop. He found it hard to see the harm in certain games forbidden on the Sabbath, but he never went against his parents’ wishes. (123:4.1, 123:4.2, 123:4.3)

Mary used the profits from selling doves as a special charity fund, and Jesus administered it after deducting the tithe and turning it over to the synagogue officer. This small responsibility, given to a seven-year-old, taught generosity with his own hands. (123:4.4)

In July of A.D. 1, during an unusual sandstorm, Jesus fell coming down the stone stairs from the roof. A primary and a secondary midwayer had been assigned to watch over him, but the narrator makes clear that the accident was not due to any neglect by the midwayers or the guardian seraphim; it simply could not have been avoided. Afterward Joseph built a balustrade on both sides of the stairway. (123:4.5, 123:4.6)

Material accidents, commonplace occurrences of a physical nature, are not arbitrarily interfered with by celestial personalities. (123:4.7)

Ordinarily only midway creatures can intervene in material conditions, and then only on the specific mandate of their superiors. That even a Son of God was not exempt from ordinary accidents keeps us from reading our own accidents as punishment or abandonment. Frightened, Mary unwisely kept Jesus close at her side for some months. (123:4.6, 123:4.7)

V. School Days in Nazareth — Three Strands of Education

In August of his seventh year Jesus entered the synagogue school, spending three years in the elementary school and three in the advanced school, where the law was learned by repeating it aloud. For his birthday text he chose words from Isaiah. (123:5.1, 123:5.2, 123:5.11)

Jesus was highly educated in that he thoroughly understood men and devotedly loved them. (123:5.8)

Nazareth was a caravan way station and a center of liberal interpretation of the law. Jesus spoke freely in Greek with caravan travelers at the spring, and on Sabbath afternoons he climbed the hill with his father to view Mount Carmel, Mount Hermon, the Decapolis, and the Mediterranean. He learned to milk the cow, make cheese, and weave, and his imagination grew beside the wheel of Nathan the potter. The chazan even admitted that he feared he had learned more from Jesus’ searching questions than he had been able to teach him. (123:5.7, 123:5.9, 123:5.12, 123:5.15)

VI. His Eighth Year (A.D. 2) — A Decision Made with the Father

Jesus was diligent enough to belong to the more progressive third of his class, which earned him one week a month on an uncle’s farm or by the Sea of Galilee. He learned new techniques of numbers from a mathematics teacher from Damascus, and by trading dairy products for lessons he became a skillful harpist by eleven. His questions about the dry and rainy seasons and the difference in temperatures never stopped. (123:6.1, 123:6.3, 123:6.5, 123:6.6)

Nahor, a teacher from a rabbinical academy in Jerusalem, came and urged that Jesus be educated in Jerusalem; Mary was half persuaded, and Joseph hesitated. At Nahor’s request the matter was laid before the eight-year-old Jesus, who for two days consulted his parents and a neighbor and talked “with my Father who is in heaven,” then answered that he should remain at home. (123:6.8, 123:6.9)

Joseph was sure his son would be a man of destiny, but profoundly uncertain what that destiny would prove to be, and the more he thought about it, the more he questioned the wisdom of a stay in Jerusalem. With the parents divided, the weight of the decision passed to the child. Jesus felt unequal to the responsibility, yet without hurrying he listened, asked, and prayed, and then decided, though he was not perfectly sure of the answer. (123:6.8, 123:6.9)

they who love me so much should be able to do more for me and guide me more safely than strangers who can only view my body and observe my mind but can hardly truly know me. (123:6.9)

They all marveled, and Nahor returned to Jerusalem. Many years passed before the subject of leaving home came up again. The judgment that those who love us know us better than expert strangers shows a deep trust in the value of the home. (123:6.9)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

The Jesus of Paper 123 is not a wonder-working child but one who asks, plays, falls, and learns. The Adjuster came quietly, his parents admitted what they did not know, and among family, neighbors, and caravan travelers he learned to understand and love people. The power of this paper lies in showing that a divine life walked the path of ordinary growth. (123:2.16, 123:5.8)
