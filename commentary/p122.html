Introduction — Why the Home of Joseph and Mary?

The narrator begins by admitting that it is hardly possible to explain fully why Michael chose Palestine, and within it the family of Joseph and Mary. Yet he makes clear that the choice was no impulse but a careful process of several stages, beginning with a review of the Melchizedek report and ending with Gabriel’s personal selection. (122:0.1, 122:0.2)

This procedure shows that even the affairs of heaven move in an orderly way—by investigation, counsel, and approval. Yet the final choice was made not by the commission’s list but by Gabriel’s own judgment. Paper 122 begins in the home that was prepared in this way. (122:0.3)

I. Joseph and Mary — A Wide Lineage in an Average Life

Joseph was a Hebrew of the Hebrews, though many non-Jewish strains had entered his line through its women, and his ancestry reached back through Abraham to Andon and Fonta. David and Solomon were not in his direct line; his immediate ancestors were mechanics—builders, carpenters, masons, and smiths. Mary was a Jew in culture and belief, but in hereditary endowment she was more a composite of Syrian, Hittite, Phoenician, Greek, and Egyptian stocks. (122:1.1, 122:1.2)

Of all couples in Palestine at that time, these two possessed the most ideal combination of widespread racial connections and superior average personality endowments. The standard of choice was not distinction but normality. (122:1.3)

It was the plan of Michael to appear on earth as an average man, that the common people might understand him and receive him (122:1.3)

II. Gabriel Appears to Elizabeth — The Forerunner’s Birth

Jesus’ lifework on Urantia was really begun by John the Baptist. (122:2.1)

The priest Zacharias and his wife Elizabeth had long been childless. Late in June of 8 B.C. Gabriel appeared to Elizabeth and announced a son, John, who would be the forerunner of the divine teacher, and told her that her kinswoman Mary would be the mother of the child of promise. Elizabeth kept the secret even from her husband for five months, and Zacharias doubted for weeks until a dream finally convinced him. (122:2.2, 122:2.3, 122:2.5)

Mary visited Elizabeth and stayed three weeks, comparing their experiences, and Elizabeth did much to strengthen Mary’s faith. John was born on March 25, 7 B.C., and from infancy his parents wisely impressed on him the idea that he was to become a spiritual leader. Even a great calling grows within relationships that encourage one another and within the teaching of the home. (122:2.6, 122:2.7, 122:2.8)

III–IV. Gabriel’s Announcement to Mary; Joseph’s Dream

One evening about sundown Gabriel appeared to Mary beside a low stone table, told her to call her son Joshua, and said that he would inaugurate the kingdom of heaven. Mary kept this to herself for many weeks; when Joseph heard it, he trusted his wife yet could not sleep for many nights. Mary’s parents and brothers and sisters were always skeptical about Jesus’ mission. (122:3.1, 122:3.2, 122:3.3)

Gabriel’s announcement to Mary was made the day following the conception of Jesus and was the only event of supernatural occurrence connected with her entire experience of carrying and bearing the child of promise. (122:3.4)

Joseph set aside his doubts only after a dream in which a celestial messenger told him that in the child would be life, and that this life would become the light of mankind. The narrator stresses that none of these visitations said anything about the house of David, about a deliverer of the Jews, or even about the long-expected Messiah. Joseph was counted of the “house of David” only because, six generations earlier, an orphaned paternal ancestor had been adopted by Zadoc, a descendant of David. (122:4.1, 122:4.2, 122:4.3)

Jesus was not such a Messiah as the Jews had anticipated, but he was the world’s deliverer. (122:4.2)

The text says that most of the so-called Messianic prophecies were applied to Jesus long after his life on earth. “A maiden shall bear a son” was made to read “a virgin shall bear a son,” and the later genealogies cannot be depended on as factual. Faith must always guard against the temptation to adjust the facts to fit its expectations. (122:4.4)

The early followers of Jesus all too often succumbed to the temptation to make all the olden prophetic utterances appear to find fulfillment in the life of their Lord and Master. (122:4.4)

V. Jesus’ Earth Parents — Two Temperaments in Harmony

Joseph and Mary were opposite in temperament. Jesus received different gifts from each, and as an adult he more often drove forward in the manner of his mother’s optimistic and determined disposition. Both were good teachers, educated well above the average of their day. (122:5.2, 122:5.3, 122:5.7)

Had Joseph lived, says the narrator, he would surely have become a firm believer in his eldest son’s divine mission. Mary alternated between belief and doubt, swayed by her other children and relatives, but the memory of Gabriel’s appearance right after the conception always steadied her final attitude. Most of Joseph’s family later believed Jesus’ teachings, while very few of Mary’s people did. (122:5.6, 122:5.10)

Their courtship began when Mary brought Joseph a cup of water during a noontime meal while he was working for her father. They married when Joseph was twenty-one and moved into the new home in Nazareth that he had built with his brothers. The heart of this section is that two very different people formed one home and together nurtured the balanced personality of one child. (122:5.8, 122:5.9)

VI–VII. The Home at Nazareth; The Trip to Bethlehem

The home at Nazareth was a one-room stone house with a flat roof; a low stone table, a loom, a lampstand, and sleeping mats made up most of its furnishings. It stood on the outskirts of the town near the high hill, which later made it easy for Jesus to stroll in the country and climb the hill. (122:6.1, 122:6.2)

As a small boy Jesus often fed grain to this mill while his mother turned the grinder. (122:6.2)

Augustus’s census was taken in Palestine a year late, in 7 B.C. Joseph could have registered for the family alone, but Mary, adventurous and aggressive, insisted on going. They had only one beast of burden, so Mary, large with child, rode while Joseph walked. (122:7.1, 122:7.2, 122:7.4)

VIII. The Birth of Jesus — A Noon Without a Star

At noon on August 21, 7 B.C., with the help of women fellow travelers, Mary gave birth to a son, and the babe was laid in a near-by manger. The seraphim sang anthems of glory over the manger, but their praise was not heard by human ears. (122:8.1, 122:8.5)

In just the same manner as all babies before that day and since have come into the world, the promised child was born (122:8.2)

The day after the birth Joseph made his enrollment, and through a man they had met at Jericho, a well-to-do friend gladly exchanged his room at the inn with them. On the eighth day the child was circumcised and formally named Joshua. A momentous event of a universe passed quietly, surrounded by the small kindnesses of strangers. (122:8.2, 122:8.3)

Zacharias, Elizabeth, and Mary believed that Jesus would become the Messiah and successor of David on the throne, and so the family stayed in Bethlehem more than a year. This section draws a clear line between beautiful legend and what actually happened. (122:8.4)

The narrator explains that in an age of oral tradition, myths easily became traditions, and traditions came to be accepted as facts. A faith that loves truth treasures what really happened more than a moving story. (122:8.7)

IX–X. The Presentation in the Temple; Herod Acts

According to the law of Moses, Joseph and Mary went to the temple in Jerusalem to redeem their first-born son and to offer the sacrifice of purification. Simeon the singer and Anna the poetess, who had been told the secret by Zacharias, recognized the child by a prearranged signal, and Simeon sang a song composed by Anna. But on the way back to Bethlehem, Joseph was not in harmony with this premature effort to make Jesus out to be the Jewish Messiah. (122:9.1, 122:9.3, 122:9.28)

To shine upon those who sit in darkness and the shadow of death (122:9.21)

A copy of parts of Simeon’s song reached Herod. When more than a year of searching failed to find the child, Herod ordered every boy under two years of age in Bethlehem to be killed. A believer at court warned Zacharias, and the night before the massacre Joseph and Mary left with the babe for Alexandria, where they stayed two full years, until after Herod’s death. (122:10.1, 122:10.3, 122:10.4)

These were hard times for the family. Joseph was afraid to seek work, their small savings were rapidly disappearing, and at the purification ceremony he offered only two young pigeons, as the law directed for poor mothers. Zacharias provided the funds for the flight, and in Alexandria well-to-do relatives of Joseph’s family gave them lodging. (122:10.2, 122:10.4)

And thus perished in one day sixteen boy babies in Bethlehem of Judea. (122:10.3)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 122 portrays the birth of a universe Son without miraculous decoration. He was born of ordinary parents, in the same way as every other baby, in a granary below a crowded inn. The only supernatural event was Gabriel’s announcement; everything after it unfolded through human faith and doubt, courage and fear. (122:3.4, 122:8.2)
