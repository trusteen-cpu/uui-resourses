Introduction — Life After Failure

Paper 76 is the last chapter in the story of Adam and Eve. Where the previous paper dealt with the moment of default, this one deals with the life that followed. Having left the first garden to the Nodites, Adam’s company could not go west for lack of boats, nor north because the Nodites were on the march, nor south because of hostile tribes. Only the east lay open, and they journeyed toward the pleasant lands between the Tigris and Euphrates. (76:0.1)

Cain and Sansa were born before the caravan reached its destination. Laotta, Sansa’s mother, died in childbirth; Eve suffered much but survived, and she took Sansa to her bosom and reared her along with Cain. Sansa grew into a woman of great ability and became the wife of Sargan, chief of the northern blue races. The mood of the paper begins here, with Eve embracing even the child born of her mate’s misstep. (76:0.2)

I. The Edenites Enter Mesopotamia — By the Sweat of Their Faces

The caravan took almost a full year to reach the Euphrates, and finding it in flood they camped nearly six weeks before crossing. Hearing that the king and high priest of Eden was coming, the inhabitants fled to the eastern mountains, and Adam found the land vacated. The site had been one of the three original choices of Van and Amadon’s committee, and to the north, where the two rivers came close together, a defense wall of fifty-six miles could be built. (76:1.1, 76:1.2, 76:1.3)

They found the first garden partially prepared for them, but the second had to be created by the labor of their own hands and in the “sweat of their faces.” (76:1.4)

II. Cain and Abel — Environment and Repentance

Abel was the first child of Adam and Eve born in the second garden. At twelve he chose to be a herder, while Cain had chosen agriculture, and each brought offerings to the priests. Abel noticed that his animal sacrifices were preferred and taunted his brother. Cain’s appeal to the first Eden’s preference for the fruits of the field had a justifiable precedent, for Adam had in fact sought to discourage animal sacrifice. (76:2.1, 76:2.2, 76:2.3)

There was a reason it turned out this way. Burdened with building, defense, and agriculture, and much depressed spiritually, Adam had entrusted worship and education to men of Nodite extraction, who soon reverted to pre-Adamic standards. Abel, moreover, never failed to remind Cain that Adam was not his father. When Cain was twenty and Abel eighteen, Abel’s taunts so infuriated Cain that he turned on him and slew him. (76:2.3, 76:2.4, 76:2.5)

Good social environment and proper education are indispensable soil and atmosphere for getting the most out of a good inheritance. (76:2.6)

Through Abel the text speaks of the weight of environment and education. Abel had an ideal inheritance, but the unfavorable surroundings of his youth virtually neutralized it; had he lived to twenty-five or thirty, he would have become an entirely different person. The observation that a bad environment can effectively spoil an excellent inheritance, at least in the younger years, speaks directly to parents and teachers today. (76:2.6)

Cain’s story leads to repentance. As a living symbol of the default he felt the unspoken resentment of those around him, and out of fear and some remorse he went to his mother Eve for spiritual help. When he honestly sought divine assistance, an Adjuster—never before present—came to indwell him. Cain went east to the land of Nod, became a great leader among one group of his father’s people, and throughout his life promoted peace between the Nodites and the Adamites. (76:2.8, 76:2.9)

But he now went to Eve, his mother, and asked for spiritual help and guidance, and when he honestly sought divine assistance, an Adjuster indwelt him. (76:2.8)

III. Life in Mesopotamia — Raising the Next Generation

Adam and Eve greatly missed their former home and the children taken to Edentia, yet they bore their diminished estate with grace and fortitude. Adam spent most of his time training his children and associates in civil administration, educational methods, and religious devotion, and thanks to that foresight his death made little difference to the conduct of his people’s affairs. (76:3.1, 76:3.2)

Had it not been for this foresight, pandemonium would have broken loose upon his death. (76:3.2)

The Sethite priesthood was a threefold undertaking of religion, health, and education: its priests officiated at ceremonies, served as physicians and sanitary inspectors, and taught in the schools. The text asks us not to confuse them with the debased and commercial priesthoods of later times. The Adamites produced the third alphabet, maintained writing, metalworking, pottery, and weaving, and left an architecture not excelled for thousands of years. (76:3.5, 76:3.8, 76:3.10)

Their religious concepts of Deity and the universe were advanced and more or less accurate, their health provisions were, for their time, excellent, and their methods of education have never since been surpassed. (76:3.10)

The text traces the persistence of fear and the struggle with so many diseases in present-day races to the fact that, owing to the early miscarriage of the uplift plans, our ancestors received so little of Adam’s life plasm. Adam tried to make up a little of this. A commission of twelve headed by Eve selected 1,682 women from the surrounding tribes, and 1,570 of their children grew to maturity. Reared among their mothers’ tribes, they formed the early beginnings of the Andite race. (76:4.6, 76:4.8)

There is a note on diet as well. Adam and Eve and their first generation of children ate no meat, living on “the fruits of the trees”; later descendants took dairy products, but many kept to a nonflesh diet, and most of these vegetarian tribes later moved east and mingled with the peoples of India. Eve, the text adds, did not suffer pain in childbirth; the severe pangs belonged only to the mixed races. (76:4.2, 76:4.4)

V. Death of Adam and Eve — Repentance, the Adjuster, and Michael’s Promise

Not long after the second Eden was founded, Adam and Eve were told that their repentance was acceptable and that they would be eligible to join the sleeping survivors. Their transgression had been an error of judgment, not the sin of deliberate rebellion. They had had no Adjusters as citizens of Jerusem or in the first garden, but soon after their reduction to mortal status they became conscious of a new presence within them. (76:5.1, 76:5.2)

they knew that they had failed as Material Sons of Satania, but they also knew that the Paradise career was still open to them as ascending sons of the universe. (76:5.2)

A comfort and also a mystery to them was the one personal message they ever received from Michael: a promise to call them from mortal slumber when he came to Urantia. They could not fully grasp its meaning, but they proclaimed that a Son of God would someday come, and Adam cherished the hope that strife-torn Urantia might yet become the envied planet of all Nebadon. Adam lived 530 years and died of old age; Eve had died nineteen years earlier of a weakened heart. (76:5.3, 76:5.4, 76:5.5)

They were buried in the center of the temple built to their plans soon after the colony’s wall was finished. The text says this was the origin of the practice of burying noted and pious men and women beneath the floors of places of worship. (76:5.5)

With Adam’s default ended a regime of physical representatives of the universe government that had lasted more than four hundred and fifty thousand years. No comprehensive plan for world welfare was proclaimed until Machiventa Melchizedek came in the times of Abraham. Yet the text says misfortune was not Urantia’s only lot, for that very background of darkness drew Michael to choose this world as the arena for revealing the Father’s love. (76:5.6, 76:5.7)

it is rather that the evil and sin on Urantia afforded the Creator Son a more striking background against which to reveal the matchless love, mercy, and patience of the Paradise Father. (76:5.7)

VI. Survival of Adam and Eve — Leaving as Sons of God, Returning as Sons of Man

Adam and Eve did not sleep long. On the third day after Adam’s death a special roll call was ordered by Lanaforge, and the pair were repersonalized in the resurrection halls of the mansion worlds of Satania together with 1,316 of their associates from the first garden. They passed quickly through the worlds of ascension to citizenship on Jerusem, and they now belong to the four and twenty counselors, the advisory-control body of Urantia. (76:6.2, 76:6.3)

They left Jerusem as permanent citizens—Sons of God; they returned as ascendant citizens—sons of man. (76:6.3)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

The narrator sums up the story of Adam and Eve as one of trial, tragedy, and triumph. They failed as Material Sons, but they repented, received Adjusters, entered the path of ascending sons, and at last returned to help the very world on which they had failed. Failure is not the end; it can be the starting point of another road to the same destination. That is the conclusion of the four-paper story of Eden. (76:5.2, 76:6.3, 76:6.4)

a story of trial, tragedy, and triumph, at least personal triumph for your well-meaning but deluded Material Son and Daughter (76:6.4)

Paper 77 turns to beings who have worked beside these stories all along: the midway creatures. It is good to read it remembering the part they played, from the days of Van and Amadon through the preparation of the Garden and the crisis of Adam.
