Introduction — The Salem Missionaries Go East

Where Paper 93 told of Machiventa’s work at Salem, Paper 94 follows his teaching as it spread eastward. The Salem teachers carried to the remotest tribes of Africa and Eurasia the gospel that faith and trust in the one universal God is the only price of divine favor. They worked by training native converts and sending them out as teachers among their own people. (94:0.1)

Urantia has never had more enthusiastic and aggressive missionaries of any religion than these noble men and women who carried the teachings of Melchizedek over the entire Eastern Hemisphere. (94:0.1)

The paper evaluates the religions of the East, but by a consistent standard: how close each tradition came to the Salem gospel of a personal God and salvation by faith, and what it missed. It should therefore be read both as a comparison and as an expression of respect for the high achievements of each tradition.

I–II. The Salem Teachings in Vedic India; Brahmanism — A Gospel Rejected

In Melchizedek’s day India had recently come under the rule of Aryan-Andite invaders from the north and west. Their polytheism was a degeneration of an earlier monotheism, as each tribe came to venerate its own god, and the many gods were being reorganized into a pantheon under Dyaus pitar, Indra, and Agni. The one God preached by the Salem missionaries was not wholly out of harmony with the emerging idea of the Father-Brahma, but the nonritualistic Salem doctrine ran directly counter to the Brahman priesthood. (94:1.1, 94:1.3, 94:1.5)

To combat the Salem teachers, the Brahmans compiled the sacred writings of their day, which became the basis of the Rig-Veda. The text judges that the Vedas at their best equal any comparable writings, yet as they mingled with countless cults they became the most variegated system of theology ever developed by mortal man. (94:1.6, 94:1.7)

As the caste system hardened, the priestly caste went so far as to set itself alongside the gods. The doctrine of transmigration, which came from the Deccan, robbed people of the old hope of deliverance and advancement in death, and it led on to the doctrine of an eternal escape from self. The author weighs this course gravely. (94:2.1, 94:2.2, 94:2.3, 94:2.4)

Of all civilizations, the Vedic-Aryan paid the most terrible price for its rejection of the Salem gospel. (94:2.4)

In the age when the Brahmanas and Upanishads were compiled, the Brahmans tried to remove human elements from their concept of deity and fell into the error of depersonalizing God. In the same period Buddhism arose, but after a thousand years of success it gave way in northern India. (94:2.6, 94:2.7, 94:2.8)

III. Brahmanic Philosophy — Speculation That Stopped Short of Truth

The text counts the highest phase of Brahmanism among the most noble reaches of the mortal mind into philosophy and metaphysics. Yet its speculation never took up the essential dual concept of religion: the existence of the Universal Father and the ascending experience of creatures who seek him. Brahman was an Absolute understood only by negating every finite quality, and so it had almost no personal attributes that an individual could experience. (94:3.1, 94:3.2)

The decisive flaw was the failure to distinguish levels of reality: what may seem illusory on the absolute level may be absolutely real on the finite level. Brahmanism also failed to take account of the personality of the Universal Father, who is personally contactable on all levels. (94:3.7, 94:3.8)

IV. The Hindu Religion — The Most Adaptive Faith

Over the centuries the people of India returned largely to the Vedic rituals as crystallized by the Brahman priesthood. The text calls Hinduism the oldest and most cosmopolitan of the world’s religions and notes that present-day Hindu theology depicts four descending levels of deity. (94:4.1, 94:4.2)

Hinduism’s strength lies in its general tolerance and its flexibility, embracing everything from lofty speculation to simple folk practice. Having no great hierarchy, it survives because it is woven into the daily life of the people. The author says that India already has the philosophical framework and the cult structure; what it needs is the original gospel of Jesus, freed from Western dogma—a brotherhood realized in loving ministry and social service. (94:4.8, 94:4.9, 94:4.10)

V–VI. The Struggle for Truth in China; Lao-Tse and Confucius

The Salemites reached China in the middle of the second millennium before Christ and for more than a hundred years kept their headquarters at See Fuch, training Chinese teachers. The direct result was early Taoism, a religion vastly different from the one bearing that name today. (94:5.1, 94:5.2)

This belief became known in Japan as Shinto, and in China it blended with ancestor worship. Since Singlangton the Chinese had never been enslaved to priestcraft and so were early freed from abject fear of the gods, but the text judges that they went no further and fell into ancestor worship. Still, the Salem seed was not sown in vain; it became the root of the great philosophers of the sixth century. (94:5.6, 94:5.7, 94:5.8)

In the sixth century before Christ, when Melchizedek’s teachings were in danger of being submerged, the Salem gospel was restated through several human teachers. Lao-tse, building on the Salem traditions, declared Tao to be the One First Cause of all creation, taught that man’s destiny was everlasting union with Tao, and gave one of the earliest presentations of returning good for evil. (94:6.1, 94:6.3, 94:6.4)

“The Absolute Deity does not strive but is always victorious; he does not coerce mankind but always stands ready to respond to their true desires (94:6.6)

Confucius built on the better moral traditions of the yellow race and put morality in the place of magic. His writings became the moral basis of the culture of almost a third of Urantians, though he is also judged to have made a new fetish of order and somewhat stifled the spirit of investigation. In the end both men were deified by their followers. (94:6.9, 94:6.11, 94:6.12)

VII–VIII. Gautama Siddhartha; The Buddhist Faith

In the same century Gautama Siddhartha arose in India. He was heir to the throne of a petty chieftain of a small mountain valley; he fought the caste system and turned people from seeking salvation through physical affliction. He did not grasp the personality of the One Universal, but after his pupil Bautan told him of the Salem traditions, he took an advanced stand on salvation through faith. (94:7.1, 94:7.2, 94:7.3, 94:7.5)

Gautama was a real prophet (94:7.4)

His gospel of universal salvation, free from priests and sacrifice, came surprisingly near to a revival of the Salem gospel. Yet his son, in later years, wavered about divine favor through faith alone. Gautama’s original gospel can be summed up in a simple profession and a few teachings. (94:7.6, 94:7.7)

The text finds Gautama’s great truth in his proclamation of a universe of absolute justice, and calls his philosophy the ideal humanism, which removed the grounds for superstition and fear of ghosts. Its great weakness was that it did not produce a religion of unselfish social service. Even so, Gautama’s life was much greater than his preaching. (94:8.18, 94:8.19)

IX–X. The Spread of Buddhism; Religion in Tibet

Buddhism represented the Melchizedek truths better than any other religious system in eastern Asia. King Asoka trained and sent out more than seventeen thousand missionaries in twenty-five years, and in one generation made Buddhism the dominant religion of half the world. (94:9.1)

And generally speaking, it was a religion vastly superior to those which it supplanted or upstepped. (94:9.1)

But the farther it spread, the more Buddhism became a miraculized gospel that made Gautama a god. In the south the Hinayana clung to the early doctrine, while in the north the Mahayana cast off its social limitations. Today Buddhism remains a living religion that promotes calmness, self-control, and serenity. (94:9.2, 94:9.4, 94:9.5, 94:9.6)

In Tibet the Melchizedek teachings are found in the strangest combination with Buddhism, Hinduism, Taoism, and Christianity. The text judges that the resulting ritual has become inordinately cumbersome, and sums it up this way. (94:10.1, 94:10.2)

The Tibetans have something of all the leading world religions except the simple teachings of the Jesusonian gospel: sonship with God, brotherhood with man, and ever-ascending citizenship in the eternal universe. (94:10.3)

XI–XII. Buddhist Philosophy; The God Concept of Buddhism

The great advance of Buddhist philosophy was its grasp of the relativity of all truth. Teaching that small truth is for little minds and large truth for great minds allowed Buddhists to reconcile differences both within their own scriptures and with other religions. The teaching that the Buddha nature resides in all men is likewise counted among the clearest expressions of the truth of the indwelling Adjuster. (94:11.4, 94:11.5)

And this teaching is one of the clearest presentations of the truth of the indwelling Adjusters ever to be made by a Urantian religion. (94:11.5)

Such concepts helped intellectuals unify their philosophy, but the text says that even an anthropomorphic Yahweh has greater religious value than a remote Absolute, for the multitudes hungered for the promise that faith in God would assure divine favor and survival. That hunger finally bore fruit in Japan in the belief in Amida Buddha, which taught salvation by faith in the mercy of Amida. (94:11.12, 94:11.13, 94:12.2, 94:12.3)

The text names Buddhism’s freedom to choose truth from all religions as its great strength and looks with hope to its twentieth-century renaissance. The paper then closes with a sentence addressed to the peoples of every faith. (94:12.4, 94:12.5)

The hour is striking for presenting to Buddhism, to Christianity, to Hinduism, even to the peoples of all faiths, not the gospel about Jesus, but the living, spiritual reality of the gospel of Jesus. (94:12.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 94 shows both the heights and the limits of the religions of the East. The deep speculation of Brahmanic philosophy, the insight of Lao-tse, and the sincerity of Gautama all touched the Salem gospel, and the way their teachings were clouded by deification and ritualism is something Western religion has experienced as well. The purpose of the comparison is not to rank religions but to train the eye to recognize the core—a personal God, faith, and service—wherever it appears.
