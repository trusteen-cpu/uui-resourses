Introduction — Two Beings Made to Serve Together

Paper 74 follows Adam and Eve from the moment of their arrival on Urantia through the life they built in Eden. They came 37,848 years before A.D. 1934, in midseason, when the Garden was at the height of bloom. The seraphic transports settled near the temple of the Father, and after ten days of rematerialization the two regained consciousness at the same moment. The narrator is the same seraphim as in the previous paper, Solonia. (74:0.1, 74:8.15)

It is the essence of their service at all times and in all places never to be separated. (74:0.1)

This sentence is the key to the whole paper. Adam and Eve were designed to work as a pair, and most of the achievements and reforms the paper describes were accomplished by the two of them together. At the same time the paper ends by foreshadowing their default, so we read each scene with both hope and regret. (74:0.1, 74:8.14)

I. Adam and Eve on Jerusem — Volunteers Well Prepared

The pair were members of the senior corps of Material Sons on Jerusem, jointly number 14,311, and a little more than eight feet tall. For more than fifteen thousand years they had directed the division of experimental energy as applied to the modification of living forms, and before that they had taught in the citizenship schools for new arrivals. The narrator asks us to bear this record in mind when we come to their later conduct. (74:1.1, 74:1.2)

When volunteers were called for the Urantia mission, the entire senior corps volunteered, and the Melchizedek examiners made the final choice. Loyal to Michael during the Lucifer rebellion, Adam and Eve were nonetheless examined and thoroughly instructed before the System Sovereign, and were advised to regard themselves as subject to the Melchizedek receivers. They left behind one hundred children on Jerusem and departed amid the acclaim and well-wishing of its citizens. (74:1.3, 74:1.4, 74:1.5, 74:1.6)

II–III. Arrival of Adam and Eve; Adam and Eve Learn about the Planet — Welcome and Loneliness

The first faces the pair saw on awakening were those of Van and Amadon. The tongue of Eden was an Andonic dialect that Van and Amadon had improved with a new alphabet of twenty-four letters, and Adam and Eve had mastered it before leaving Jerusem. Carrier pigeons spread the word that the promised Son had come; the senior Melchizedek delivered the charge of planetary custody; and Gabriel’s broadcast decreed the second judgment roll call as the third planetary epoch opened. (74:2.1, 74:2.2, 74:2.3, 74:2.6, 74:2.8)

Yet on the night of their welcome the two became painfully aware of their planetary isolation. The familiar broadcasts were silent, and every circuit of extraplanetary communication was absent. Their fellows had gone to well-ordered worlds, but here the fallen Planetary Prince was still present, shorn of most of his power yet still able to make their task difficult. (74:3.1)

they walked and talked far into the night, their first night on earth—and it was so lonely. (74:3.2)

The second day’s lesson is especially weighty. The two learned of the utter collapse of the Caligastia scheme for accelerating social evolution and came to see the folly of seeking progress apart from the divine plan. On the fourth day Eve shared with a man the honors and responsibilities of world affairs, which the text stresses was an astounding innovation, the first such thing on earth since the days of Dalamatia. IV. The First Upheaval — Worship Belongs to the Father Alone

The amazing events of those six days were too much even for the best minds of the world. On the night after the sixth day, the temporary administrators concluded that Adam and Eve were far too modest and unassuming—that they were in reality gods, or near enough to be worshiped. Van protested but was swept aside, and he sent the leader of the midwayers in haste to Adam. The people meant well; they simply could not understand the simplicity of their new rulers’ fraternal and democratic manner. (74:4.1, 74:4.2, 74:4.3)

Adam made it plain that he would accept any honor and receive all respect, but worship never! (74:4.4)

Just before noon on the seventh day, Adam and Eve pointed the people to the Father’s temple and bade them worship him who made us all. The pair stood alone on the mount with bowed heads while the people bowed down around the temple. This was the origin of the Sabbath-day tradition. The scene sets in sharp contrast the human tendency to make idols of leaders and the true leader’s way of turning that glory toward God. (74:4.5, 74:4.6)

V. Adam’s Administration — Between Ideal and Reality

After almost seven years the Melchizedek receivers returned to Jerusem. Adam asked them several times to stay, but always the petitions were denied; the time had come for the Material Sons to assume full responsibility. Van and Amadon were translated that same night. Adam fostered the arts of manufacture to develop trade, and when Eden was disrupted over one hundred primitive manufacturing plants were in operation. (74:5.1, 74:5.2, 74:5.3)

But his efforts to establish a world government met stubborn resistance at every turn. Caligastia and Daligastia, deposed but not removed from the planet, opposed his work outside the Garden with well-planned resistance, and even within Eden some minds confused by the teaching of unbridled personal liberty upset his best plans. Adam had come to institute representative government but found none worthy of the name, and he fell back on Van’s method of organization. (74:5.5, 74:5.6, 74:5.7)

The sending of ambassadors from one tribe to another dates from the times of Adam. (74:5.8)

VI–VII. Home Life of Adam and Eve; Life in the Garden — Play and the Golden Rule

Before the default Eve bore sixty-three children, and when they left the Garden the family numbered four generations, 1,647 pure-line descendants. There was no cooking in Adam’s household; they ate fruits, nuts, and cereals once a day. Their bodies gave forth a shimmer of light, but since clothing obscured it only the glow from their heads could be seen—the origin of the halo tradition. They could exchange thoughts over about fifty miles, but this power was instantly suspended upon the mind’s surrender to the discord of evil. (74:6.2, 74:6.4, 74:6.5, 74:6.6)

The sight of Adam’s children playing simply for the fun of it was something new on Urantia, and the text says that much of the play and humor of present-day races comes from the Adamic stock. The whole purpose of the western schools was socialization, while religious and sexual training were left to the home as the duty of parents. (74:6.7, 74:7.2)

The play and humor of the present-day races are largely derived from the Adamic stock. (74:6.7)

The schools, and indeed every activity of the Garden, were always open to visitors. Unarmed observers could enter freely; to stay, a person had to be “adopted,” learning the plan of the Adamic bestowal and declaring loyalty to the social rule of Adam and the spiritual sovereignty of the Universal Father. It was a community that asked for both openness and commitment. (74:7.11)

Adam showed sex equality by the very way Eve worked at his side. VIII. The Legend of Creation — Adam’s Six Days Become Creation’s Six Days

The last section traces the origin of the six-day creation story. It grew from the tradition that Adam and Eve spent just six days surveying the Garden—days not planned in advance but worked out one at a time. The choice of the seventh day for worship was wholly incidental to those events. The story of Eve made from Adam’s rib is a confused condensation of Adam’s arrival with the celestial surgery that accompanied the coming of the Prince’s staff. (74:8.1, 74:8.3)

The text says that the idea of evolution is not a modern discovery. Away from the influence of Dalamatia and Eden, belief in gradual ascent was common, and many primitive tribes chose animals they took for ancestors as totems. The Old Testament account of creation dates from long after Moses and was shaped after the Babylonian captivity by a modified Mesopotamian version. The outlook of a golden age and a fall, the text judges, was discouraging because it rested on belief in retrogression rather than progression. (74:8.5, 74:8.7, 74:8.9, 74:8.13)

The “golden age” is a myth, but Eden was a fact, and the Garden civilization was actually overthrown. (74:8.14)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Adam and Eve carried on in the Garden for one hundred and seventeen years. This paper carefully records what they achieved: an administration shared by men and women, the resolve to direct worship to God, schools that taught play and the golden rule. Then, in its final sentence, it foretells how the impatience of Eve and the errors of judgment of Adam led them to turn aside from the ordained way. Even a great beginning can falter when patience is lost—this is the lesson carried into the next paper. (74:8.14)
