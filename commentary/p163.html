Introduction — Intensive Training at Magadan

A few days after the return from Jerusalem, Abner came from Bethlehem to Magadan with about fifty of his disciples. Gathered at the camp were the evangelistic corps, the women’s corps, and about one hundred and fifty tried disciples from all parts of Palestine. Jesus opened an intensive course of training for them, intending to choose seventy teachers from among them and send them out. (163:0.1)

The training ran from Friday, November 4, to the Sabbath, November 19. Jesus lectured every morning, and the apostles divided the work according to their experience and gifts. (163:0.1, 163:0.2)

I. Ordination of the Seventy — Lambs Among Wolves

On the Sabbath afternoon of November 19, amid passing showers, more than four hundred believers watched the ordination by the shore of the Sea of Galilee. The seventy were made up of ten of Abner’s fellow apostles of John, fifty-one of the earlier evangelists, and eight other disciples who had distinguished themselves in the service of the kingdom; Abner was made their chief. (163:1.1, 163:1.2)

Before laying his hands on them, Jesus told them how they were to go. They were to go two and two, without purse or extra clothing, stay in a home where peace-loving people lived, and not move from house to house for the sake of better lodging. (163:1.3)

therefore be as wise as serpents while you are also as harmless as doves. (163:1.3)

Even when leaving a city that rejected the gospel, they were not to withdraw their message. People may refuse the truth, but the fact that the kingdom of God has come near remains. The next morning Abner sent the thirty-five pairs out to the cities of Galilee, Samaria, and Judea; they taught for about six weeks and returned on December 30 to the new camp near Pella. (163:1.4, 163:1.6)

Notwithstanding you reject the truth, it remains that the kingdom of God has come near you. (163:1.4)

II. The Rich Young Man and Others — Undivided Affections

More than fifty disciples who sought ordination as members of the seventy were rejected by a committee made up of Andrew, Abner, and the acting head of the evangelistic corps. Candidates on whom the committee disagreed were brought to Jesus, and he never directly turned anyone away. But more than a dozen, after talking with him, withdrew of their own accord. (163:2.1)

To a disciple who wished first to go and bury his father, Jesus said he could return home to care for his loved ones and still remain a loyal disciple. But the messengers of the gospel, he said, were those who had forsaken all. The distinction matters: Jesus clearly separated being a disciple from being an ordained messenger. (163:2.2, 163:2.3)

The rich young man Matadormus, a member of the Sanhedrin, said he had kept the commandments from his youth and asked about eternal life. Jesus answered that since he believed the gospel, he was already a disciple and a child of the kingdom. But Matadormus was not content to be a disciple; he wanted to be a messenger, and Jesus told him to sell his goods, give them away, and follow him. (163:2.4, 163:2.5)

salvation is the reward of faith, not merely of works. (163:2.4)

He went away sorrowful. The text explains that what Jesus wanted was to deliver him not from his wealth as such but from the love of wealth. Had he given up his property, it would probably have been put right back into his hands as treasurer of the seventy. Years later he did obey the Master’s injunction, but by then it was too late to be one of the seventy. (163:2.6, 163:2.7, 163:2.9)

Almost every human being has some one thing which is held on to as a pet evil, and which the entrance into the kingdom of heaven requires as a part of the price of admission. (163:2.7)
The spiritual loyalties of the kingdom are incompatible with servility to materialistic mammon. (163:2.10)

The text also draws attention to freedom of choice in this episode. The forces of the spiritual world do not coerce a person; they allow him to go the way he has chosen. The decision always remains a person’s own. (163:2.8)

III. The Discussion about Wealth — The Parable of the Vineyard Laborers

After the young man left, Jesus said how hard it is for those who trust in riches to enter the spiritual life. When the astonished Peter asked who then could be saved, he answered that not all who have wealth are excluded, and that with God all things are possible. He grieved that Matadormus had not stayed, for he greatly loved him. (163:3.1, 163:3.2, 163:3.3)

If one’s wealth does not invade the precincts of the soul, it is of no consequence in the spiritual life of those who would enter the kingdom. (163:3.3)

To Peter’s question about what those who had left everything would receive, Jesus answered that they would receive many times as much in this world and eternal life in the world to come, adding that the first may be last. Then he told the parable of laborers hired at different hours of the day who all receive a denarius. The point of the parable is that the Father deals with his creatures according to their needs and in accordance with his just laws of merciful consideration. (163:3.4, 163:3.5, 163:3.7)

IV. Farewell to the Seventy — Seven Charges and Six Virtues

On the morning the seventy departed, Jesus emphasized seven points in his farewell talk. Then Peter took them aside and preached a sermon expounding the Master’s ordination charge, urging six virtues upon them. (163:4.1, 163:4.9)

The text puts no symbolism on the number seventy. The Jews did regard seventy as a significant number, but it was an accident that this group numbered exactly seventy. Jesus would have accepted at least six more, but they were unwilling to pay the price of giving up wealth and family. (163:4.17)

V–VI. Moving the Camp to Pella; The Return of the Seventy

On December 6 a company of nearly three hundred moved to Pella, near the place where Jesus had been baptized, by the spring where John the Baptist had once camped. David Zebedee, on his own initiative, brought the camp equipment from Bethsaida and prepared to receive nearly fifteen hundred people, for even in the rainy season earnest seekers came from all over Palestine and the Roman world. (163:5.1, 163:5.2, 163:5.3)

When the seventy returned on December 30, their reports delayed the evening meal by more than an hour. Jesus saw that people would go out proclaiming the good news without his presence, and he knew his departure would not greatly hinder the progress of the kingdom. He told them to rejoice in something other than the subjection of minor spirits. (163:6.1, 163:6.2)

be not lifted up because of this experience but the rather rejoice that your names are written on the rolls of heaven, and that you are thus to go forward in an endless career of spiritual conquest. (163:6.2)

Just before the evening meal Jesus, in one of those rare moments of emotional ecstasy, gave thanks to the Father that this gospel had been hidden from the wise and self-righteous but revealed to the children of the kingdom, and that the good news would spread through all the world after his departure. He said that when he had finished his revelation to his brethren in the flesh, he would continue it to the creatures on high. Then he turned to his disciples and reminded them of the privilege they enjoyed. (163:6.3)

Blessed are the eyes which see and the ears which hear these things. (163:6.4)

He called blessed the communities that had received the gospel, yet lamented over Chorazin, Bethsaida-Julias, and Capernaum, which had not received the messengers well. The more light a place has received, the heavier its responsibility for rejecting that light. (163:6.5)

The next day he solemnly warned the seventy against the subtlety of spiritual pride: if they understood the fall of Lucifer, they would shun every form of spiritual pride. He told them not to grow weary in well doing and repeated his invitation to all who labor and are heavy laden. (163:6.6, 163:6.7)

Take my yoke upon you and learn of me, for I am true and loyal, and you shall find spiritual rest for your souls. (163:6.7)

VII. Preparation for the Last Mission — The Phase of Spiritual Depth

The Pella camp prepared for a final three-month tour throughout Perea. Jesus no longer needed to go far afield; people came to him from Palestine, the Roman world, and the Near East. The twelve women trained a new women’s corps of fifty, and Perpetua, Peter’s wife, took charge of the enlarged women’s ministry. (163:7.1, 163:7.2, 163:7.3)

The text calls this the phase of spiritual depth, in contrast to the wonder-seeking crowds of the Galilean days. Even so, not a few still failed to grasp the truth that the kingdom of heaven is the spiritual brotherhood of man founded on the universal fatherhood of God. (163:7.4)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 163 marks the turning point at which the kingdom’s work passes from Jesus alone to many trained hands. At its center is a clear distinction between levels of calling. Anyone becomes a child of the kingdom by faith, but special service carries the price of undivided affections. And whether to pay that price is always a person’s own decision. (163:2.4, 163:2.8, 163:3.3)
