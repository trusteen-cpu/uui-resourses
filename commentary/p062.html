Introduction — Where Did Man Come From?

In Paper 62 a Life Carrier resident on Urantia introduces, one after another, the immediate animal ancestors of mankind. About one million years ago, three successive and sudden mutations from early stock of the lemur type of placental mammal produced human beings. The paper follows these three stages under the names dawn mammals, mid-mammals, and Primates. (62:0.1, 62:4.7)

The paper also sets out the line of descent. The dominant factors came from the western, or later American, life group, reinforced by contributions from the central life implantation that evolved in Africa; the eastern life group contributed little or nothing to the actual production of the human species. The paper is a biological chronicle, but it is also the record of the moment when mind and will first awoke. (62:0.1)

I. The Early Lemur Types — A Long Migration to a Sheltered Peninsula

The early lemurs in mankind’s ancestry were not directly related to the gibbons and apes then living in Eurasia and northern Africa. Nor were they descended from the modern lemur; the ancestor the two shared has long been extinct. Several million years ago North American lemurs crossed the Bering land bridge westward and slowly worked their way southwest along the Asiatic coast. (62:1.1, 62:1.2)

They came at last to a salubrious region west of India, between the then expanded Mediterranean and the rising mountains of the Indian peninsula. Later the coast sank, and this Mesopotamian or Persian peninsula became isolated, reachable only from the north—and that route was often cut off by glaciers. In this sheltered corner two great groups branched apart: the simian tribes of modern times and the human species. (62:1.2, 62:1.3)

II. The Dawn Mammals — The Beginnings of Fear and Tribal Spirit

A little more than one million years ago the Mesopotamian dawn mammals suddenly appeared. They were active little flesh eaters almost three feet tall, able to stand erect with ease, and they had a primitive opposable thumb. For their size they had the largest brains of any animal yet to exist, and they showed curiosity, elation at success, tenderness in the family, and a sense of self-abasement bordering on shame. (62:2.1, 62:2.3)

They reached full growth at three or four years of age and had a potential life span of about twenty years. They were touchingly loyal to their mates and fought fiercely to defend their kindred. Even in so short a life, the outline of the emotions and instincts that would later mark man was already being drawn. (62:2.2, 62:2.3)

Because they were small and keenly aware of the dangers of the forest, these creatures developed an extraordinary fear, which led them to wise precautions such as building shelters in the high treetops. Here the Life Carrier points to one root of the human temperament. (62:2.4)

The beginning of the fear tendencies of mankind more specifically dates from these days. (62:2.4)

They were highly gregarious yet quick to fight, and through selective survival, as superior groups warred on inferior neighbors, the species steadily improved. Just seventy generations after their origin from the highest lemur ancestor, the ancestors of the next stage suddenly appeared. (62:2.5, 62:2.6)

III. The Mid-Mammals — Where Man and Ape Parted Ways

In the treetop home of a superior pair of dawn mammals, twins were born. This brother and sister grew to a little over four feet tall, walked upright, and had almost perfectly opposable thumbs. From early on they were recognized as heads of the tribe, and they instituted a primitive form of social organization and a crude division of labor. Their twenty-one children formed the nucleus of the mid-mammals. (62:3.1, 62:3.2, 62:3.3)

The mid-mammals could show disgust, hid food for later use, and had a hoarding instinct for collecting stones. They were the first mammals to provide for safety in both treetop and underground shelters. But as their numbers grew, competition for food and mates led to internecine battles that nearly destroyed the species, until only one group of fewer than a hundred was left. (62:3.6, 62:3.7, 62:3.8)

You can hardly realize by what narrow margins your prehuman ancestors missed extinction from time to time. (62:3.9)

The closest call was a lightning bolt. It struck the tree where the future parents of the Primates twins were sleeping and killed three of their seven children. The surviving couple led the more progressive group about two miles away, and there the first Primates twins were born. At the same time an inferior pair in the tribe also had twins, and these became the founders of the modern simian tribes. (62:3.9, 62:3.10, 62:3.11)

So the paper draws a clear line in describing the relation between man and the apes. They came from the same tribe and species, but not from the same parents; they are linked only through their common origin in the mid-mammals. (62:3.12, 62:3.13)

In this section chance and choice overlap. The inferior couple survived only by hiding for more than two weeks in an underground food store during the tribe’s last fierce battle, while the superior pair were born in a new home because their leader parents made a decision to move. Even within one tribe, what was chosen made a great difference in where the descendants would go. (62:3.13, 62:3.9)

IV–V. The Primates; The First Human Beings — Twins Born 993,419 Years Ago

The Primates twins stood erect from the beginning, grew to over five feet tall, and communicated with each other by signs and sounds, though they could never make their people understand these symbols. The Primates had fully developed human hands and feet, yet they did not have minds that could really be called human. After almost nine hundred generations, about twenty-one thousand years from the origin of the dawn mammals, the Primates gave birth to the first two true human beings. (62:4.1, 62:4.4, 62:4.6)

the Primates suddenly gave birth to two remarkable creatures, the first true human beings. (62:4.6)

The most remarkable advance in these twins was the appearance of the worshipful feelings: awe, reverence, humility, and even a primitive gratitude. Higher sentiments such as pity and shame, love and jealousy also appeared in rudimentary form. By ten they had worked out a language of almost fifty ideas, and at about nine they went down the river and held a momentous conference, at which they agreed to live with and for each other. (62:5.4, 62:5.5, 62:5.7, 62:5.8)

The celestial intelligences watched this decision with intense interest, but they did not control the twins’ minds. The Life Carriers guided their surroundings only within permissible limits; the journey north was the twins’ own intelligent choice. Just before they left, their mother gave her life to save them from a gibbon raid, and their father, left behind, wandered off in grief and died. (62:5.9, 62:5.10, 62:5.11)

These ministers of mind had widened their contact as the brain capacity of evolving animals increased. The section shows how the ministry of mind opened step by step alongside biological evolution. VII. Recognition as an Inhabited World — The Circuit Opened by a First Decision

Over the newly established planetary mind circuit came the first message from Salvington. It called the twins’ resolve the first decision of the human type of mind and said that this decision had automatically established the circuit of communication. (62:7.1, 62:7.2, 62:7.3)

This is the first decision of mind—the human type of mind—on Urantia (62:7.3)

The Life Carrier makes clear that we should not think of the Life Carriers as interfering arbitrarily and mechanically in the natural unfolding of planetary evolution. What they had been permitted until then was a special but wholly natural supervision—manipulating the environment and shielding the life plasm—and this had to end once beings with will appeared. When creatures can choose, the manner of care must change. (62:7.4)

So the Life Carriers’ long supervision formally came to an end. Because Urantia was a life-modification world, two senior Life Carriers and twelve assistants were permitted to remain, and the author of this paper was one of them. Urantia was formally recognized as a planet of human habitation 993,408 years before A.D. 1934. (62:7.4, 62:7.6, 62:7.7)

Biologic evolution had once again achieved the human levels of will dignity; man had arrived on planet 606 of Satania. (62:7.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 62 traces a long chain of evolution, but at its end stands a decision. The choice of two young twins to live for each other and to set out in search of a better way opened the universe circuits. Our tendency to fear we inherited from distant ancestors, but worship, wisdom, and purposeful decision have been ours since the day we became human. (62:2.4, 62:6.5)
