Introduction — A Word for Each One

About seven o’clock on Tuesday morning Jesus met the apostles, the women’s corps, and some two dozen other prominent disciples at Simon’s home. He said farewell to Lazarus, giving him the instruction that soon led him to flee to Philadelphia, and he said good-bye to the aged Simon. He also gave his parting advice to the women’s corps, for he never again formally addressed them. (174:0.1)

That morning Jesus greeted each of the twelve with a personal salutation. His words go straight to each man’s temperament and to the trial awaiting him. Remembering the very different reactions of the apostles described in the previous paper, we can see how personal these brief charges were. (174:0.2)

The word to Peter about weapons and the word to Judas about ridicule connect directly with the events of the preceding papers. Jesus knew of the hidden swords and of the ridicule that had shaken Judas, yet instead of exposing and rebuking them, he offered his counsel as a parting charge. (174:0.2)

After these greetings Jesus set out for Jerusalem with Andrew, Peter, James, and John, while the others went to establish the Gethsemane camp, which became their headquarters for the rest of the Master’s life in the flesh. About halfway down Olivet Jesus paused and talked with the four for more than an hour, and from that conversation came his teaching on forgiveness. (174:0.3)

I. Divine Forgiveness — The Understanding Love of a Parent

Where they had stopped on the slope, Peter asked a question. James held that the Father forgives us even before we ask, while Peter maintained that repentance and confession must come first. Which of them was right? Jesus answered that both erred, because neither grasped the nature of the intimate and loving relations between the creature and the Creator. (174:1.1, 174:1.2)

Jesus explained with the picture of a wise parent. In a relationship of love and understanding, the very estrangements that would later require repentance and forgiveness never arise. Between the earthly child and the heavenly Father, the divine parent has infinite sympathy and understanding, so divine forgiveness is inevitable, inherent in God himself. (174:1.2, 174:1.3)

It is indeed doubtful whether intelligent and affectionate parents are ever called upon to forgive an average and normal child. (174:1.2)
Divine justice is so eternally fair that it unfailingly embodies understanding mercy. (174:1.3)

A part of every father, Jesus said, lives in the child. The parent sees the child’s immaturity in the light of riper experience and so enjoys a priority of understanding. The same principle holds between people. Whoever understands the inner impulses of his fellows will love them, and this capacity to understand human nature and forgive apparent wrongdoing is Godlike. (174:1.3, 174:1.4)

Because the child is immature, he often feels a guilty sense of separation from his father’s approval, but the true father is never conscious of any such separation. That is why Jesus said that sin is an experience of creature consciousness, not a part of God’s consciousness. This teaching brings deep comfort to anyone weighed down by guilt. (174:1.4)

And when you love your brother, you have already forgiven him. (174:1.4)

Conversely, an inability or unwillingness to forgive is the measure of our immaturity, and we hold grudges in direct proportion to our ignorance of the inner nature of others. To see forgiveness not merely as an act of will but as a widening of understanding is an insight of great practical use in real relationships. (174:1.5)

Love is the outworking of the divine and inner urge of life. It is founded on understanding, nurtured by unselfish service, and perfected in wisdom. (174:1.5)

II–IV. The Trap Questions — Tribute, Resurrection, and the Great Commandment

On Monday evening the Sanhedrin met with some fifty additional leaders chosen from the scribes, Pharisees, and Sadducees. They agreed that arresting Jesus in public was dangerous and that he must first be discredited before the people. So Pharisees, Sadducees, and even Herodians united in a plan to fill the whole day with difficult questions. (174:2.1, 174:4.1)

To the question of tribute Jesus answered by pointing to the image on the coin. The narrator explains that a well-known ruling of the Sanhedrin already held that the right of coinage carried with it the right to levy taxes. Jesus did not dodge the question; he used the wisdom of a double reply. (174:2.2, 174:2.5)

Having failed the day before to trip him on ecclesiastical authority, the rulers now tried to entangle him in civil authority. Both Pilate and Herod were in Jerusalem. If he advised against the tribute, they could charge him with sedition before Rome; if he advised paying it, they could alienate the multitude by wounding its national pride. Even the youths who set the trap marveled at his answer. (174:2.3, 174:2.4)

Jesus was never evasive, but he was always wise in his dealings with those who sought to harass and destroy him. (174:2.5)

Because the Sadducees accepted only the five Books of Moses, Jesus appealed to Moses alone. He firmly affirmed the survival of mortals through the resurrection, but he did not in any sense endorse the belief in the resurrection of the literal human body. The point he stressed was that God said, “I am the God of Abraham,” not “I was.” (174:3.2, 174:3.4)

And so, along with Moses, do I declare that my Father is not the God of the dead but of the living. (174:3.2)

The Sadducees aimed not at open persecution but at ridicule, knowing well that public persecution would only win him more sympathy. Yet another group of Sadducees, who had prepared questions about angels, saw what had befallen their comrades and wisely withdrew without a word. (174:3.5, 174:4.1)

The lawyer who asked about the greatest commandment saw that Jesus had answered in keeping with the highest concept of Jewish religion and openly commended him. Jesus told him he was not far from the kingdom of God, and that very night the man went to the camp near Gethsemane, professed faith in the gospel, and was baptized. Even among those who came to set a trap, there was one whose heart was open to truth. (174:4.2, 174:4.3, 174:4.4)

My friend, I perceive that you are not far from the kingdom of God. (174:4.3)

Near noon, when no more questions came, Jesus asked one of his own: if the Messiah is the son of David, how can David in the Psalm call him Lord? The rulers never answered, and the narrator records that after Jesus’ death they tried to escape the difficulty by reinterpreting the Psalm or by denying that David wrote it. (174:4.6)

The Pharisees had enjoyed the Sadducees’ defeat, and the Sadducees in turn enjoyed the Pharisees’ failure. But the rivalry was only momentary; they soon forgot their time-honored differences in their united effort to stop Jesus. Through it all the common people heard him gladly. (174:4.7)

V. The Inquiring Greeks — A Grain of Wheat Falls into the Earth

About noon a group of believing Greeks from Alexandria, Athens, and Rome asked Philip to let them see Jesus. Perplexed by the instruction not to teach publicly and by the fact that these men were foreign gentiles, Philip fetched Andrew, and together they brought the Greeks to Joseph’s home. Thirty of these men had decided the night before, at the home of Nicodemus, to enter the kingdom. (174:5.1, 174:5.2, 174:5.5)

With Jews and gentiles gathered in about equal numbers, Jesus perceived the end of one dispensation and the beginning of another. He said he would not sit in judgment on those who rejected him, for he had come not to judge the world but to offer it salvation. (174:5.4, 174:5.6, 174:5.7)

Grieving that the leaders of his people were set on rejecting him, Jesus noted that in that very room were a full score of men who had once sat in the Sanhedrin or the high councils of the nation. Yet some of them still shrank from openly confessing the truth for fear of being cast out of the synagogue. The temptation to love the glory of men more than the glory of God remains a test even for people of faith. (174:5.3)

except a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it abides alone; but if it dies in good soil, it springs up again to life and bears much fruit. (174:5.8)

Jesus confessed that his soul was troubled by the approaching hour, yet he did not pray to be saved from it. Instead he prayed that the Father would glorify his name and that his will be done. Then the Personalized Adjuster who had indwelt him before his baptism appeared and answered; the people heard no voice, but they sensed that an angel had spoken to him. (174:5.9, 174:5.10, 174:5.11)

I have glorified my name in your bestowals many times, and I will glorify it once more. (174:5.10)

Jesus told them this had happened not for his sake but for theirs, and he urged them to walk in the light while they had it. Then he led them all back to the temple to deliver his farewell discourse. (174:5.12, 174:5.13, 174:5.14)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 174 shows how, amid the tension of the final days, Jesus never lost his calm or his personal care. He left each of the twelve a charge suited to him, explained forgiveness through the understanding love of a parent, and met trap questions with neither evasion nor attack but with wisdom. And facing his approaching death, he chose the Father’s will. (174:0.2, 174:5.9)
