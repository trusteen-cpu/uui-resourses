Going into Jerusalem

Introduction — A Majestic Calm

Jesus and the apostles reached Bethany on Friday afternoon, March 31, A.D. 30. So many people came daily to talk with Lazarus about his resurrection that Jesus lodged with a neighboring believer named Simon. Many expected him to defy the Sanhedrin’s decree of death, enter Jerusalem, and proclaim himself king of the Jews. Lazarus, Martha, and Mary understood more fully that he was not that kind of king, and they dimly sensed that this might be his last visit. (172:0.1, 172:0.2)

The chief priests chose not to seize him among his friends and decided to wait until he came into Jerusalem. Jesus knew all this, yet he remained majestically calm; his friends had never seen him more composed and congenial. The apostles, by contrast, watched over him through the night in pairs, many of them girded with swords. This contrast sets the tone for the whole paper. (172:0.3)

The paper covers three days, from the Friday arrival to Sunday night: the feast at Bethany, the Sunday-morning instructions, the entry on a donkey, a walk about the temple, and the varied reactions of the twelve. On the surface it is a triumphal procession, but the narrator also shows the currents of rejection and betrayal running beneath it. (172:1.9, 172:5.12)

I. Sabbath at Bethany — Mary’s Ointment

On the evening after the Sabbath, the people of Bethany and Bethpage gave a public banquet at Simon’s home in honor of Jesus and Lazarus—a banquet held in open defiance of the Sanhedrin. Jesus had been playing with the children until he came to the table, and he talked with Simon about Joshua of old, whose namesake he was, and about the walls of Jericho. (172:1.2, 172:1.3, 172:1.4)

but I would cause the walls of prejudice, self-righteousness, and hate to crumble before this preaching of the Father’s love for all men. (172:1.3)

Near the close of the feast Mary opened a cruse of costly ointment, anointed the Master’s head, poured it on his feet, and wiped them with her hair. Some murmured at the waste, and Judas Iscariot went to Andrew saying the ointment should have been sold for the poor. Jesus laid his hand on Mary’s head and defended her: she had done a good thing in her heart. (172:1.5, 172:1.6)

The ointment cost as much as a man’s earnings for a year—enough to feed five thousand—so the murmuring was understandable. But Jesus said that the poor are always with us to be served whenever we choose, while he was soon going to the Father. Love has its moment, and a heart that recognizes that moment should not be overruled by the logic of cost. (172:1.6, 172:1.7)

Mary has reproved all of you in that by this act she evinces faith in what I have said about my death and ascension to my Father in heaven. (172:1.6)

Mary had believed his warnings about his death and had long saved to buy the ointment for his burial, and Lazarus and Martha heartily approved. The same scene worked on Judas very differently. He took the Master’s words as a personal reproof, and thoughts of revenge that had lingered in his subconscious now entered his open, conscious mind. That same day the chief priests concluded that Lazarus too must die. (172:1.7, 172:1.8, 172:1.9)

II. Sunday Morning with the Apostles — Final Instructions

On Sunday morning, in Simon’s garden, Jesus gave the twelve their final instructions before entering Jerusalem. They were to do no public work during this Passover, to stay near him, and to watch and pray. He knew that some carried hidden swords but said nothing of it. The apostles listened in silence as he reviewed their ministry from the day of their ordination. (172:2.1, 172:2.2)

He instructed them to remain near him and to “watch and pray.” (172:2.1)

Jesus also warned Lazarus not to sacrifice his life to the vengefulness of the Sanhedrin, and a few days later, when officers came to arrest him, Lazarus fled to Philadelphia. The followers dimly sensed the coming crisis, but the Master’s unusual cheerfulness and good humor kept them from grasping how serious it was. (172:2.4, 172:2.5)

III. The Start for Jerusalem — The King of Peace on a Donkey

Unlike Nazareth, Capernaum, and Jerusalem, Bethany had accepted him and believed in him. The narrator notes that he did not raise Lazarus so that the villagers might believe, but because they already believed. Until now Jesus had always suppressed public acclaim of himself as the Messiah, but this time he decided to let his disciples express their feelings freely. (172:3.1, 172:3.2)

He did not raise Lazarus that the villagers might believe, but rather because they already believed. (172:3.1)
Jesus did not decide to make this public entrance into Jerusalem as a last bid for popular favor nor as a final grasp for power. (172:3.3)

Among the many conflicting Messianic prophecies, he took as his guide the words of Zechariah about a lowly king riding upon an ass. What he had failed to accomplish by plain teaching, he would attempt through a symbolic appeal. Peter and John brought the colt from Bethpage. (172:3.4, 172:3.6)

As the procession climbed Olivet, the crowd spread their garments and branches and shouted hosannas. At the brow of the hill, where the city and temple came into view, Jesus halted the procession and wept, grieving that the city about to reject the Son of Peace did not know the time of its divine visitation. The thousands who came out from Jerusalem to meet him had been stirred up by David Zebedee and others; Jesus never premeditated anything dramatic. (172:3.7, 172:3.9, 172:3.10, 172:3.11)

And all this shall befall you because you knew not the time of your divine visitation. (172:3.10)
It would be useless to stop them lest in their stead these stones by the roadside cry out. (172:3.13)

The Pharisees hurried ahead to the Sanhedrin and reported that the people had gone mad over him and that, unless he was stopped, all the world would go after him. The whole city was stirred, and people asked, “Who is this man?” The multitude answered that he was the prophet of Galilee, Jesus of Nazareth. They hailed him as king, yet their answer stopped at “prophet.” (172:3.14, 172:3.16)

The narrator states plainly that no deep significance should be attached to this enthusiasm. The welcome was joyous and sincere, but it reflected no deep-seated conviction. Later that same week, when they realized he would not set up the kingdom they expected, the same crowds were just as quick to reject him. The warning to distinguish acclaim from faith applies to religious communities in every age. (172:3.15)

IV. Visiting about the Temple — The Widow’s Two Mites

After the entry, Jesus and ten of the apostles strolled about the temple. The Sanhedrin, fearing the people, made no move against him. The apostles little understood it, but the public entry was the only human procedure that could have prevented his immediate arrest on entering the city. Through it Jesus gave the people of Jerusalem and the Passover pilgrims one last chance to receive the Son of Peace. (172:4.1)

Toward evening they sat by the treasury and watched people make their offerings. The rich put in much, and then a poor, scantily clad widow cast in two mites. (172:4.2)

but this poor woman, even though she is in want, gave all that she had, even her living. (172:4.2)

Mary’s costly ointment and the widow’s tiny coins stand at opposite ends in value, yet Jesus saw the same thing in both: each gave not according to the size of her possessions but with the whole of her heart. (172:1.6, 172:4.2)

V. The Apostles’ Attitude — Twelve Different Nights

No one spoke on the way back to Bethany. The narrator says that no twelve human beings ever felt such diverse and inexplicable emotions, and he describes each apostle’s response in turn. The heart of the section is that the same event was received in entirely different ways according to each man’s temperament and expectations. (172:5.1)

John, Nathaniel, and Thomas each drew a little nearer to the meaning of the day—by way of symbol, of political reasoning, and of analysis. The twins simply enjoyed the day without analyzing it, and the memory carried them right up to the hour of the crucifixion. By contrast, Simon Zelotes and Peter, who had expected the most, were the most deeply disappointed. The passage quietly reminds us that the greater our expectations, the more a reality that does not match them can wound. (172:5.5, 172:5.10, 172:5.11)

For Judas the decisive moment was the ridicule of a Sadducee friend at the city gate. He had never shrunk from persecution, but he could not bear ridicule. To his long-nourished desire for revenge was now added the fear of being ashamed of his Master and his fellow apostles. (172:5.13)

At heart, this ordained ambassador of the kingdom was already a deserter (172:5.13)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 172 depicts a day of acclaim, but at its center is the calm of a Master unmoved by applause. Choosing a donkey rather than a horse, he showed by a symbol the kingdom of peace, and in the middle of a joyful procession he wept for the city. Through the very different ways twelve men experienced the same day, the paper also shows how strongly expectation governs what we see. (172:3.5, 172:3.10, 172:5.1)
