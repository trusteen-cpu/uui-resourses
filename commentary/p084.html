Introduction — The Channel of Culture and Knowledge

Following two papers on the evolution of marriage, Paper 84 examines its fruit, the home. The author sums up the story of marriage in a single sentence: material necessity founded it, sex hunger embellished it, religion sanctioned and exalted it, the state demanded and regulated it, and in later times evolving love has begun to glorify it as the creator of the home. (84:0.1)

home building should be the center and essence of all educational effort. (84:0.1)

Individuals are very temporary as planetary factors, but the family is a continuing agency of social evolution. The home is the one institution that embraces all three essential functions of human existence: self-maintenance, self-perpetuation, and self-gratification. This paper follows that institution from its beginnings toward its ideals. (84:0.2, 84:0.3)

The family is the channel through which the river of culture and knowledge flows from one generation to another. (84:0.2)

I. Primitive Pair Associations — The Nucleus of Mother and Child

Marriage did not grow out of sex relations. Primitive man could satisfy his sex desires without responsibility, and he did not even know the connection between sex relations and childbirth. People believed that babies were made in the spirit world and that pregnancy came when a spirit entered a woman’s body. What first drew a man to the shelter of a woman and her children was not love but hunger for food. (84:1.1, 84:1.2, 84:1.3)

Maternal love, by contrast, is instinctive. It does not come from the mores but is bestowed by the adjutant mind-spirits of the local universe, and its strength is in proportion to the length of the species’ helpless infancy. This strong attachment drew woman into the protective enclosure of marriage and, at the same time, put her at a great disadvantage in her struggle with man. (84:1.6, 84:1.7)

While the mother-child association is neither marriage nor home, it was the nucleus from which both sprang. (84:1.8)

The home came into being when these temporary partnerships lasted long enough to rear the offspring. The author says that the co-operation of one man and one woman is in most respects far superior to that of two of the same sex, and that the pairing of the sexes improved survival and became the beginning of human society. (84:1.8, 84:1.9)

II–III. From the Mother-Family to the Father-Family

Descent was at first reckoned only through the mother, because that was the only inheritance that was at all certain. In the mother-family the wife’s mother held the highest authority in the household, and the wife’s brothers were more involved in managing the family than the husband was. Then, when herding gave men control of the food supply, the mother-family quickly passed away. (84:2.1, 84:2.3, 84:2.6)

The author regards this change as one of the most radical about-faces the human race has ever made. Under father dominance the arbitrary authority of the father was the basis of family unity, and every society passed through a stage of patriarchal autocracy. The paper records calmly that woman’s low status came partly from the handicap of maternity, which kept her from being the hero of a crisis, and partly from her own exaltation of the warrior, which made her still more dependent. (84:2.7, 84:3.1, 84:3.3, 84:3.4)

Yet woman was always the real producer. Her first liberation came when man consented to till the soil and began to use captives as agricultural laborers instead of killing them. The milk of cows and goats greatly reduced infant mortality, and when men brought to agriculture the organizational techniques they had learned in hunting and war, farming methods greatly improved. (84:3.7, 84:3.8, 84:3.9, 84:3.10)

IV–V. Woman’s Status — A Measure of Civilization

the progress of marriage itself is a reasonably accurate gauge registering the advances of human civilization. (84:4.1)

The early woman was not a partner but a piece of property, and for a long time she was even regarded as having no soul. Taboos surrounding childbirth and menstruation made her unclean and dangerous. The author makes the folly of these ideas clear, yet also records the irony that the monthly seclusion gave overworked women a time of rest and meditation. (84:4.3, 84:4.5, 84:4.8, 84:4.9)

Woman’s social position has generally varied inversely with the degree of militarism in any nation or age. (84:5.3)

The author does not see this as men deliberately seizing women’s rights and then grudgingly giving them back; it was all a process of unplanned social evolution. Tribes that persisted in cruelty to women did not survive. And it was not religion but science and industry that truly emancipated woman, so that evolution accomplished what even revelation had failed to do. (84:5.4, 84:5.7, 84:5.8)

The weaker always makes disproportionate gains in every adjustment of the mores in the progressive evolution of society. (84:5.9)

The paper also sets the task that follows emancipation. How woman responds to her newly won recognition, dignity, and equality is the decisive test of the twentieth-century woman. Each sex has its own sphere and its own rights: woman cannot thrive on man’s rights, nor man on woman’s. Equality does not mean sameness. (84:5.10, 84:5.11, 84:5.12)

VI. The Partnership of Man and Woman — A Complement That Endures

Passion brings men and women together, but the weaker parental instinct and the social mores hold them together. Marriage is the highest manifestation of the antagonistic co-operation found where nature and society meet. The author frankly admits that man and woman are two distinct varieties of the same species, and that complete mutual understanding is impossible. (84:6.2, 84:6.3)

While the sexes never can hope fully to understand each other, they are effectively complementary (84:6.7)

This difference does not end with life on earth. The differing viewpoints of men and women continue through the morontia and spirit careers and into Havona, and even in the Corps of the Finality these personality trends are not erased. The two varieties will always intrigue, stimulate, and help each other. Hence the counsel to see the difference not as a concern but as a gift. (84:6.5, 84:6.6)

Marriage is the mother of all human institutions (84:6.8)

VII. The Ideals of Family Life — The First School of Brotherhood

When the home is good, society is good. The cultural stability of the Jewish and Chinese peoples came from strong family groups. Marriage is now passing out of the property stage into the personal era, and as woman ceases to be property, new mores are emerging to stabilize marriage and the home. (84:7.1, 84:7.3)

The animals love their children; man—civilized man—loves his children’s children. (84:7.10)

The large families of ancient times were wanted for reasons such as labor, security in old age, the extension of the family name, and fear of ghosts. Today’s ideal is different. Bringing a child into the world is not a parental right but carries the supreme responsibility of human existence; civilization regards the parents as assuming all duties and the child as having all the rights. A child’s respect grows not from the fact of being born but from care and training lovingly given. (84:7.11, 84:7.25, 84:7.26)

The paper’s observations on discipline are sharp as well. Ancient children learned early that disobedience meant failure or death, but civilization, by shielding children from the natural results of foolish conduct, sometimes fosters disobedience. The author names three factors that make modern child rearing difficult: a high degree of race mixture, artificial and superficial education, and parents absent so much that children cannot learn by watching and imitating them. (84:7.20, 84:7.21, 84:7.24)

The true parent is engaged in a continuous service-ministry which the wise child comes to recognize and appreciate. (84:7.26)

The home is the basic unit of fraternity, where patience, altruism, tolerance, and forbearance are learned. The author recommends that families follow the Andite family council, which discussed everything freely and frankly, in place of patriarchal rule. And a good home, he says, shows children the first of a long series of disclosures of the love of the Paradise parent. (84:7.28, 84:7.29, 84:7.30)

Family life is the progenitor of true morality, the ancestor of the consciousness of loyalty to duty. (84:7.30)

VIII. Dangers of Self-Gratification — The Pleasure Mania and the Home

The greatest threat to family life is the modern pleasure mania. Property has served as the institution of self-maintenance, and marriage as the institution of self-perpetuation, but no distinct institution for self-gratification was ever established. As a result, the pursuit of pleasure permeates every institution, and even marriage is easily regarded merely as a means of pleasure. (84:8.1, 84:8.2)

The play instinct and the sense of humor were new traits brought into human experience by the violet race, and the Adamic stock lifted them into new and glorified forms of self-gratification. But real danger arises when the restlessness, curiosity, and adventure of the post-Andite races combine with abandonment to pleasure. Even if every resource of art, color, and music is exhausted, these alone cannot elevate the soul or nourish the spirit, and vanity and fashion contribute nothing to home building. (84:8.3, 84:8.4)

The hunger of the soul cannot be satisfied with physical pleasures (84:8.4)

The author does not forbid enjoyment. Celestial beings too enjoy rest and the ministry of the reversion directors, and wholesome play, rest, and recreation all have value. Human beings are entitled to the full enjoyment of present joys. But if pleasure undermines property and destroys marriage and the home, it has been bought at a fatal price. (84:8.5, 84:8.6)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 84 portrays the family as man’s greatest purely human achievement. It began with the small nucleus of mother and child, passed through the antagonistic co-operation of man and woman, and became the place where children learn brotherhood and first glimpse the love of the Paradise parent. What guards this achievement is not the strength of institutions but the daily choice of parents to put responsibility before pleasure and service before rights. (84:6.8)
