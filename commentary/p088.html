Introduction — Not the Object, but the Spirit Within

Following the paper on the ghost cults, Paper 88 examines the very ancient belief that a spirit can enter an inanimate object, an animal, or a human being. Its author, a Brilliant Evening Star of Nebadon, defines this doctrine of spirit possession as fetishism. At first the fetish was thought to be inhabited by the ghost of a dead person; later, higher spirits were believed to reside in it. (88:0.1, 88:0.2)

The savage does not necessarily worship the fetish; he very logically worships and reverences the spirit resident therein. (88:0.1)

This perspective keeps us from treating fetishism as simple foolishness. The urge to tie an unseen power to something visible had its own logic for primitive man, and that logic bound together all his early ideas of ghosts, souls, spirits, and demon possession. The paper patiently traces the forms in which that logic survives today. (88:0.2)

I. Belief in Fetishes — Anything Unusual Became a Fetish

Primitive man made a fetish of anything out of the ordinary. If he fell ill and chanced to recover, the object he carried at the time became a fetish; so did objects connected with dreams. Volcanoes became fetishes but mountains did not; comets did, but stars did not. Unusualness, together with chance, made fetishes. (88:1.1)

Animals were especially frequent fetishes. An animal that had eaten human flesh could become one, and when a ghost was believed to dwell permanently in a fetish animal, fetishism could shade into the idea of reincarnation. The savage envied animals and did not feel superior to them, and he often named himself after his favorite beast. (88:1.4, 88:1.5)

The most consequential practice was making fetishes of human beings. A genius was thought to be a fetish personality indwelt by a wise spirit, and such persons soon began to resort to fraud and trickery for their own advantage. From the fetish person, revered as more than human and even infallible, grew the unlimited authority of chiefs, kings, priests, and church rulers. (88:1.9, 88:1.10)

Other details are striking as well: the ancients opposed taking a census because counting livestock or property was thought to bring bad luck, and primitive man did not make an undue fetish of sex and was not obscene. Superstitions stay with us longer than we think, and we should not be quick to look down on primitive people. (88:1.6, 88:1.7)

II. Evolution of the Fetish — From Relics to Words

Ghosts were believed to prefer to dwell in objects they had owned in life, and this belief led to the veneration of relics and to pilgrimages to the tombs of great men. The author sees the relics of modern religions as an attempt to rationalize the savage’s fetish and raise it to a place of dignity. (88:2.1, 88:2.2)

The hearth of the home became a sacred place, and shrines and temples became fetish places because they were at first burial places of the dead. The author relates that Moses exalted the Hebrew fetish hut into the place that housed a superfetish, the law of God, yet the Israelites never gave up the Canaanite belief that the spirit of God dwelt in stone altars. (88:2.3)

It is heathenish to believe in fetishes and magic but supposedly all right to accept relics and miracles. (88:2.2)

Images and idols were an elaborate form of fetishism. Moses tried to check fetish worship by adding the second commandment against graven images, and in his wisdom he did not sweep away the old fetishes all at once but had certain relics kept in the ark together with the law. But even that commandment later became a supreme fetish, used to stifle art and the enjoyment of beauty. (88:2.4, 88:2.5, 88:2.6)

The author warns against believing that everything in a book is true and that all truth is contained in it. Opening the book at random to let it decide one’s life, or swearing with a hand on the book, are forms of refined fetishism. Yet he fairly acknowledges that moving from fear of a chief’s nail parings to reverence for a collection of the moral wisdom of centuries is real progress. (88:2.7, 88:2.8, 88:2.9)

The practice of opening one of these sacred books to let the eye chance upon a passage, the following of which may determine important life decisions or projects, is nothing more nor less than arrant fetishism. (88:2.8)

III. Totemism — From Clan God to National Flag

Fetishism ran through every primitive cult, from sacred stones through idolatry and nature worship to totemism. Totemism combined social and religious observances: the totem was at once the symbol of the group and its god, the clan personified. The author regards it as one phase of the attempt to socialize an otherwise personal religion. (88:3.1, 88:3.2)

The totem eventually evolved into the flag, or national symbol, of the various modern peoples. (88:3.2)

Just as the medicine man never let his medicine bag touch the ground, people today take care that the flag does not touch the ground. The fetish of the supreme state evolved from clan to tribe, from suzerainty to sovereignty, from totem to flag. The author observes that man has even made a fetish of democracy: he takes lightly the ordinary thinking of one person, yet when it is gathered into “public opinion,” he honors it as the standard of justice. (88:3.3, 88:3.4)

The story of the totem does not condemn symbols as such. A symbol that binds a group together served its purpose as a stage of socialization. The trouble begins when the symbol is raised above the values it points to and is revered for its own sake, like the insignia of priesthood and kingship. The fetish kings who ruled by “divine right” are an example. (88:3.2, 88:3.4)

IV–V. Magic and Magical Charms — Techniques for an Illusory Spirit World

Civilized man attacks the problems of a real environment through his science; savage man attempted to solve the real problems of an illusory ghost environment by magic. (88:4.1)

Mankind moved from magic to science not through meditation and reason but through long experience. As the author puts it, man backed into the truth and only faced forward once the scientific method arrived. The fear and curiosity in superstition were the first expressions of the desire to understand and control the planetary environment. (88:4.5, 88:4.6)

The fascination of early superstition was the mother of the later scientific curiosity. (88:4.6)

Because natural death was not understood, magic held the savage in a powerful grip. Ten innocent people might be executed for a single natural death, and fear itself could kill, which only confirmed the false belief. Hair and nails, spittle and food scraps were hidden or buried for fear an enemy would use them for sorcery, so even habits that look like hygiene actually came from fear. (88:4.7, 88:4.8, 88:5.1)

Charms were made from all sorts of things, from tiger claws and crocodile teeth to snake venom, bones of the dead, and dust from footprints. People believed that mistreating or honoring an image would affect the real person it represented. Notice that the customs of new or higher civilizations were suspected of being evil magic and shunned; writing, printing, and pictures were long treated this way. The fear that labels the unfamiliar as magic is an old habit that slows progress. (88:5.2, 88:5.3, 88:5.4)

In case of failure there was always some plausible explanation; the cure for defective magic was more magic. (88:4.8)

Names too were objects of magic. A name was regarded as a precious entity, like the soul or the shadow, and could even be pawned; the real name was not told to strangers, and so nicknames arose. The author sees a trace of this in people today signing promissory notes and striving for titles and degrees. (88:5.5)

VI. The Practice of Magic — From Incantation to Prayer, From Magic to Science

There was public magic and private magic. The magic of medicine men, shamans, and priests was thought to serve the welfare of the whole tribe, while the magic of witches and sorcerers was private and selfish, aimed at harming enemies. Here the dualism of good and bad spirits led to the belief in white and black magic. (88:6.2)

In magic, “medicine” meant not healing but mystery. The savage did not treat himself and never used remedies without the advice of a magic expert. The paper also records that among primitive magicians there were more women than men. (88:6.1)

Some chants and incantations eventually evolved into prayers. Prayers were also acted out; magical dances were dramatic prayers. Gestures, being older than words, were considered more sacred, and imitation, as in the buffalo dance, was believed to have great power. (88:6.3, 88:6.4)

Prayer gradually displaced magic as the associate of sacrifice. (88:6.3)

Yet a world full of charms crushed individual ambition and initiative. A man whose harvest exceeded his neighbor’s was suspected of luring grain from the other’s field, and knowing too much risked being branded a black magician. The author warns that if education fails, people will promptly return to belief in magic, and he points to the linguistic fossils that remain in our speech as evidence. (88:6.6, 88:6.7)

But if modern methods of education should fail, there would be an almost immediate reversion to the primitive beliefs in magic. (88:6.7)
Ancient magic was the cocoon of modern science, indispensable in its time but now no longer useful. (88:6.8)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 88 does not mock fetishes and magic so much as show how they have changed shape and survived in today’s relics and veneration of scripture, flags and public opinion, and belief in luck and astrology. The author observes that Urantia is in the twilight zone of intellectual evolution: half the world reaches for the light of truth and science, while the other half lies in the arms of thinly disguised magic. The reader is led to ask which half is larger within himself or herself. (88:6.8)

Nor does the paper simply praise science. Magic was a branch of the tree of evolutionary religion that bore the fruit of a scientific age, and prayer grew from the same root and displaced magic. Laying aside fear and facing reality honestly is the road that science and true religion travel together. (88:6.3, 88:6.5)
