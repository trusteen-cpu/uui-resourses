Introduction — Man in Debt to the Spirits

Paper 89 traces how primitive religion developed its ideas of sin and sacrifice. According to its author, a Brilliant Evening Star of Nebadon, primitive man regarded himself as being in debt to the spirits and in need of redemption. Over time this notion grew into a doctrine of sin and salvation: the soul was thought to come into the world under forfeit—original sin—and so had to be ransomed. (89:0.1)

The savage believed that the spirits took satisfaction in the sight of human misery and humiliation. At first he worried only about sins he had committed; later he became anxious about the things he had failed to do as well, and the whole sacrificial system grew up around these two ideas. The rituals of avoidance and exorcism, of coercion and propitiation, all blended into one another. (89:0.2)

only advanced civilization recognizes a consistently even-tempered and benevolent God. (89:0.2)

I. The Taboo — The First Social Regulation

A taboo was the avoidance of some act in order to dodge ill luck and keep from offending the spirit ghosts. Taboos were not religious at first, but once they gained the sanction of ghosts and spirits, they became lawmakers and institution builders. The respect a taboo commanded exactly equaled the savage’s fear of the powers believed to enforce it; a man who broke one sometimes died of sheer fright. (89:1.1, 89:1.2)

The taboo is the source of ceremonial standards and the ancestor of primitive self-control. (89:1.1)

The later commandments were truly emancipating, because they replaced thousands of older taboos, and they definitely promised something in return for obedience. Taboos were highly effective in organizing society, yet they were also a terrible burden, preserving obsolete and useless bans alongside useful ones. Even so, the author judges that these achievements of self-control were the real rungs on which mankind climbed the ladder of civilization. (89:1.4, 89:1.6, 89:1.7)

II. The Concept of Sin — Breaking the Taboo

Every primitive tribe had its tree of forbidden fruit—literally the apple, but figuratively a tree whose thousand branches hung heavy with all sorts of taboos—and that tree always said, “Thou shalt not.” Once the savage mind could imagine both good and bad spirits, and once taboo received the sanction of evolving religion, the stage was set for the idea of sin. That idea was universal long before revealed religion appeared. (89:2.1, 89:2.2)

Sin was the transgression of taboo, and death was the penalty of sin. (89:2.2)

Sin in this sense was an act, not a thought; a matter of ritual, not of reason. The traditions of Dilmun and Eden reinforced the belief that man began in perfection and fell through breaking taboos. Habitual violation became a vice; primitive law made vice a crime, and religion made it a sin. The remark that hells had to be invented to explain the prosperity of the wicked shows what ideas people devised in their longing for justice. (89:2.3, 89:2.4)

Confession and forgiveness appeared early too, but confession was merely a rite of remission—men would even ask forgiveness for sins they intended to commit the following week—and a public announcement of defilement. Many apparently hygienic customs were in fact largely ceremonial. (89:2.5)

III. Renunciation and Humiliation — From Self-Denial to Self-Control

Renunciation was the next step. Fasting and the giving up of pleasures became customary; property came to be seen as a spiritual handicap, and tens of thousands of earnest souls began to court poverty. Flogging and the mortification of the flesh were regarded as ways of earning extra credit on the gods’ ledgers. Yet the author holds that all this taught the savage self-control. (89:3.1, 89:3.2, 89:3.3, 89:3.5)

Self-denial and self-control were two of the greatest social gains of early evolutionary religion. Man learned to enlarge life’s fraction by lowering the denominator of personal demands rather than always trying to raise the numerator of selfish gratification. The continence cult, on the other hand, tolerated marriage only as a lesser evil than fornication. The author explains that the Apostle Paul’s personal views on celibacy reflected this cult, and that Paul himself knew such teachings were not part of Jesus’ gospel, saying he spoke “by permission and not by commandment.” (89:3.3, 89:3.6)

Self-control is a better human policy of behavior regulation than is extreme self-denial. (89:3.7)

The counsel that someday man should learn to enjoy liberty without license, nourishment without gluttony, and pleasure without debauchery offers today’s reader a standard of balance. So does the warning that the continence cult leads to a war against marriage and the home, society’s veritable foundation. (89:3.6, 89:3.7)

IV. Origins of Sacrifice — Gift, Debt, and Substitution

The tendency to bow before power is foreshadowed in the fawning of a dog before its master, and from the impulse of worship to the act of sacrifice was but one step. Primitive man measured the value of a sacrifice by the pain it cost him, so the first sacrifices were such acts as plucking hair, cutting the flesh, and cutting off fingers. (89:4.1)

Sacrifice came to be thought of as a message bearer to the gods, and incense and other aesthetic features were added; rites of conciliation and propitiation replaced the older methods of avoidance and exorcism. The doctrine of original sin started every person out in serious debt to the spirit powers. If renunciation was the negative form of propitiation, sacrifice became its positive form. (89:4.3, 89:4.5, 89:4.6, 89:4.7)

Present-day forms of worship are simply the ritualization of these ancient sacrificial techniques of positive propitiation. (89:4.7)

The staggering list of offerings claimed by a certain Egyptian ruler suggests how heavily such sacrifices must have taxed his toiling subjects. In the end, sheer necessity drove people to eat the material part of their sacrifices, the gods having enjoyed the soul of it, and the custom was justified as a sacred meal. (89:4.9, 89:4.10)

V–VI. Sacrifices and Cannibalism; Evolution of Human Sacrifice — An Honest Record of a Dark Past

The author says that modern ideas of early cannibalism are entirely wrong. It arose from necessity and persisted through superstition and ignorance; it was a social, economic, religious, and military custom of early society. Because ghost spirits were thought to be merely modified men, whose greatest need must be food, human flesh was offered to them. The Andonites, Nodites, and Adamites were originally not cannibals. (89:5.1, 89:5.2, 89:5.3)

Human sacrifice was an indirect result of cannibalism as well as its cure. (89:6.1)

Human sacrifice was virtually universal; no race has been entirely free of it. The Chaldeans were among the first to substitute animals, and a tenderhearted Japanese emperor introduced clay images in place of human victims. The author calls the Hebrew story of Jephthah, who sacrificed his only daughter because of a foolish vow, the most tragic record of the clash between time-honored custom and advancing civilization. He adds that the story of Abraham and Isaac was not a strange idea to the people of that day. (89:6.2, 89:6.3, 89:6.8)

The “foundation sacrifice,” in which a person was slain when an important new building was begun, survives in the modern custom of placing keepsakes in a cornerstone. The contrast between “life for life,” once spoken at sacrifice, and “dust to dust,” now spoken at death, shows how far ritual has traveled. (89:6.4, 89:6.6, 89:6.7)

VII–VIII. Modifications of Human Sacrifice; Redemption and Covenants — From Bargain to Trust

Moses tried to end human sacrifice by setting up a system of ransom, under which lands, properties, and children could be redeemed for fixed fees. Groups that stopped sacrificing their first-born soon held great advantages over neighbors who lost sons and saw even their succession of leadership broken. Human sacrifice changed form many times: blood smeared on doorposts, infants set adrift on the water, exile and colonization, temple prostitution, vows of virginity, and the offering of parts of the body. (89:7.1, 89:7.2, 89:7.3, 89:8.1, 89:8.2)

In time sacrifice became linked with the idea of the covenant. Man could not imagine a dependable God until he himself had become relatively dependable and moral, so the thought of making a covenant with Deity was evidence of growing moral dignity. Still, the author warns us not to mistake these early sacrifices for spontaneous gifts of gratitude; they were not expressions of true worship. (89:8.4, 89:8.5, 89:8.6)

Law, a covenant, takes the place of luck, fear, and superstition. (89:8.4)

Early prayer, too, was bargaining with the spirits and argument with the gods. As commerce developed, the spirit of trade entered worship, and just as some men were better traders, some were thought better at praying. The observation that prayers are still read from books, recited by rote, and hung on wheels and trees for the wind to say invites us to ask how much our own praying has really changed. (89:8.7, 89:8.8)

Early prayer was hardly worship (89:8.8)

IX–X. Sacrifices and Sacraments; Forgiveness of Sin — Loyalty Restored

Human sacrifice advanced from the bloody business of man-eating to higher and more symbolic levels, and the early rituals of sacrifice bred the later sacraments. A sacrament of cakes and wine took the place of the flesh and blood of human sacrifice, and from the Hebrew Passover ceremonial came the later Christian sacrament. Paul may have burdened Christianity with teachings about blood and sacrifice, but by presenting Christ as the last and all-sufficient sacrifice, he made an end once and for all of the doctrine of redemption through sacrifice. (89:9.1, 89:9.2, 89:9.3)

His theologic compromises indicate that even revelation must submit to the graduated control of evolution. (89:9.3)

The last section speaks to the modern reader. The consciousness of sin persists, but the old thought patterns of salvation from it have become outworn, and modern man must develop new ways of achieving the consciousness of salvation. The author begins by defining sin anew. (89:10.1)

Sin must be redefined as deliberate disloyalty to Deity. (89:10.2)

The feeling of guilt is the awareness of having violated the mores; it is not necessarily sin. Indeed, the capacity to recognize guilt is not a mark of human meanness but a badge of a creature of potential greatness, the first stimulus toward the conquests of faith. Confession is a manful repudiation of disloyalty; it does not erase the consequences, but it is essential to spiritual growth. (89:10.3, 89:10.4, 89:10.5)

The forgiveness does not have to be sought, only received as the consciousness of re-establishment of loyalty relations between the creature and the Creator. (89:10.6)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 89 does not hide humanity’s dark past of taboo, cannibalism, and human sacrifice. But its purpose is not condemnation. It shows how a relationship that began in debt and fear grew, through covenant and symbol, into a relationship of loyalty and trust. Its final section, which sees sin as disloyalty and forgiveness as restored loyalty, invites us to rebuild our relationship with God as one of love rather than bargaining.
