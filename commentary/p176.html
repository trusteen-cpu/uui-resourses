Introduction — A Question Asked While Looking at the Temple

On Tuesday afternoon, as they left the temple, Matthew pointed to its massive stones and beautiful adornment and asked whether such buildings could really be destroyed. Jesus answered that not one stone would be left upon another. The apostles could conceive of no event short of the end of the world that could bring about the temple’s destruction. This paper shows how these two ideas—the fate of Jerusalem and the end of the world—became tangled in the apostles’ minds. (176:0.1)

To avoid the crowds, the group climbed a little way up the western slope of Olivet and looked down on the temple glowing in the setting sun. When they had sat down under the full moon, Nathaniel asked how they would know when these things were about to happen. With that question the long teaching of the evening began. (176:0.2)

The paper has four sections: the destruction of Jerusalem, the Master’s second coming, the later discussion at the camp with the parable of the talents, and the narrator’s own account of the return of Michael. As we read, it is important to hold two questions apart. One concerns a historical catastrophe soon to fall on a single city; the other concerns a visit, at a time no one knows, by the sovereign of a universe. The apostles’ confusion came from merging the two. (176:2.8)

I. The Destruction of Jerusalem — When to Flee

Jesus first warned them not to be deceived. Many would come claiming to be deliverers, and wars, famines, and earthquakes would not themselves mean that the end had come. The apostles would be thrown out of the synagogue, imprisoned, even betrayed by their own kinsfolk, but when they stood before judges, the spirit would teach them what to say. (176:1.1)

but even in these persecutions I will not forsake you; my spirit will not desert you. (176:1.1)

The narrator names the cause of Jerusalem’s fall plainly: the rejection of the spiritual concept of the Messiah and the blind determination to cling to a material deliverer would bring the Jews into direct conflict with the Roman armies. Because Jerusalem was to be the cradle of the early gospel movement, Jesus did not want its teachers and preachers to perish in the coming revolts, and so he gave specific instructions. (176:1.2, 176:1.3)

Because of this warning, practically all the believers fled to Pella in the north when the Roman troops first appeared. Yet many followers took the prediction to refer to the New Jerusalem and the end of the world. Jesus lamented that they always erred because they tried to attach the new teaching to the old. (176:1.5, 176:1.6, 176:1.7)

The scene shows the practical value of a warning. The prophecy was meant not to frighten but to help people avoid harm that could be avoided. At the same time, settled beliefs will bend even the plainest words to fit their own frame. Readers may ask whether they, too, are fitting the teachings they hear into conclusions they already wanted. (176:1.7)

II. The Master’s Second Coming — An Evening of Two Tangled Subjects

Jesus had more than once implied that he would leave and yet return, and the apostles promptly tied that promise to the destruction of Jerusalem. He reminded them again that his kingdom was not of this world, and that what was now ending would be a new beginning, out of which the gospel would go to all the world. Meanwhile his Spirit of Truth would comfort and guide them. (176:2.1, 176:2.2, 176:2.3)

You behold me now in weakness and in the flesh, but when I return, it shall be with power and in the spirit. (176:2.4)

The time of his return, he said, is known only in the councils of Paradise. But as the fig tree’s tender leaves tell us that summer is near, so after the long winter of material-mindedness they should discern the spiritual springtime of a new dispensation. Then he connected the teaching with each person’s death: what the whole world faces at the end of an age, each individual faces as a personal experience at the end of life. (176:2.5, 176:2.6, 176:2.7)

The narrator says that no discourse ever became so confused in the apostles’ minds as this one. The later written accounts did not agree, and a Jewish apocalyptic written by one Selta was even copied into the Gospel records; the parable of the ten virgins first appeared in those writings. The Apostle John, however, never became thus confused. (176:2.8)

Jesus also reminded them that the Father had already sent several bestowals to this dark world: the one who became the prince of darkness, then Adam, then Melchizedek, and in these days the Son of Man. The promise of an enlarged revelation of truth when the kingdom comes to full fruition offers a view in which revelation is not a single event but continues age after age. Seen this way, his return is less a dreadful end than one more scene in the Father’s ongoing mercy. (176:2.3, 176:2.5)

The thirteen walked toward the camp in silence. On this very road Judas finally confirmed his decision to abandon his associates. Though it was late, the apostles did not want to sleep; they wanted to hear more about Jerusalem, the Master’s departure, and the end of the world. (176:2.9)

III. Later Discussion at the Camp — The Parable of the Talents

Late that night, with some twenty gathered around the campfire, Thomas asked what their attitude should be while the Master was away on the Father’s business. Jesus answered that their connection with the kingdom was wholly spiritual and personal. The fall of nations, the end of an age, even the end of the world—none of these could seriously concern one who had hidden his life in the eternal kingdom. (176:3.1, 176:3.2)

Kingdom builders, the accredited citizens of the heavenly worlds, are not to be disturbed by temporal upheavals or perturbed by terrestrial cataclysms. (176:3.2)

But Jesus quickly added a warning. Survival faith is a living faith that bears ever more fruit of the spirit. Having once accepted sonship will not save one who knowingly and persistently refuses to bear that fruit. Then he told the parable of the master who, before a long journey, entrusted his goods to his servants. (176:3.3, 176:3.4)

this survival faith is a living faith, and it increasingly manifests the fruits of that divine spirit which first inspired it in the human heart. (176:3.3)

Jesus compared waiting for his return to the way we face death. Just as each believer carries on his lifework in view of an inevitable and ever-impending natural death, so each generation should carry on its work in view of the Son of Man’s possible return. The comparison frees belief about the end from fear and excitement and turns it into a calm, steady, everyday responsibility. (176:3.3)

The narrator extends the parable to the stewardship of truth. Truth is living, so to boast of having preserved what was entrusted in fixed form, changing nothing, is merely an excuse for spiritual indolence. And the one-talent servant who blamed his failure on his master is like every selfish mortal who puts the blame for his own failures on others. (176:3.7, 176:3.9)

It matters that this parable is an answer to the question about the second coming. The servants could not know when their lord would return. What was asked of them was not to predict the date but to make gains with what they had been given. In the next world we will give account of our endowments and stewardships, and whether our talents are few or many, the reckoning will be just and merciful. The right way to await his return, then, is faithful service day by day. (176:3.6, 176:3.8)

Truth is living; the Spirit of Truth is ever leading the children of light into new realms of spiritual reality and divine service. (176:3.7)
Freely have you received; therefore freely should you give of the truth of heaven (176:3.10)

IV. The Return of Michael — What We Know and What We Do Not

No part of the Master’s teaching has been so misunderstood as his promise to return. His followers first connected it with the fall of Jerusalem, and when the temple fell and still he did not appear, they connected it with the end of the age and even the end of the world. The narrator sets out the two things Jesus promised to do after his ascension. (176:4.1, 176:4.2, 176:4.3)

Source 176:4.3
PromiseWhat the narrator makes clear
The first promiseTo send another teacher, the Spirit of Truth — fulfilled on the day of Pentecost
The second promiseTo return personally to this world someday — without saying how, where, or when
What many of the narrators believeThat he will most probably return many times in the ages to come
What is certainWhen he returns, he will come as the supreme ruler of a universe, not as an obscure babe
You would do well, therefore, to disassociate the Master’s personal return to earth from any and all set events or settled epochs. (176:4.6)

The narrator says that the second advent has great sentimental value, but for human beings it is of no more practical importance than natural death, which brings us directly into the presence of the sovereign of our universe. Whether we go to him or he comes to us first is of no serious concern. (176:4.7)

In this section the narrator honestly separates what is known from what is not. Urantia will forever be one of the seven nativity spheres of Michael, and among his titles he carries that of Planetary Prince of Urantia, so it is natural that he should wish to return. Yet whether that will be with the judgment of this age, at the close of some later age, or as an unannounced, isolated event, the narrator says plainly that he does not know. This willingness to say “we do not know” is the soundest antidote to the endless speculation surrounding the second coming. (176:4.1, 176:4.4, 176:4.5)

Be you therefore ever ready to welcome him on earth as he stands ready to welcome you in heaven. (176:4.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

To those who ask for a timetable of the end, Paper 176 offers an attitude instead. About the destruction of Jerusalem Jesus gave a specific way of escape, but about the time of his return he said that no one knows. What he asked instead was that we increase what has been entrusted to us and live ready to welcome him at any time. From this paper readers can learn a faith that stands firm even amid unsettling news of the times. (176:3.6)
