Introduction — The Heaviest of Adolescences

Paper 127 covers Jesus’ sixteenth through twentieth years. Within a few years of his father’s death all the family property was gone, and the eldest son became the head and sole support of a large family. At the same time he grew more and more conscious of his pre-existence and of his purpose: to reveal his Paradise Father to the children of men. (127:0.1)

No youth of Urantia will ever be called upon to pass through more testing conflicts or more trying situations than Jesus himself endured during those strenuous years from fifteen to twenty. (127:0.2)

The narrator states the meaning of these years at the outset. Having actually lived through adolescence on a world beset by evil and distraught by sin, the Son of Man became the understanding refuge for the distressed and perplexed adolescents of all the worlds of Nebadon. This paper, then, is both the story of a great life’s growing years and good news for struggling young people today: there is One who knows them. (127:0.3, 127:0.4)

I. The Sixteenth Year (A.D. 10) — Full Growth of Body and Mind

This year Jesus attained his full physical growth and the fullness of capacity for intellectual development. The text describes him carefully, noting that even in the most commonplace contacts there appeared the touch of a twofold nature, the human and the divine. (127:1.2, 127:1.3)

Yet his family understood him less and less. Mary had given them to understand that he was destined to be the deliverer of the Jewish people, so the more frankly Jesus denied such ideas, the more confused they became. Knowing that his mission was so unusual that no one on earth could advise him about it, he spoke of it less and less. (127:1.4, 127:1.7)

Even so, because the synagogue school would not receive girls, he conducted a home school so that his sisters could learn just as the boys did. He spent the year almost confined to the workbench, yet he was a real, though youthful, father to the family. (127:1.5, 127:1.6, 127:1.8)

Mary grieved to see her son toiling at the carpenter’s bench instead of studying with the rabbis in Jerusalem. But though she could not understand him fully, she loved him, and she appreciated most of all the willing way he shouldered the responsibility of the home. Love and responsibility can walk together even where understanding is lacking. (127:1.8)

II. The Seventeenth Year (A.D. 11) — The Zealots’ Call, and “Money Cannot Love”

Amid agitation against paying taxes to Rome, a nationalist party arose that would soon be called the Zealots. When its organizers came to Nazareth to see Jesus, he listened carefully and asked many questions but refused to join. His mother, his uncle, and even his brother James urged him to enlist, and Isaac, a wealthy moneylender, offered to support his family if he would lead the Nazareth patriots. (127:2.1, 127:2.2, 127:2.5, 127:2.7)

The narrator explains why this was so delicate. Patriotic issues tangled with tax-gathering foreign oppressors are always difficult for spiritual leaders, and doubly so here because the Jewish religion itself was involved in the agitation against Rome. Mary’s brother Simon had already joined and later became an officer, and for several years Jesus and his uncle were estranged. (127:2.4, 127:2.6)

For the first time Jesus had to resort consciously to public strategy, because he could not reveal that he was more than a man or disclose his future mission. With counsel from his old teacher, the chazan, he stated his position bravely and diplomatically, holding to his first duty to his family. (127:2.7, 127:2.8)

making his never-to-be-forgotten statement that “money cannot love.” (127:2.8)

An unscheduled speech by James disarmed the remaining opposition, and the crisis passed. But Nazareth remained divided about the Son of Man, and this was one of the chief reasons he later moved to Capernaum. That same year Jesus made great progress in bringing his divine and human natures together, accomplishing it by the force of his own decisions with only the aid of his indwelling Monitor. (127:2.9, 127:2.10, 127:2.12)

So far, nothing supernatural had happened in this young man’s career except the visit of a messenger, dispatched by his elder brother Immanuel (127:2.12)

III. The Eighteenth Year (A.D. 12) — Passover with James, and the Death of Amos

All the family property except the home and garden was disposed of. With the pressure eased for a time, Jesus took James to the Passover. On the brow of Olivet, while James exclaimed in wonder at the temple, Jesus gazed on Jerusalem in silence, and in the temple discussions he asked no questions. To his disappointed brother he only replied, “My hour has not yet come.” (127:3.1, 127:3.5)

In September Elizabeth and John came to visit. After many intimate talks, the two young men agreed not to see each other again until they met in public service when the heavenly Father should call them. In December little Amos died of a high fever, and the cost of a funeral on top of everything else staggered the family. In this sorrow Mary at last recognized Jesus as the real head of the family in the fullest sense. (127:3.11, 127:3.12, 127:3.13)

Mother-Mary, sorrow will not help us (127:3.14)

The narrator says that his sturdy, practical optimism was contagious and helped form strong and noble characters in the children despite the depressiveness of poverty. With his power to concentrate on one problem and his untiring patience, he lived as if “seeing Him who is invisible.” This scene shows that hope is not something that comes after circumstances improve; it is the strength to endure them. (127:3.14, 127:3.15)

Back in Nazareth he worked in the old repair shop he was buying back and was greatly cheered to meet people from all over the country. The text says he truly loved people—just common folks. In the synagogue he arranged the order of readings so that one passage would illuminate another, and whenever the weather permitted he took his brothers and sisters on Sabbath-afternoon nature strolls. By joining the chazan’s young men’s club for philosophic discussion, he regained some of the standing he had lost in the nationalist controversy. (127:3.7, 127:3.8, 127:3.9)

IV. The Nineteenth Year (A.D. 13) — The Father-Brother’s Home Training

This year Jesus fully won his mother over to training the children by the positive injunction to do good rather than by the older method of forbidding evil. At home and throughout his public career he always exhorted in the form “You shall do this,” and prayer time became the occasion for discussing everything that concerned the family’s welfare. (127:4.2)

He refrained from placing emphasis on evil by forbidding it, while he exalted the good by commanding its performance. (127:4.2)

The text sketches the character of each of the seven children, showing how differently they grew up under the same father-brother. Jesus never disciplined them arbitrarily, and the children, especially the girls, confided their troubles to him as they would to an affectionate father. (127:4.4, 127:4.6, 127:4.7)

Jesus gradually liberalized family practices such as Sabbath observance, and Mary gave hearty assent. When Jude started school this year, Jesus sold his harp, his last recreational pleasure, to pay the expenses—comforting himself that at least the harp was now safe from seizure by the tax collector. (127:4.9, 127:4.10)

V. Rebecca, the Daughter of Ezra — A Declaration of Love Declined

Rebecca, the eldest daughter of Ezra, a wealthy merchant of Nazareth, fell in love with Jesus. When Mary and Miriam tried to dissuade her by telling of their belief that he was a son of destiny, her resolve only grew, and her father supported her, offering to compensate the family for the loss of Jesus’ earnings. (127:5.1, 127:5.2)

Jesus replied that no amount of money could take the place of his personal obligation to rear his father’s family. Through this he faced for the first time the question of marriage, which every average human being must confront. He sincerely thanked Rebecca for her admiration but explained that he was not free to enter into any relationship beyond brotherly regard and pure friendship. (127:5.3, 127:5.4, 127:5.5)

“We can’t have him for a son; he is too noble for us.” (127:5.3)
“If I am a son of destiny, I must not assume obligations of lifelong duration until such a time as my destiny shall be made manifest.” (127:5.5)

Rebecca moved to Sepphoris, but she waited for him all her life, followed him devotedly during his public career, and stood among the women by the cross. His refusal did not end her love; it turned it into a greater devotion. (127:5.6)

VI. His Twentieth Year (A.D. 14) — A Passover Without a Lamb, at the Threshold of Manhood

At Passover this year in Bethany, Jesus celebrated the feast with Lazarus, Martha, and Mary without the paschal lamb. He taught that the Father in heaven was not truly concerned with such meaningless rituals and served them unleavened bread and wine as “the bread of life” and “the water of life.” At home, however, he continued to keep the Passover with his family according to the law of Moses. (127:6.6, 127:6.7)

Let us be free in the knowledge of the truth of our Father’s eternal love. (127:6.6)

When Mary asked him about marriage, he answered that all such things must await “my hour,” and he had already settled in his mind that he would not become the father of children in the flesh. The paper then lists in turn what the twenty-year-old Jesus was learning. (127:6.8, 127:6.12)

He is becoming experienced in the skillful wresting of victory from the very jaws of defeat; he is learning how to transform the difficulties of time into the triumphs of eternity. (127:6.12)

As a child he accumulated knowledge; as a youth he sorted and correlated it; and now as a man he begins to organize these mental possessions for his future teaching and service. Full of sympathy for the frailties of human nature, he prepares for his mission of revealing God to men and leading men to God. (127:6.14, 127:6.15, 127:6.16)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

The Jesus of Paper 127 is not a wonder-working youth but a young man who grows by choosing day after day amid poverty, misunderstanding, and temptation. All that human experience became the eternal possession of the Universe Sovereign, and so he became our understanding brother and sympathetic friend. (127:6.13)
