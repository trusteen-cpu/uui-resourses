Introduction — From the Upper Room to the Camp

About ten o’clock on Thursday night, Jesus led the eleven from the home of Elijah and Mary Mark back toward the Gethsemane camp. John Mark, who had been resting downstairs, heard them coming down, threw a linen coat about himself, and followed. From this time until the crucifixion he stayed close enough to witness everything and to overhear much of what the Master said. (182:0.1)

On the way back, the apostles for the first time suspected that something was wrong with Judas. Finding him absent from the camp, they besieged Andrew, who could only say that he did not know where Judas was but feared he had deserted them. The paper tells this tense night in three scenes. (182:0.2)

I. The Last Group Prayer — That They May Be One

Jesus led the apostles a short way up Olivet to a large flat rock in full view of Jerusalem and had them kneel in a circle about him, as they had done on the day of their ordination. Then, standing in the moonlight, he lifted his eyes to heaven and prayed. The prayer has three parts: for himself, for the eleven, and for all who would come to believe through their word. (182:1.1, 182:1.2)

And this is eternal life, that my creatures should know you as the only true God and Father of all, and that they should believe in him whom you sent into the world. (182:1.3)

In the first part of the prayer, Jesus acknowledged that he had exalted the Father on earth and accomplished the work given him, and that only the laying down of his life in the flesh remained. He told the Father that he had manifested him to the men chosen out of the world, and that they were learning that all he had came from the Father. As he had represented the Father in this world, so they would now represent him. When the prayer ended, the eleven remained kneeling for several minutes and then went back to the camp in silence. (182:1.3, 182:1.4, 182:1.7)

The prayer for the apostles is full of trust. Jesus said they were weak and frail, but that they could be trusted, and that he had kept them all save one, the son of revenge, who would not have further fellowship. He knew there was no need to ask the Father to watch over them, yet he asked, so that the apostles might better realize that the Father loves them as the Son does. (182:1.4, 182:1.5)

I do not ask that you take them out of the world, only that you keep them from the evil in the world. (182:1.5)

The narrator stresses that Jesus prayed for unity but did not desire uniformity. Sin creates a dead level of evil inertia, but righteousness nourishes the creative spirit of individual experience in living truth. In the believer’s spiritual fellowship with the Father, then, there can be no doctrinal finality or sectarian superiority. This reminds communities of faith not to mistake oneness for thinking the same thoughts in the same forms. (182:1.8)

In the spiritual fellowship of the believer-son with the divine Father there can never be doctrinal finality and sectarian superiority of group consciousness. (182:1.8)

In the course of the prayer Jesus said that he had manifested the Father’s name to the world. The text contrasts this with the revelation in the time of Moses, when the Father could proceed no further than “I AM.” But by the end of Jesus’ earth life, that name had been so fully revealed that he could speak of himself in sixteen “I am” sayings. (182:1.9)

II. The Last Hour Before the Betrayal — Each in His Place

David Zebedee and John Mark told Jesus they had kept Judas under observation for several days and knew what he intended. Jesus only replied that nothing could happen to the Son of Man unless the Father so willed. His cheerfulness was waning, and he grew more serious, even sorrowful. He sent the apostles to their tents, asking only Peter, James, and John to stay with him. (182:2.1, 182:2.2)

Let not your hearts be troubled; all things will work together for the glory of God and the salvation of men. (182:2.1)

Before sleeping, Simon Zelotes handed out the swords stored in his tent. Only Nathaniel refused to arm himself, saying that the Master’s kingdom was not of this world and his disciples should not fight with the sword. He would pray, he said, but he would not wield the sword. Hearing this, Andrew handed his sword back, and so nine of them were armed as they parted for the night. (182:2.3)

I will pray, but I will not wield the sword. (182:2.3)

For a while, resentment that Judas was a traitor eclipsed everything else in the apostles’ minds; the Master’s reference to Judas in the last prayer had opened their eyes. Ordinarily they would have bidden him a personal good night, but that evening, overcome by the shock and by the weight of the farewell prayer, they went away in silence. Still, they expected any arrest to come early in the morning, since no secular work was done after noon on the preparation day for the Passover. Only David and John Mark knew Judas was coming that very night. (182:2.4, 182:2.7, 182:2.9)

To the end, Jesus remembered those far away. He sent Jacob, David’s fleetest messenger, to Abner at Philadelphia with word that he would be put to death but would rise and appear to him before going to the Father. He forewarned the chief of the Greeks encamped with them in the same way and told him to strengthen his brethren. And he asked Andrew to keep the brethren together until he came again. (182:2.5, 182:2.6, 182:2.8)

David took the watch on the upper trail toward the Bethany road, and John Mark on the road by the Kidron. In saying farewell, David told Jesus that though he was not an apostle, he had delighted to do the lesser things as they should be done. Jesus answered that others had done what they were directed to do, but David had served from his own heart. David also told him that Jesus’ family was in Jericho that night and would arrive early the next morning. (182:2.10, 182:2.11)

David, my son, others have done that which they were directed to do, but this service have you done of your own heart (182:2.10)

III. Alone in Gethsemane — Not My Will, but Yours

When the camp was quiet, Jesus took the three apostles up a nearby ravine where he often prayed. They had never seen their Master so heavy-laden. He told them to watch with him and went about a stone’s throw away, fell on his face, and prayed three times. Each time he returned, he found the three asleep. John Mark, who had left his post to follow, saw and heard it all from the bushes. (182:2.12, 182:3.1, 182:3.2, 182:3.3)

The narrator reads this scene as new evidence of Jesus’ humanity. Before the greatest revelation of his divinity, the resurrection, must come the greatest proofs of his mortal nature, his humiliation and crucifixion. Each time he prayed, his humanity laid a firmer faith-hold on his divinity, and his human will became more completely one with the Father’s will. The angel brought word that the Father desired his Son to finish his bestowal by passing through the experience of death, as all mortal creatures must. (182:3.5, 182:3.6)

The text does not hide his anguish. He was weary from long labor and anxiety for his apostles, and the perspiration rolled off his face in great drops. Knowing how weak they were, he dreaded to leave them, and his human heart searched for some legitimate avenue of escape. When that search failed, he was willing to drink the cup. He was fully determined to use none of his sovereign power as head of a universe to save himself, and the armies of heaven had been warned not to intervene unless he ordered it. (182:3.7, 182:3.8, 182:3.9)

His suffering came less from death itself than from baffled love. He was isolated from his family in the flesh, one of his chosen associates was betraying him, and his father Joseph’s people had rejected him and so sealed their doom as a people. The divine mind of Michael knew he had done his best for the twelve, but the human heart of Jesus wished more might have been done for them before they were left alone. Any reader who knows what it is to leave loved ones behind will feel the Master very near in this passage. (182:3.9)

In this great sorrow his mind went back to his childhood in Nazareth and his early work in Galilee. He soothed himself with memories of Capernaum and Mount Hermon and of sunrise and sunset on the Sea of Galilee. And before Judas and the soldiers arrived, he had fully regained his customary poise. (182:3.10, 182:3.11)

the spirit had triumphed over the flesh; faith had asserted itself over all human tendencies to fear or entertain doubt. (182:3.11)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 182 records the night when the Son of God struggled in the most human way. He prayed first for those he would leave behind, sent word even to those far away, and only then wrestled alone with his own cup. His peace came not from the absence of anguish but from passing through it and wholly committing his will to the Father’s. The reader sees that the same path lies open in the Gethsemanes of his or her own life. (182:3.6, 182:3.11)
