Introduction — Proclaimed with Power, Without Miracles

During the Perean ministry Jesus was usually accompanied by ten apostles, since at least two always stayed at Pella to teach the crowds there. This time Peter and Andrew went back to Pella, and when Jesus arrived at Philadelphia he had more than six hundred followers with him. (167:0.1)

The text stresses again that, apart from the ten lepers, there were no miracles on this mission. The gospel was proclaimed with power without miracles, and for the most part without the personal presence of Jesus or his apostles. Arriving at Philadelphia on February 22, the company rested for two days and heard good news from Alexandria and Damascus. (167:0.2, 167:0.3)

I. Breakfast with the Pharisees — The Lowest Seat and the Guest Who Cannot Repay

A wealthy Pharisee of Philadelphia who had accepted Abner’s teaching gave a Sabbath breakfast in Jesus’ honor, to which some forty leading citizens and lawyers were invited. A Pharisee from Jerusalem, a former member of the Sanhedrin, made for the seat of honor as was his habit, was moved four places down, and was deeply offended. (167:1.1, 167:1.2)

Near the end of the meal a believer suffering from severe dropsy came in. He did not ask to be healed, but he hoped to gain the Master’s compassion here, away from the crowds. Jesus smiled kindly at him and asked the guests whether it was lawful to heal the sick on the Sabbath. No one answered. (167:1.4)

You have not asked to be healed, but I know the desire of your heart and the faith of your soul. (167:1.5)

Jesus said that the Father does such works not to tempt people into the kingdom but to reveal himself to those already in it. Then, apparently with the scramble for the seat of honor in mind, he told them not to seek the chief seats at a feast, and urged them to invite those who cannot repay. (167:1.5)

II. Parable of the Great Supper — A Meaning Each Must Find in His Own Soul

To break the awkward silence, a lawyer offered the common saying that blessed is he who shall eat bread in the kingdom of God, and Jesus answered with the parable of the great supper. Those invited excused themselves on account of a field, oxen, and a marriage, so the master sent for the poor and outcast from the streets and lanes, and even for people from the highways and hedges, until his house was full. (167:2.1, 167:2.2)

At least one of the Pharisees who sneered that day grasped the meaning of the parable, was baptized, and publicly confessed his faith. The next day the apostles each tried to interpret the parable, but Jesus only listened with interest and offered no further explanation. (167:2.3, 167:2.4)

Let every man find out the meaning for himself and in his own soul. (167:2.4)

III. The Woman with the Spirit of Infirmity — Loosed from the Bondage of Fear

On that Sabbath Jesus taught in a synagogue for the first time since the synagogues had been closed to him by order of the Sanhedrin. After the service he went over to an elderly woman who for more than eighteen years had been bowed down by fear and had lost all joy in life, and he laid his hand on her shoulder. She believed his words and by faith straightened up at once. (167:3.1)

Woman, if you would only believe, you could be wholly loosed from your spirit of infirmity. (167:3.1)

The text makes clear that this woman’s affliction was entirely mental and that her bent body was the result of her depressed mind. The people thought a physical disease had been cured, and the hostile ruler of the synagogue was indignant at a Sabbath healing. Jesus answered that they would loose an ox on the Sabbath to lead it to water, so should not this daughter of Abraham be loosed from bondage to drink the waters of life? That ruler was later removed from office. (167:3.2, 167:3.3, 167:3.4)

Jesus often freed victims of fear in this way from the spirit of infirmity and depression of mind. People regarded all such afflictions as bodily disease or possession by evil spirits, but the text shows plainly how deeply the state of the mind affects the body. To be released from fear can itself be healing. (167:3.5)

IV. The Message from Bethany — One Last Appeal

Late on the night of February 26, word came from Martha and Mary that the one the Master loved was very sick. After a strange interval in which he seemed to commune with something beyond himself, Jesus said that this sickness was not unto death and would serve to glorify God. (167:4.1)

His first human thought was to go at once to help them. But another idea came into his combined mind: to make this final appeal, giving the Jerusalem leaders one more chance, the most profound outward ministry of his earth life, if the Father would allow it. He had refused any display of political power, but he sought the Father’s consent to show his power over life and death. (167:4.2)

The Jews of the time believed that the soul might linger near the body for three days but departed after the third, and that no one had ever come back from the grave after the fourth day. For this reason Jesus stayed two full days longer at Philadelphia. Then on Wednesday morning he said, “Let us go into Judea.” (167:4.3, 167:4.4)

As long as my day lasts, I fear not to enter Judea. (167:4.5)

The apostles conferred under James’s leadership and were united in opposing the journey. Jesus said that while his day lasted he was not afraid, and that he would go to wake Lazarus from sleep. When they did not understand, he told them plainly that Lazarus was dead. Then Thomas spoke up before his fellows with his resolve. (167:4.4, 167:4.5, 167:4.6)

if that is the Master’s choice, then let us acquit ourselves like men of courage; let us go also that we may die with him. (167:4.7)

The text says that in matters requiring deliberate and sustained courage, Thomas was always the mainstay of the twelve. Here we see another side of a man too easily remembered only as the doubter. (167:4.7)

V. On the Way to Bethany — The Pharisee and the Publican, and Marriage

At noon Jesus taught on “The Conditions of Salvation,” saying that salvation is the free gift of God to all who have faith. God’s favor cannot be bought with self-righteous works. He then told the parable of the Pharisee and the publican who prayed in the temple; it was the publican who went home justified before God. (167:5.1)

The law of the universe is: Ask and you shall receive; seek and you shall find. (167:5.2)

That night in Jericho the Pharisees tried to trap him over divorce. Jesus would not be drawn into a dispute over divorce laws, yet he exalted marriage as the most ideal and highest of all human relationships. He sharply condemned the practice of putting away wives for trivial reasons and pointed out the injustice it did to women and children. He endorsed only teachings that granted women equality with men. (167:5.2, 167:5.3, 167:5.4)

The apostles could not understand why he avoided definite statements on scientific, social, economic, and political questions. It was because his mission was devoted to the revelation of spiritual and religious truth. That night he explained that his carrying out his mission alone was no reflection on the desirability of marriage. (167:5.6, 167:5.7)

in the creation of whom these parents become copartners with the Makers of heaven and earth. (167:5.7)

VI–VII. Blessing the Little Children; The Talk about Angels

The next morning mothers crowded in with their children, and when the apostles rebuked them, Jesus was indignant and told them not to forbid the children to come. He laid his hands on the children and gave the mothers courage and hope. The text notes that the intelligences of Nebadon were watching as these children played with the Creator of a universe. (167:6.1, 167:6.2, 167:6.3)

The text then says that the status of women in Palestine was greatly improved by Jesus’ teaching, and adds that the same would have been true throughout the world had his followers not departed so far from what he taught. His regard for marriage and for children reached well beyond the customs of his time. (167:6.4)

At Jericho Jesus stressed the great value of beauty in awakening the urge to worship in the early religious training of children. A child should first meet worship in nature, and afterward go to places of public worship marked by simplicity and beauty. (167:6.5, 167:6.6)

Beauty is most religious when it is most simple and naturelike. (167:6.6)

On the road up to Bethany, Nathaniel asked what they should teach about angels. Jesus’ answer sums up the nature and ministry of angels concisely. The talk was cut short when Martha came hurrying out to meet him. (167:7.1, 167:7.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 167 records the journey just before the great event of the raising of Lazarus, yet the teachings along the way all point to everyday life: where to sit at a feast, whom to invite, how to pray, how to treat marriage and children. Even with a great work ahead of him, Jesus did not neglect small people and small places. (167:1.5, 167:5.1, 167:6.1)
