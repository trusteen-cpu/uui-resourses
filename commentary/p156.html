Introduction — A Door Opened Where He Went to Rest

On June 10 Jesus and his company reached the environs of Sidon and stayed at the home of Karuska, a well-to-do woman who had once been a patient in the Bethsaida hospital. They spent about six weeks in Phoenicia—in Sidon, the coast cities, and Tyre. Jesus had come there to rest, yet at the very time the doors had closed in Galilee, the work among the gentiles proved remarkably fruitful. (156:0.1, 156:2.3)

The first Sabbath was one of great quiet. The apostles and evangelists meditated on the discourses on religion they had heard along the way, but none of them fully grasped their meaning. This paper shows how that teaching came alive in actual ministry among the gentiles. (156:0.2)

I. The Syrian Woman — The Faith of a Believing Dog

Karuska’s servant told a neighbor, a Syrian woman named Norana, that Jesus was lodging there. Norana came with her daughter, about twelve years old, who suffered from convulsions and a grievous nervous disorder; she believed the child was possessed by an unclean spirit. One apostle after another tried to send her away because the Master was resting, but she would not give up. (156:1.1, 156:1.2)

Jesus, who had heard everything through an open window, came out and told her that her faith was great indeed, and the girl was well from that hour. The scene sets the disciples’ prejudice sharply against the faith of a gentile woman. The next day Jesus said that the Father’s kingdom would be taken by the gentiles if the children of Abraham did not show faith enough to enter it. (156:1.7, 156:1.8)

O woman, great is your faith (156:1.7)

The text adds that what touched the Master’s heart was not only Norana’s persistent faith but also her sense of humor. Her way of turning a cutting remark aside with good-humored wit shows a humble strength that does not lose its purpose even when insulted. (156:2.8)

II. Teaching in Sidon — A Life on the Bridge

On entering Sidon, many in the company crossed a bridge for the first time in their lives, and Jesus used it to sum up the nature of this world. He then stayed at the home of Justa and her mother Bernice, teaching the twenty-four each morning, while they preached in Sidon in the afternoons and evenings. (156:2.1, 156:2.2)

This world is only a bridge; you may pass over it, but you should not think to build a dwelling place upon it. (156:2.1)

These Greek-speaking gentile believers in many ways appreciated the teachings more fully than the Jews. They came to know not only that Jesus was like God but also that God was like Jesus, and they grasped that there is no favoritism with the Universal Father and that the universe is ever law-abiding and dependable. (156:2.4)

All down through the ages men have not been unable to comprehend Jesus; they have been afraid to. (156:2.4)

Jesus made it clear that he had not left Galilee for lack of courage, but because the time had not yet come for an open clash with established religion. His theme at Sidon was spiritual progression. One cannot stand still but must go forward or retrogress; we must not only cease to do evil but learn to do well, and once our sins are confessed, we must refuse to harbor even the feelings of guilt. (156:2.5, 156:2.6, 156:2.7)

My people take themselves too seriously (156:2.8)

Jesus greatly enjoyed the keen humor of these gentiles, and said that the burdensome religion of the Pharisees could never have arisen among a people with a sense of humor. That earnest faith and a light touch toward oneself can go together is a valuable lesson for believers today. (156:2.8)

III–IV. The Journey up the Coast; At Tyre

Using Sidon as their base, the company taught in the coast cities to the north and as far south as Tyre. The apostles and evangelists were greatly surprised by the eagerness of these gentiles and the readiness with which they believed. The text explains that these people were in reality mainly descended from the earlier Canaanite tribes. (156:3.1, 156:3.2)

The polyglot population of Tyre heard them gladly, and many were baptized. Interest ran so high that the doors of the Melkarth temple were opened to Jesus, and in later years a Christian church was built on that very site. Many of the makers of Tyrian purple believed, and when they migrated to the ends of the earth in search of new shellfish beds, they carried the gospel of the kingdom with them. (156:4.1, 156:4.2, 156:4.3)

V. Jesus’ Teaching at Tyre — The White Lily, the Beam, and Temptation

In his address of July 20 Jesus first told the story of the white lily: though its roots are grounded in the dark muck below, it rears its pure head high into the sunshine. (156:5.1)

mortal man, while he has his roots of origin and being in the animal soil of human nature, can by faith raise his spiritual nature up into the sunlight of heavenly truth and actually bear the noble fruits of the spirit. (156:5.1)

In the same sermon he told his first and only parable about carpentry. Do not be the foolish carpenter who spends all his labor squaring and smoothing a worm-eaten beam, only to find it unfit for the foundation. The intellectual and moral foundations of character must be sound enough to support the superstructure of the spiritual nature. Mind and morals are the soil; the soul is the living growth that springs from it. (156:5.2)

That evening Nathaniel asked why they should pray not to be led into temptation if God never does such a thing. Jesus answered that their forefathers, seeing God’s hand in everything, had attributed even temptation to him, but that men are usually led into temptation by their own selfishness and the impulses of their animal natures. He then contrasted two ways of meeting temptation. (156:5.3, 156:5.4)

The questions and answers that went on long into the night are given restated in modern phraseology. Like short proverbs, they serve as a mirror of a believer’s character. Human nature may tend toward evil, but it is not inherently sinful, and spiritual destiny is conditioned only by spiritual longings and purposes. (156:5.6, 156:5.8, 156:5.9)

The mistakes which you fail to forget in time will be forgotten in eternity. (156:5.8)

These sayings also address the believer’s inner stability. Believers must learn to step aside from the rush of life and renew the soul in worshipful communion. God-knowing people are not discouraged by misfortune nor depressed by material upheavals, and they endure unavoidable suffering without complaint. Difficulty only whets the ardor of the truth lover. (156:5.12, 156:5.13, 156:5.20, 156:5.21)

Every day a true believer lives, he finds it easier to do the right thing. (156:5.13)

These sayings do not confine faith to inner feeling. Self-respect cannot exceed love of one’s neighbor; we are to be citizens of heaven and useful citizens of earth; and we must seek no unearned recognition. The day before leaving Tyre, Jesus sent the twelve evangelists home by another route, and they were never again so intimately associated with him. (156:5.14, 156:5.16, 156:5.19, 156:5.23)

VI. The Return from Phoenicia — The Battle Lines Drawn

Jesus and the twelve went by way of Ptolemais, Jotapata, Ramah, and Zebulun to Gennesaret, where they met David Zebedee. They then crossed to near Kheresa and, in the park where the five thousand had been fed, held three days of conferences with about fifty men and women—the remnant of the once numerous believers of Capernaum. (156:6.1, 156:6.3, 156:6.4)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 156 shows that the place to which we are driven can become the place where we bear fruit. The humble and good-humored faith of a gentile woman, the view of this world as a bridge, and the white lily blooming from the muck all point in the same direction. And the sayings at Tyre remind us that faith must become a practical skill for dealing with failure, grudges, and temptation. (156:2.1, 156:5.1)
