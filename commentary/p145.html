Four Eventful Days at Capernaum

Four days in Capernaum: a great catch of fish, a Sabbath sermon proclaiming personal religion, 683 healed at sundown, and a Sunday dawn on which Jesus chose his spiritual mission over wonders.

Introduction — On the Threshold of Public Ministry

After John the Baptist had been sent to his death, Jesus and the apostles arrived in Capernaum on the evening of Tuesday, January 13, and as usual made the home of Zebedee in Bethsaida their headquarters. From Wednesday through Friday Jesus instructed the apostles in preparation for their first public preaching tour of Galilee, and through Andrew he arranged to speak in the synagogue on the coming Sabbath. This paper records the many events that crowded into those four days. (145:0.1, 145:0.2)

Late on Friday evening Jesus’ baby sister, Ruth, secretly came to see him, and the two spent almost an hour together in a boat anchored near the shore. The narrator tells us that Ruth was the only member of the family who believed in the divinity of his mission consistently and unwaveringly from beginning to end, and that she was his chief comfort among his earth family during his trial, rejection, and crucifixion. Set before the dramatic events that follow, this quiet meeting shows how great a comfort steadfast faith can be. (145:0.3)

I. The Draught of Fishes — A Miracle That Was No Miracle

On Friday morning, when the crowd pressed Jesus to the water’s edge, he stepped into David Zebedee’s boat and went on teaching for more than two hours. That boat, named “Simon,” was Peter’s old fishing vessel, built by Jesus’ own hands. When he had finished, Jesus told the fishermen, who had toiled all night and caught nothing, to put out into the deep and let down their nets, and they took so many fish that three boats were filled almost to sinking. From that day David Zebedee and his associates forsook their nets and followed Jesus. (145:1.1, 145:1.2)

But this was in no sense a miraculous draught of fishes. (145:1.3)

The narrator explains that Jesus, a close student of nature and an experienced fisherman, simply directed the men to the place where the fish were usually found at that time of day. Yet his followers always regarded it as a miracle. This short section is a key to the whole paper. People wanted to see miracles even in ordinary events, and Jesus was wary lest that tendency obscure his spiritual mission. (145:1.3)

II. Afternoon at the Synagogue — From National Religion to Personal Religion

On the Sabbath afternoon Jesus preached on “The Will of the Father in Heaven.” At this time more people believed in him in Capernaum than in any other city on earth. The aim of the sermon was to make clear that religion is a personal experience. (145:2.1, 145:2.3)

All these generations have you had a national or racial religion; now have I come to give you a personal religion. (145:2.4)

Jesus reminded his hearers that this was not an entirely new idea: Jeremiah and Ezekiel had already pointed the way. The proverb about the fathers eating sour grapes and the children’s teeth being set on edge would no longer hold; the law would be written in men’s hearts; and all would come to know God personally. (145:2.5, 145:2.7)

Jesus went on to say that once a person discerns this spiritual freedom, the Father wills that he should begin the eternal ascent of the Paradise career. That career consists in the creature’s conscious response to the divine urge of the indwelling spirit to find the Creator, to know God, and to seek to become like him. Personal religion, in the end, is a life that answers day by day to the spirit within. (145:2.9)

Jesus added that individual members of a family must often suffer the material consequences of group mistakes. Through this sermon the apostles came to see more fully that the gospel of the kingdom is a message directed to the individual, not to the nation. For readers who lean on the faith of a community, the passage invites reflection on what it means to stand before God as one person. (145:2.8, 145:2.10)

Do you not realize that the hope of a better nation—or a better world—is bound up in the progress and enlightenment of the individual? (145:2.8)

II (continued). After the Sermon — Coincidences Turned into Miracles

As the sermon ended, a young man in the congregation was seized with an epileptic attack and cried out; Jesus took him by the hand, said “Come out of it,” and he awoke. The narrator makes clear that he was not possessed by a demon but was a victim of ordinary epilepsy who had been taught that his affliction came from an evil spirit, and that his real healing came only after sundown. Likewise, the malarial fever of Amatha, Peter’s mother-in-law, merely happened to leave her while Jesus stood beside her. (145:2.12, 145:2.13, 145:2.16)

The narrator calls these cases typical of the way a wonder-seeking generation and a miracle-minded people seized on coincidences as proof that yet another miracle had been wrought. The Sabbath was just the time for such a rumor to spread quickly, and it soon reached the surrounding settlements. (145:2.14, 145:2.17)

III. The Healing at Sundown — Creative Power That Was Not Planned

Because Jewish teaching forbade seeking health on the Sabbath, the sick waited until the sun went down and then streamed toward Zebedee’s home, until almost a thousand people filled the front yard and the road. The narrator says that the power and authority of that afternoon’s sermon had set the stage for this scene, and records the two sayings with which it closed. (145:3.2, 145:3.3, 145:3.6)

Hate is the shadow of fear; revenge the mask of cowardice. (145:3.4)

The hearers could not forget another saying either: that man is a son of God, not a child of the devil. The narrator adds that this Sabbath was a great day not only in Jesus’ life but in the life of a universe; to all local universe intents and purposes the little city of Capernaum was the real capital of Nebadon. The handful of Jews in the synagogue were not the only beings who heard those words. (145:3.4)

Man is the son of God, not a child of the devil. (145:3.4)

The suffering throng peculiarly touched Jesus’ human heart. Yet he knew he could never build an enduring spiritual movement on purely material wonders, and since Cana it had been his consistent policy not to exhibit his creator prerogatives. In that moment, when divine wisdom and human compassion were interlocked, he sought refuge in appeal to his Father’s will. (145:3.7, 145:3.8)

Jesus said that, if it were not inconsistent with the Father’s will, he would desire to see his children made whole. His Personalized Thought Adjuster ruled instantly that such an act would not transgress the Father’s will; the celestial retinue descended, and in a moment 683 men, women, and children were healed. The narrator says that no such mass physical healing took place on earth before or after, and that of all who witnessed it, Jesus himself was the most surprised. (145:3.9, 145:3.10, 145:3.11)

What a Creator Son desires and his Father wills IS. (145:3.11)

The narrator sums up: the healing wonders were not part of Jesus’ plan for proclaiming the kingdom but were incidental to having on earth a Creator with divine mercy and human sympathy. The observation that a remarkable experience does not by itself become spiritual growth is a check on any tendency to measure faith by the intensity of experience. (145:3.14, 145:3.15)

IV. The Evening After — The Apostles’ Elation and Jesus’ Absence

That evening the apostles’ hopes rose higher than ever before. They believed they were seeing the fulfillment of Jesus’ promise that the hour had come for the kingdom to be proclaimed in power, and their lingering doubts about his divinity were banished. But Jesus was nowhere to be found. When he returned late, he refused their congratulations and adoration. (145:4.1, 145:4.2)

Rejoice not that my Father is powerful to heal the body, but rather that he is mighty to save the soul. (145:4.2)

The apostles went to bed disappointed and perplexed; except for the twins, few of them slept much. Only one thought remained in their minds: “We cannot understand him.” (145:4.3)

V. Early Sunday Morning — Teaching Before Healing

Jesus, too, slept little that night. He considered the danger that, in a world full of physical distress, caring for the sick would consume all his time and push the mission of establishing the spiritual kingdom into second place. Before daybreak he went out alone and prayed for wisdom and judgment, so that his human sympathy joined with his divine mercy would not crowd out his spiritual ministry. He did not wish to avoid ministering to the sick; he wished to protect the more important work of spiritual teaching. (145:5.1, 145:5.2)

Peter, James, and John found him, and for more than four hours Jesus tried to explain why the kingdom could not be built on wonder-working and physical healing, but they could not comprehend. Then Andrew arrived, saying that all men were seeking him and urging him to come back. Jesus answered that the people were flocking to him because their bodies had been healed rather than because they had received spiritual truth. (145:5.3, 145:5.4, 145:5.5, 145:5.6)

I have ordained you to preach the gospel and minister to the sick, but I must not become engrossed in healing to the exclusion of my teaching. (145:5.7)

And so, on the afternoon of Sunday, January 18, A.D. 28, Jesus and the apostles set out on their first public preaching tour of Galilee. The apostles regretted leaving, Peter reckoning that a thousand believers could have been baptized, until Thomas broke the silence. (145:5.8, 145:5.10)

We follow a teacher who seeks no glory for himself. (145:5.10)

Shortly after they left for Rimmon, Jesus’ brothers James and Jude called at Zebedee’s house to see him, but he had already gone. On this first tour they preached in many cities but did not visit Nazareth. The apostles followed reluctantly, yet their steps led on into the wider field of Galilee. (145:5.8, 145:5.9)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 145 sets the most spectacular day of Jesus’ life side by side with one of his firmest decisions. People saw miracles even in coincidences, and even the genuine creative healing left most of its recipients without lasting spiritual benefit. At the very peak of popularity and expectation, Jesus walked away to return to his true mission: proclaiming the truth that saves the soul. (145:3.14, 145:5.7)
