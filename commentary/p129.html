The Later Adult Life of Jesus

Introduction — A Departure Made Hard by Love

Paper 129 covers Jesus’ twenty-seventh through twenty-ninth years, after he had fully and finally stepped out of the management of the Nazareth household. Right up to his baptism he went on contributing to the family finances and taking a keen interest in the spiritual welfare of his brothers and sisters and the happiness of his mother, but he no longer directed the affairs of the home himself. (129:0.1)

The more fully we bestow ourselves upon our fellows, the more we come to love them (129:0.2)

That is why leaving was not easy for him. He had given himself so fully to his family that he loved them with a great and fervent affection. Over more than four years he let them see, little by little, that a parting was coming, so that the sadness of separation could be borne. Even when we must leave those we love, the passage suggests, the way we leave can be full of care. (129:0.2, 129:0.3)

I. The Twenty-Seventh Year (A.D. 21) — The Boatbuilder of Capernaum

On a rainy Sunday morning in January of A.D. 21, Jesus took unceremonious leave of his family, saying only that he was going to Tiberias and on to other cities about the Sea of Galilee. He was never again a regular member of that household. Finding little of interest in Tiberias, he went on to Capernaum and joined the business of his father’s friend Zebedee, the boatbuilder. (129:1.1, 129:1.2)

In little more than a year, using greatly improved methods of steaming the boards, he created a new style of boat far safer for sailing the lake, and in less than five years practically every craft on the lake had come from Zebedee’s shop. For tax purposes he registered himself as a “skilled craftsman of Capernaum,” and from then to the end of his earth life he was known as a resident of Capernaum. (129:1.3, 129:1.8)

He lived in Zebedee’s home. Having long worked alone in the world without a father, he greatly enjoyed this time of working with a father-partner. Zebedee’s wife, Salome, a relative of the former high priest Annas, loved him as she loved her own sons, and her four daughters looked on him as an elder brother. He often went down to the caravan station to keep observing how men lived, and he conducted the services more than half the time that year in the new synagogue built by the Roman commander. (129:1.4, 129:1.5, 129:1.7)

The text names a twofold secret behind the ease young people felt with him: he was always interested in what they were doing, and he seldom offered advice unless they asked for it. He talked freely about politics, sociology, science, and philosophy, but he spoke with authoritative finality only when discussing religion—the relation of man to God. (129:1.9, 129:1.10)

And it was among these workers that Jesus was first called “the Master.” (129:1.11)

He enjoyed his life in Capernaum, but he missed the children who had played beside the Nazareth carpenter shop. His brother Jude often came over on the Sabbath to hear him in the synagogue, and the more Jude saw of his eldest brother, the more convinced he became that Jesus was a truly great man. In October Jesus went back for Martha’s wedding, but after that he was not in Nazareth again for over two years. (129:1.6, 129:1.11, 129:1.13)

Zebedee’s sons each saw him differently. James was most drawn to him as a teacher and philosopher, John to his religious teaching, while David respected him only as a mechanic. This year Jesus attained new and high levels of conscious contact with his indwelling Thought Adjuster, and it proved to be the last year he lived a settled life in one place. (129:1.12, 129:1.14, 129:1.15)

This was the last year of his settled life. (129:1.15)

Now, between his simple but intensely active past and the still more strenuous public ministry to come, there would intervene a few years of extensive travel. Before he could begin his career of teaching and preaching, his training as a man of the realm had to be completed. (129:1.15)

II. The Twenty-Eighth Year (A.D. 22) — John’s Promise and the Traveler from India

Before leaving Capernaum in March of A.D. 22, Jesus told John Zebedee that he planned to travel widely until his hour should come, and he asked John to send money to the family at Nazareth each month in his stead. John promised to watch over Jesus’ family as he would his own. (129:2.3)

Go your way in peace. I will act in your stead in all these matters. (129:2.3)

John and Zebedee used the funds entrusted to them to buy a little house in Capernaum and hold it in trust, so that Jesus became the owner of a house without having been told about it. In Jerusalem, with a letter of introduction from Salome, he met the former high priest Annas and inspected the academies of the religious teachers, yet he never asked a single question in public. Annas looked on him as a great man, but he knew Jesus would never be granted the status of a regular teacher, since he had not been trained in those schools. (129:2.4, 129:2.7)

The Nazareth family, knowing nothing of the arrangement with John, decided the time had come to get along without further help from Jesus. James remembered his contract with Jesus and, with his brothers, at once took full responsibility for the family. Years of training had borne their fruit. At Passover the whole Zebedee family came up to Jerusalem and celebrated the feast in Annas’s home as one happy family. (129:2.5, 129:2.8)

During Passover week Jesus met a wealthy traveler from India and his son. They asked him to come with them to Rome and around the Mediterranean as interpreter and as tutor for the son, and when Jesus raised the needs of his family, the father offered to advance him a year’s wages. Jesus turned that sum over to John and set out on the journey. Only Zebedee knew of it, and he kept the secret to the end. (129:2.9, 129:2.10)

While he was away the family did well. Jude increased his share and kept it up until he married, and though they needed little help, John took presents each month to Mary and Ruth, as Jesus had instructed. Yet as the journey lengthened, the family all but gave Jesus up for dead; only Zebedee’s assurances kept hope alive in Mary’s heart. (129:2.10, 129:2.11)

III. The Twenty-Ninth Year (A.D. 23) — A Mediterranean Journey to Know Men

The whole of the twenty-ninth year was spent on the tour of the Mediterranean world. Throughout the journey Jesus was known as the Damascus scribe, and on the return trip as the Jewish tutor. His family believed he had been in Alexandria, and he did nothing to change that belief. (129:3.1, 129:3.2, 129:3.4)

To understand such apparently strange conduct, the narrator says, we must keep in mind the motive of the bestowal. Jesus was careful not to build up an overattractive and attention-consuming personal career, or to make overpowering appeals to his fellows. And though he lived on Urantia, he lived his life for his entire universe. (129:3.5, 129:3.6)

He wanted to make no unusual or overpowering appeals to his fellow men. (129:3.5)

This journey was a phase of his life that he never revealed to his family or to the apostles. Some friends thought he had gone to Damascus, others to India. Yet in that quiet time he practically completed his educational contact-training with the diverse peoples of his day, and he just about learned how man lived and wrought out his existence on Urantia. (129:3.3, 129:3.7)

The real purpose of his trip around the Mediterranean basin was to know men. (129:3.8)

By the end of this journey he virtually knew, with all human certainty, that he was a Creator Son. The Adjuster brought up in his mind, little by little, shadowy memories of his Paradise experience; the last of these, his farewell conference with Immanuel, became clear only on the day of his baptism in the Jordan. (129:3.9)

IV. The Human Jesus — Not a Pattern but a Way

To the onlooking celestial intelligences, this journey was the most enthralling of all his earth experiences up to the cross. He was still the carpenter of Nazareth, the boatbuilder of Capernaum, the scribe of Damascus; the Adjuster had not yet fully mastered his mortal identity. He was still a man among men. (129:4.1)

His purely human religious experience well-nigh reached its apex in this year. From the arrival of his Adjuster until the day the material mind and the spirit mind were made one, it was a steady, gradual growth, and its completion came on the day of his baptism. Though he did not appear to spend many seasons in formal prayer, he perfected ever more effective ways of communing with the indwelling spirit presence. (129:4.2, 129:4.3)

He was a child of joy and a being of rare good humor (129:4.4)

He was also a man acquainted with grief, and he knows the thoughts, feelings, and impulses of mortals from birth to death. He even experienced the higher phases of human and Adjuster reconciliation that so few Urantia mortals ever attain. His life did not win the universal approval of his contemporaries, but it received full and unqualified acceptance by the Universal Father. (129:4.4, 129:4.5, 129:4.6)

The narrator sums up that his life achieved two things at once: the fullness of the revelation of the eternal God to mortal man, and the presentation of a perfected human personality to the satisfaction of the Infinite Creator. Yet, as the table below makes plain, that perfection is not a demand that we copy the same life. (129:4.6, 129:4.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

By the end of his twenty-ninth year Jesus had virtually finished living the life required of mortals as sojourners in the flesh. He came as the fullness of God made manifest to man; he had now become well-nigh the perfection of man awaiting the occasion to be manifest to God—and he did it all before he was thirty. The paper shows that this perfection came not through special wonders but through ordinary days of working, learning, and meeting people. (129:4.8)

And he did all of this before he was thirty years of age. (129:4.8)
