Introduction — A First Taste of Freedom, and a First Disillusionment

The narrator says that no incident in Jesus’ earth career was more engaging or more humanly thrilling than this first visit to Jerusalem. The days after the Passover were the first complete freedom from responsibility he had ever known, and many years would pass before he enjoyed such freedom again. He virtually refused to go unless his mother came too, and so this company had more women, in proportion to men, than any Passover company that had ever left Nazareth. (125:0.1, 125:0.2)

Seen from the Mount of Olives, the temple was all he had hoped for, but once he entered its gates the disillusionment began. The first shock came when his mother left for the women’s gallery and could not attend his consecration. He was thoroughly indignant at this unjust discrimination, yet apart from a few words of protest to his father he said nothing—though he thought, and thought deeply. The consecration rituals seemed perfunctory and routine, and he missed the personal warmth of the synagogue at Nazareth. (125:0.3, 125:0.4, 125:0.5)

Jesus would never accept explanations of worship that involved the wrath of God or the anger of the Almighty. When his father pressed him to accept orthodox beliefs, he looked into his father’s eyes and reasoned from the love of his earthly father to the love of the heavenly Father. (125:0.6)

The heavenly Father cannot love his children less than you love me. (125:0.6)

Joseph and Mary held their peace and never again tried to change his mind about the love of God. In this short answer, which starts from the love of earthly parents and reasons upward to the love of the heavenly Father, his understanding of God is already plain. (125:0.7)

I. Jesus Views the Temple — Beauty and Spiritual Ugliness

Throughout the temple courts Jesus was shocked by a spirit of irreverence unfit for “his Father’s house.” In the court of the gentiles, the din of the money-changers and vendors gave him the shock of his young life, and the sight of courtesans parading within the temple precincts outraged his sense of propriety. (125:1.1, 125:1.2)

Jesus admired the sentiment and service of the temple, but he was shocked by the spiritual ugliness which he beheld on the faces of so many of the unthinking worshipers. (125:1.3)

Joseph wisely led his son to see the “gate beautiful” and then walked outside with Mary, talking about the feasts. But Jesus had had enough for one visit. A love of beauty and symbol, and an eye that could see through to the emptiness behind form, were awakening together in one boy. (125:1.5)

II. Jesus and the Passover — Dreaming of a Bloodless Feast

Jesus persuaded his parents to keep the Passover with the family of Simon of Bethany. Asked, as a new son of the covenant, to recount the origin of the Passover, he did it well, but he disconcerted his parents by weaving in impressions of what he had recently seen and heard. (125:2.1, 125:2.2)

He felt assured in his own mind that the Father in heaven was not pleased with this spectacle of sacrificial offerings (125:2.3)

He became more and more determined someday to establish a bloodless Passover. That night he hardly slept for dreams of slaughter, his heart torn by the inconsistencies of the whole ceremonial system. His parents also slept little but feared to talk frankly with him. The narrator adds that Jesus would gladly have talked if they had dared to encourage him. Opening the door to conversation is often the adult’s task. (125:2.3, 125:2.4)

From the next day, guided by Lazarus, he explored Jerusalem and spent most of his time at the teaching conferences near the temple. On Wednesday night at Bethany, Lazarus, Martha, and Mary heard him speak of things temporal and eternal and from then on loved him as their own brother. He talked with more than one hundred and fifty boys his age, learned how people lived across the world, and began to wish to travel the world himself. (125:2.5, 125:2.7, 125:2.12)

All through Passover week, as a new son of the commandment, he had to sit outside the rail that separated full citizens, so he held back the questions surging in his mind until the feast was over. Again and again his parents found him sitting alone with his head in his hands, but not knowing how troubled his spirit was, they did not know what to do. Before leaving they made definite arrangements for him to return at fifteen to study at a famous rabbinical academy, but they were distressed by his indifference during their visits to the school. (125:2.6, 125:2.9, 125:2.11)

III–IV. Departure of Joseph and Mary; First and Second Days in the Temple

On the first day of the week after the festival, the party set out, the men in one group and the women in another. Jesus was so absorbed in a temple discussion about angels that he forgot the time of departure. Mary assumed he was with the men and Joseph thought he was with the women, so they did not discover his absence until they reached Jericho. (125:3.1, 125:3.2)

With the crowds gone, the temple had a quieter and more decorous atmosphere. On the second day Jesus boldly asked questions, yet always in a manner consistent with his youth. His pointed questions sometimes embarrassed the teachers of the law, but because he showed candid fairness and an evident hunger for knowledge, most of them treated him with every consideration. (125:4.1, 125:4.3)

When he questioned the justice of putting to death a drunken gentile who had wandered into the forbidden precincts, an intolerant teacher demanded his age, and the teachers mocked, “he is from Nazareth.” But the leader ruled that the boy was not to blame and could continue in the discussions. That night, again, he meditated and prayed in the garden at Bethany. (125:4.3, 125:4.4)

V. The Third Day in the Temple — Teaching by Questions

On the third day word had spread and many spectators gathered; Simon too came down from Bethany. Before the day ended, the entire attention of the chief discussion group was focused on his questions. The text records five of them. (125:5.1, 125:5.2)

He conveyed his teaching by the questions he would ask. (125:5.8)

Rather than refuting his elders, he challenged their teaching and suggested his own through the questions themselves. His manner combined sagacity and humor, endearing him even to those who resented his youth, and he was always fair and considerate. This reluctance to take unfair advantage of an opponent marked his entire public ministry. (125:5.8)

he seemed to be utterly free from all egoistic desire to win an argument merely to experience logical triumph over his fellows (125:5.8)

That evening on Olivet he did not weep; he only bowed his head in silent devotion. Late into the night in the garden he tried to work out a definite plan for freeing his countrymen from their bondage to tradition, but no answer came. (125:5.9, 125:5.10)

But the clear light did not come to the truth-seeking lad. (125:5.10)

VI. The Fourth Day in the Temple — “I Will Await My Hour”

Strangely, Jesus seemed not to grasp how worried his parents would be. On the afternoon of the fourth day, just as the leader had seated him at his side and asked his views on prayer and worship, his parents found him. Mary upbraided him before everyone, and the narrator says this brought an inglorious end to one of the greatest opportunities he would ever have as a teacher of truth. (125:6.1, 125:6.3, 125:6.5, 125:6.6)

Earlier that morning the teachers had marveled at his familiarity with the Scriptures in Hebrew as well as Greek, though they were amazed more by his youth than by his knowledge of truth. Before judging the scene, the narrator asks us to remember that Jesus was by then regarded as a young man—a son of the law and a consecrated citizen of Israel. (125:6.2, 125:6.6)

“Why is it that you have so long sought me? Would you not expect to find me in my Father’s house since the time has come when I should be about my Father’s business?” (125:6.7)

When the crowd had withdrawn, he first relieved his parents’ embarrassment. His maturity shows in a single sentence that blames no one. (125:6.8)

none has done aught but that which he thought best. (125:6.8)

On the way home, on the brow of Olivet, he lamented that Jerusalem was enslaved to the Roman yoke and to its own traditions and cried out that he would return to cleanse the temple. Once home, he assured his parents of his affection and implied that he would never again cause them such anxiety. (125:6.9, 125:6.11)

“While I must do the will of my Father in heaven, I will also be obedient to my father on earth. I will await my hour.” (125:6.11)

On the three-day journey back to Nazareth Jesus said little, and his parents said little too. They could not understand their first-born son, but they treasured his sayings in their hearts. (125:6.10)

He was an artist in the matter of adjusting his dedication to duty to his obligations of family loyalty and social service. (125:6.12)

Mary, however, took the cry on Olivet as a prophecy of her son’s mission as Israel’s deliverer, and with the help of her brother she began to steer his thoughts into patriotic and nationalistic channels. The scene shows how the same words can be read in entirely different ways, according to the hearer’s expectations. (125:6.13)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 125 shows a boy holding to God as a loving Father in the very center of institutional religion. He was indignant but never rude; he questioned but never sought to win; he sensed his calling but did not abandon his family. The conclusion of the paper is that he kept both the will of his heavenly Father and obedience to his earthly father, and waited for his hour. (125:6.11, 125:6.12)
