Introduction — Waiting After Disappointment

By March 10, after the rejection at Nazareth, all the groups had gathered again at Bethsaida. Jesus spent most of the Sabbath alone in the hills, and that night he spoke for more than an hour on “The mission of adversity and the spiritual value of disappointment.” His hearers never forgot the lesson. (151:0.1)

The apostles noticed a peculiar sadness mingled with his usual cheerfulness; Jesus had not fully recovered from the sorrow of Nazareth. Meanwhile the women went from house to house in Capernaum and the surrounding villages, teaching the gospel and ministering to the sick. Even in a season of disappointment, the work did not stop. (151:0.2)

I. The Parable of the Sower — The First Story from the Boat

About this time Jesus first began to teach the multitudes in parables. On a Sunday morning, as he sat alone meditating in Andrew and Peter’s old fishing boat, almost a thousand people gathered on the shore. When Peter offered to speak, Jesus answered, “No, Peter, I will tell them a story,” and from the boat’s elevated seat he told the story of the sower. (151:1.1)

That evening Matthew asked why he spoke in dark sayings to those seeking the truth. Jesus answered that from now on he would present the mysteries of the kingdom in parables to the undiscerning multitudes and to those seeking his destruction. Those who truly desired to enter the kingdom would discern the meaning, while those who listened only to ensnare would hear without understanding. (151:1.3, 151:1.4)

to him who has shall be given so that he shall have an abundance; but from him who has not shall be taken away even that which he has (151:1.4)

II. Interpretation of the Parable — What Happens When It Is Read as Allegory

Peter and those with him concluded that every feature of the parable had a hidden meaning and asked for an explanation. Jesus first asked for their own interpretation. Peter and Nathaniel each offered an elaborate reading, and the apostles split into two nearly equal camps in heated debate. (151:2.1, 151:2.3, 151:2.4)

Source 151:2.2
ElementPeter’s interpretationNathaniel’s interpretation

The birdsSatan, or the evil oneHabits of life, the temptation of evil, the desires of the flesh

ThornsThe cares of the world and the deceitfulness of richesThe pride of life, jealousy, envy, and anxieties

Jesus let the confusion reach its height and then called them together. Thomas spoke up, reminding them that the Master had taught them to use a story to illustrate one central truth and not to seek a spiritual application for every detail. The proof, he said, was that those who had been of one mind an hour before were now divided into two camps. (151:2.5)

all such differences in results are directly due to conditions inherent in the circumstances of our ministry, conditions over which we have little or no control (151:2.6)
In your own hearts you may often profitably engage in such flights of the speculative imagination, but you make a mistake when you seek to offer such conclusions as a part of your public teaching. (151:2.7)

Jesus said that Thomas had discerned the true meaning of parables, but that Peter and Nathaniel had done everyone equal good by showing the danger of turning his parables into allegories. Speculation in one’s own heart is fine; offering it as public teaching is the mistake. Once the tension eased, Peter and Nathaniel congratulated each other on their interpretations. Ending a debate by congratulating one another is a mature way of handling differences. (151:2.7, 151:2.8)

III. More about Parables — Why Teach in Stories?

The next evening Jesus said that truth must be suited to the minds and hearts of those who hear it. One cannot speak different words to each kind of hearer in a crowd, but a story lets each person interpret it according to his own endowments. He told them not to hide the lamp under a vessel, and to pay attention not only to how the multitudes hear but to how they themselves hear. (151:3.1)

Think not only of the multitudes and how they hear the truth; take heed also to yourselves how you hear. (151:3.1)

Jesus advised against fables and allegories but recommended the free use of parables, especially nature parables. The method was found in the Hebrew scriptures, yet as he used it from this time on it became almost a new way of teaching. The narrator summarizes in modern terms the values of parables that Jesus pointed out. (151:3.3, 151:3.4, 151:3.5)

Source 151:3.6

The parable proceeds from the things which are known to the discernment of the unknown. (151:3.7)

Toward the end of that evening Jesus made his first comment on the parable of the sower. It referred to two things: a review of his own ministry so far and a forecast of the rest of his life, and a hint of what the messengers of the kingdom could expect in their ministry from generation to generation. Coming just after Nazareth, the parable was also a comfort to disappointed workers. (151:3.13)

Jesus also used parables as the best refutation of the charge that he worked by the help of demons: an appeal to nature contradicted that teaching, and the method let him convey vital truths to seekers while giving his enemies less cause for accusation. That night he added the story of seed that sprouts and grows, blade, ear, and full grain, while the sower knows not how, but he never mentioned it again. (151:3.14, 151:3.15, 151:3.16)

IV. More Parables by the Sea — “The Kingdom of Heaven Is Like…”

From the next day on Jesus told one parable after another from the boat. From this time forward he seldom taught the masses except by this means, and in the evening classes he expounded his teachings more fully to the apostles and evangelists. (151:4.1, 151:4.7)

By the principle Thomas recalled, each of these parables should also be read as told for one central truth. Rather than mining every detail for symbols, readers come closer to Jesus’ intention by grasping the one thing each story points to. (151:2.5, 151:3.3)

V. The Visit to Kheresa — The Storm and a Coincidence

Weary of the crowds, Jesus proposed resting for a day on the other side. Crossing the lake, the boat was caught in a violent evening gale. The narrator explains that the lake lies far below sea level, surrounded by high banks, so that after sunset the cooling air of the gorges rushes down on it in sudden squalls. The sail was torn away and the boat began to fill, yet Jesus slept on in the stern until Peter shook him awake. (151:5.1, 151:5.2, 151:5.3, 151:5.4)

Why are all of you so filled with fear? Where is your faith? Peace, be quiet. (151:5.5)
All this was purely coincidental as far as we can judge (151:5.5)

In this scene Jesus’ rebuke is addressed not to the wind but to the men. He looked first at Peter, then at the struggling oarsmen, then back at Peter, and bade him seek peace to quiet his troubled soul. (151:5.5)

Hardly had he spoken when the air settled into a great calm and the stars came out. Yet the narrator says that, as far as they could judge, this was purely coincidental. Jesus plainly explained that he had spoken to their fear-tossed minds, not to the elements, but it was no use; Peter never tired of saying that even the winds and the waves obey him. People do not easily give up their own interpretations. (151:5.5, 151:5.6)

Reaching shore, the party rested in the boats through the calm night. The next morning, with about forty gathered, Jesus proposed going up into the hills for a few days to ponder the problems of the Father’s kingdom. That wish soon gave way to an unexpected encounter. (151:5.7)

VI. The Kheresa Lunatic — You Are a Son of God

In the tomb caverns of the hillside lived a man named Amos. He suffered from a periodic form of insanity, and during a lucid interval he had heard the gospel at Bethsaida and become a halfhearted believer. He believed he was possessed by many devils, but the narrator makes clear that his troubles were mostly emotional. (151:6.2, 151:6.3, 151:6.4)

Amos, you are not possessed of a devil; you have already heard the good news that you are a son of God. (151:6.5)

Amos was immediately restored to his right mind. But while the swine herders ran off with the news, dogs drove an untended herd of swine over a precipice into the sea. This incidental event gave rise to the legend that the devils had entered the swine. Amos believed it, and the narrator says this belief actually helped make his cure permanent. All the apostles except Thomas believed it too. (151:6.5, 151:6.6)

Source 151:6.6

The gentiles, fearing to lose their swine, asked Jesus to leave. When Amos begged to go with him, Jesus told him to remember that he was a son of God and return to his own people. Amos went through all the cities of the Decapolis telling what great things had been done for him. (151:6.7, 151:6.8)

Forget not that you are a son of God. Return to your own people and show them what great things God has done for you. (151:6.8)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 151 sets side by side Jesus’ adoption of the parable as a new method and the human tendency to interpret events in one’s own way. The apostles turned a parable into allegory, a squall into a miracle, and a commotion among dogs into a migration of demons. The paper teaches the honesty to separate fact from interpretation, and the simplicity to hold on to one central truth. (151:2.7, 151:5.6)
