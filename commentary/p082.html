Introduction — The Home, Chief Civilizing Agency

Following the earlier papers on primitive institutions, Paper 82 looks at how the institution of marriage arose and was refined. The author, the Chief of Seraphim stationed on Urantia, defines marriage as man’s reactional adjustment to bisexuality, and family life as the sum total of all such adjustments. Marriage is not inherent in biologic evolution, but it is the basis of all social evolution and is therefore certain to endure in some form. (82:0.1, 82:6.12)

Marriage has given mankind the home, and the home is the crowning glory of the whole long and arduous evolutionary struggle. (82:0.1)

Religious, social, and educational institutions are also needed for the survival of civilization, but it is in the home and the neighborhood that a child learns most of the essentials of living. The civilizations of the past kept evolving with a bare minimum of other institutions because the home was functioning well. The starting point of this paper is that the rich heritage humanity now possesses must likewise pass to the next generation through the home. (82:0.2, 82:0.3)

The family as an educational institution must be maintained. (82:0.3)

I. The Mating Instinct — A Test of Civilization

The personality differences between men and women are great, yet the sex urge is enough to bring them together for the reproduction of the species. This instinct operated long before human beings knew love, devotion, or marital loyalty. The author distinguishes mating, an inherent tendency, from marriage, the evolutionary social repercussion of that tendency. (82:1.1)

The mating instinct, the paper says, is the one emotion that leads selfish people, under the guise of personal gratification, to place the welfare and perpetuation of the race above their own ease. In animals, instinctive periodicity restrains mating, but in man it does not, so society must impose self-control on the individual. The paper also observes that as food becomes more plentiful, the sex urge tends to become dominant. (82:1.7, 82:1.9)

The author says that the sex regulations connected with marriage reveal two things. One is the relative progress of a civilization, which has increasingly demanded that the sex urge be gratified in useful channels and according to the mores. The other is the amount of Andite stock in a people; this inheritance added imagination and aesthetic sense, and so allowed sex to be expressed in both the highest and the lowest ways. Greater sensitivity brings with it a greater task of self-control. (82:1.2, 82:1.4, 82:1.5)

Self-control, more and more self-control, is the ever-increasing demand of advancing mankind. (82:1.10)

The statement that the intelligent submission of this impulse to the regulations of society is the supreme test of the actuality of any civilization carries real weight. Secrecy, insincerity, and hypocrisy may hide the problem, but they do not solve it, nor do they bring ethical progress. An honest approach to sex is itself a measure of a civilization’s maturity. (82:1.10)

II. The Restrictive Taboos — Between Instinct and Ethics

The author sums up the evolution of marriage as the history of sex control through social, religious, and civil restrictions. Nature takes no interest in individuals or in morals; it insists only on reproduction and leaves the resulting problems to society. (82:2.1)

This social conflict consists in the unending war between basic instincts and evolving ethics. (82:2.1)

The early races had little regulation, but as soon as social groups formed, marriage rules and restrictions grew up. Even crude taboos, by separating the sexes, fostered peace, order, and industry, and from them came the customs of dress, ornament, and religious practice, and even the ideas of vice, crime, and sin. Women were always bound by more taboos than men, and married women were marked off by such signs as hair arrangement, the veil, or a ring. (82:2.3, 82:2.4, 82:2.5)

But all ancient peoples should always be studied and judged in the light of the moral standards of the mores of their own times. (82:2.2)

This principle is the key to reading the whole paper. The strange and sometimes harsh customs that follow are not there to be mocked or condemned by today’s standards; they should be read as traces of the path human beings have found between instinct and ethics.

III. Early Marriage Mores — Marriage as Status, Duty, and Test

Marriage has always had two realms: the mores and laws that regulate the outward side of mating, and the secret, personal relations of man and woman. Individuals have always rebelled against the regulations society imposes, and this is the root of the age-old sex problem. Even so, the races that exalted and practiced marriage evolved to higher levels and survived in greater numbers. (82:3.2, 82:3.3)

Marriage standards have always been a true indicator of the current power of the mores and the functional integrity of the civil government. (82:3.3)

The ways of qualifying for marriage changed as well. Tribes that had demanded feats of thievery introduced athletic contests in place of raids, and as wife purchase declined, riddle contests appeared. Later still, the woman’s side tested a man’s ability to hunt, fight, and provide for a family, or the groom proved his worth by working at least a year in the bride’s home. The belief that the unmarried could not enter the spirit world even led to child marriages and marriages for the dead. (82:3.5, 82:3.6, 82:3.8)

The paper also describes the custom of widows dying on their husbands’ graves, and trial marriages in which the couple married only after pregnancy was confirmed. The author says these trial marriages were free from every semblance of license and were sincere tests of fertility, and then draws a pointed comparison for modern readers: those who marry with the thought of convenient divorce in the back of their minds are entering a form of trial marriage far beneath the honest adventures of their ancestors. (82:3.12, 82:3.14, 82:3.15)

IV. Marriage under the Property Mores — Stability and Its Shadow

Property has been the stabilizer of marriage; religion, the moralizer. (82:4.1)

In primitive society marriage was more a business investment than a romance, arranged by parents and elders for the good of the group. As a result, the marriages of early tribes lasted longer than those of many modern peoples. But the same property idea treated a woman as a possession handed from father to husband, and regarded adultery as theft, a violation of the husband’s property rights. (82:4.2, 82:4.3)

Modern sex jealousy is not innate; it is a product of the evolving mores. (82:4.4)

The observation that primitive man was not jealous but simply guarding his property reminds us that even feelings we take to be natural were shaped by history. The chastity taboo also began as a phase of the property mores and gave rise to the custom of the bride price; when it hardened too far, it produced the practice of confining girls and the tragedy of rejected brides. A device for stability could at the same time become a device for oppression. (82:4.4, 82:4.5)

V. Endogamy and Exogamy — Marriage Widens the Peace

Primitive man early observed that outbreeding improved the quality of offspring. Inbreeding was not always bad, but the results of inbreeding among defective stocks made a strong impression, and the taboo against close marriage became fixed. The author is clear that there is no biologic instinct against in-marriage; such restrictions are wholly a matter of taboo. (82:5.1, 82:5.2, 82:5.5)

Superior groups, when isolated, always returned to consanguineous mating. The Nodites were one of the great in-marriage groups for over one hundred and fifty thousand years, and brother and sister marriages were common in early Egypt, Mesopotamia, and the lands once occupied by the Andites. Abraham himself married his half sister, but the later mores of the Jews did not allow such unions. That the same custom could be permitted in one age and forbidden in another shows clearly the evolutionary character of the mores. (82:5.4)

Outmarriage itself was a peace promoter (82:5.9)

Marriage between tribes lessened hostility and produced alliances, and together with adventure and exploration it furthered the cross-fertilization of racial cultures. The fact that the taboos extended even to in-laws with no blood relation shows that these rules came from sociology, not biology. Learning to become family with strangers was the very process by which the circle of peace was widened. (82:5.9, 82:5.10)

VI. Racial Mixtures — Telling Prejudice from Biology

There are no pure races in the world today. (82:6.1)

The last section treats a sensitive subject from a biological point of view. According to the author, all existing races are already much mixed. The paper divides the Sangik races into primary and secondary, but adds that the secondary races also had many desirable traits and were in some respects superior to the primary races. It reads less as a simple ranking than as a statement that each stock has its own contribution to make. (82:6.1, 82:6.2, 82:6.10)

The standard the paper uses is not color but the quality of the stocks. The union of superior stocks increases vigor and creativity, while the union of degenerate stocks yields unsatisfactory results even within one race. Most objections to race mixture, in the paper’s judgment, arise from social and cultural prejudice rather than biology, and the danger of disharmony has been greatly exaggerated. (82:6.3, 82:6.5, 82:6.7)

The chief troubles of "half-breeds" are due to social prejudices. (82:6.8)

A present-day reader will be closer to the paper’s intent by taking this passage not as grounds for looking down on any group, but as a warning that prejudice makes problems larger than they really are. Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 82 shows that marriage did not descend from heaven as a finished institution. It was shaped little by little, through taboo, property, and religion, amid the unending clash of instinct and ethics. The way was clumsy and at times cruel, yet at its end humanity gained the home, its crowning glory. We who come after are responsible for guarding that inheritance with self-control and honesty.
