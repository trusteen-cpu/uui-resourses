Introduction — Quiet Preparation Before the Public Ministry

In September and October of A.D. 27, Jesus and the apostles lived at a secluded camp on the slopes of Mount Gilboa. The Jerusalem leaders were hostile, Herod Antipas held John in prison and was suspicious of his connection with Jesus, and tension was growing between John’s disciples and Jesus’ apostles. These three circumstances led to the retirement. (144:0.1, 144:0.2)

Jesus knew that the days of preliminary work were nearly over. He wanted to begin the final effort of his life, but in a way that would cause John the Baptist no pain or embarrassment. So he chose to train the apostles while awaiting John’s fate. This is also the paper that treats the subject of prayer most deeply. (144:0.3)

I. The Gilboa Encampment — Three Reasons for Retirement

Source 144:1.2
Reason for retirementContent
FirstTo confirm their understanding of, and faith in, the gospel of the kingdom
SecondTo allow opposition in Judea and Galilee to quiet down
ThirdTo await the fate of John the Baptist

The apostles’ devotion still rested mainly on personal loyalty; they had not yet grasped the full meaning of Jesus’ teaching and bestowal. They also differed about the kingdom: John and Andrew held that it had already come, Peter and James that it had not yet come, while the twins remained serene, hardly aware of the debate. Jesus spent most of this time alone on the mountain. (144:1.1, 144:1.7, 144:1.8)

The text says that what Jesus did in those hours, after the forty days following his baptism, is better called personal communion with the Father than prayer or worship. Through September the discussions centered on prayer and worship, until at last Thomas asked, “Master, teach us how to pray.” Jesus did not favor set forms of prayer, but since the common people needed a simple prayer, he granted the request. (144:1.8, 144:1.9, 144:1.10)

II. The Discourse on Prayer — The Breath of the Soul

Jesus said that the short prayer John had taught was meant for the multitude, not as a mold into which the apostles should pour their own souls. Then he defined what prayer is. (144:2.1)

Prayer is entirely a personal and spontaneous expression of the attitude of the soul toward the spirit (144:2.2)

With the stories of the friend who went at midnight to borrow bread and the widow who kept coming to the unjust judge, Jesus taught persistence. But he made it clear that these stories did not mean petitions can change the Father. Even when his children ask foolishly, the Father gives what is fitting, not a stone or a serpent. (144:2.3, 144:2.4, 144:2.5)

Your persistence, however, is not to win favor with God but to change your earth attitude and to enlarge your soul’s capacity for spirit receptivity. (144:2.5)

This passage matters especially for readers discouraged by unanswered prayer. The first fruit of prayer is not getting what we want but becoming a larger vessel for receiving the spirit. Jesus added that people exercise too little faith when they pray. (144:2.6)

III. The Believer’s Prayer — The Prayer of the Nazareth Home

James Zebedee wanted a form of prayer to teach new believers, not the apostles themselves. Jesus offered the prayer he had taught his brothers and sisters in Nazareth. He always taught that one should pray in secret, he was reluctant to pray in public, and he never taught a form of individual prayer, only group or family prayers. (144:3.1, 144:3.2, 144:3.14, 144:3.16)

Refresh our souls with the water of life. (144:3.8)

Following the request for tomorrow’s bread, this line asks for food for the soul along with food for the body. The text notes that in later copying two lines were lost and other words added. The closing request, to be made more and more like the Father in perfection, shows that this prayer reaches beyond daily needs toward growth in perfection. (144:3.12, 144:3.15)

In his own all-night prayers on the mountain, Jesus prayed mainly for his disciples and very little for himself. Instead he gave much time to worship of a kind that was understanding communion with the Father. (144:3.23)

IV. More about Prayer — Batteries and Receivers

Jesus’ answers to the questions that followed for several days are summarized in modern terms. Sincere prayer offered in faith, even for things foolish or impossible to grant directly, never fails to enlarge the soul’s capacity for spiritual receptivity. Sonship is not earned by effort but received as a gift of grace through faith; what a person does achieve is righteousness, the progressive growth of character. (144:4.2, 144:4.3)

Prayer is the breath of the spirit life in the midst of the material civilization of the races of mankind. (144:4.7)

Peter, James, and John spent long nights with Jesus without hearing him pray, because most of his praying was done silently, in spirit and mind. His habit shows that prayer is a matter not of the quantity of words but of the soul’s direction. (144:4.10)

V. Other Forms of Prayer — Seven Specimen Prayers

To illustrate other matters, Jesus presented several more forms of prayer, asking the apostles not to teach these “prayer parables” to the multitude. The text explains that many of them came from other inhabited planets, and that specific permission was granted to transcribe seven of them into this record. (144:5.1, 144:5.102)

Different as their wording is, the seven prayers share a common movement. They ask for the Father’s character, seek guidance for today, long to become more and more like him, and hope at last to be received into his arms. The apostles could not teach them publicly, but they profited much from them in their personal religious experience. (144:5.47, 144:5.102)

VI. Conference with John’s Apostles — Learning to Co-operate Without Jesus

A chance meeting in a market in early October led to a three-week conference at Gilboa between Jesus’ twelve apostles and John’s twelve. Jesus firmly declined to take part in the discussions and spoke only three times, on sympathy, co-operation, and tolerance. He said that he would help with personal religious problems as the Father’s representative, but that questions of the socialization of religion they must settle themselves, and he left them for two weeks. (144:6.1, 144:6.2, 144:6.3)

I am the representative of the Father to the individual, not to the group. (144:6.3)

The text calls this the first attempt of Jesus’ followers to socialize their personal religious practice. The twenty-four men disagreed, debated, prayed, and compromised, and learned to tolerate one another’s honest opinions. On his return Jesus promised to help them carry out the spirit of what they had agreed together. His way of leaving a community to settle its own affairs has much to say to communities of faith today. (144:6.10, 144:6.11, 144:6.12)

VII–VIII. In the Decapolis Cities; In Camp near Pella — An Answer for John

In November and December Jesus and the twenty-four worked in pairs in Scythopolis, Gerasa, Abila, and Gadara, John’s apostles baptizing and Jesus’ apostles teaching. The text observes that the socialized religion of a new revelation always pays the price of compromise with the forms of earlier religion, and explains that baptism was the price paid for bringing John’s followers along. (144:7.1, 144:7.3)

At the end of December, at the camp near Pella, messengers came from John in prison to ask whether Jesus was the true Deliverer. Jesus told them to report that John was not forgotten and that the gospel was being preached to the poor. Then he told the crowd that John was asking not from doubt but to reassure his disciples, and that John was no reed shaken by the wind but more than a prophet. (144:8.2, 144:8.3)

among those born of women there has not arisen a greater than John the Baptist; yet he who is but small in the kingdom of heaven is greater (144:8.4)

The reason is that even the least in the kingdom knows he has been born of the spirit and has become a son of God. The saying shows that the measure of greatness is not achievement but the awareness of sonship. Jesus then invited all who labor and are heavy laden to come and find rest for their souls. (144:8.4, 144:8.8)

IX. Death of John the Baptist — Now Openly

John was executed by order of Herod Antipas on the evening of January 10, A.D. 28. When his disciples brought the news, Jesus called the twenty-four together, told them to arrange their affairs that night, and declared that the time had come to proclaim the kingdom openly and with power. On the morning of January 13 they set out for Capernaum. (144:9.1, 144:9.2)

The hour has come to proclaim the kingdom openly and with power. (144:9.1)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 144 records the quiet preparation just before the public ministry and places prayer at its center. Prayer is not a technique for persuading God; it changes the attitude of the soul and enlarges its capacity to receive the spirit. And having taught them to pray, Jesus stepped back so that his followers could learn to reconcile their opinions and work together. (144:2.5, 144:6.11)
