Introduction — John Goes for the Master’s Mother

Before he was taken to Herod, Jesus told John that there was nothing more he could do for him, and asked him to bring his mother to see him before he died. John was reluctant to leave his Master alone among his enemies, but he hurried off at once to Bethany, where Jesus’ family was gathered at the home of Martha and Mary. (186:0.1)

John, you can do no more for me. Go to my mother and bring her to see me ere I die. (186:0.1)

The family had reached Bethany only a few minutes before John arrived. Mary set out with John immediately, and Jesus’ sister Ruth, refusing to stay behind, followed along with her brother Jude. But by the time they reached the city, Jesus had already arrived at Golgotha. At James’s direction the rest of the family remained in Bethany, receiving news from David’s messengers. (186:0.2, 186:0.3)

I. The End of Judas Iscariot — The True Face of Sin

About half past eight in the morning Pilate’s examination ended, and Jesus was handed over to the Roman soldiers. The high priest and his associates had returned to the temple’s hall of hewn stone to report to the Sanhedrin. Just then Judas appeared to claim his reward. Although his conscience had smitten him throughout the trial, he still expected to be praised before the council and to receive honors. (186:1.1, 186:1.2)

Instead, a servant of the high priest called him out of the chamber and handed him a bag holding thirty pieces of silver, the current price of a good, healthy slave. Judas was stunned. He tried to go back in but was turned away by the doorkeeper. As if in a daze, he left the temple and wandered along with the crowds heading out to watch the crucifixion. (186:1.2, 186:1.3)

When he saw from a distance the cross being raised, Judas rushed back to the temple, forced his way past the doorkeeper, and stood before the council, which was still in session. Nearly breathless, he stammered out a confession of his wrong and tried to return the money. His words came too late, but his remorse was real. (186:1.4)

I have sinned in that I have betrayed innocent blood. (186:1.4)

In this scene the narrator lays bare the nature of sin. The glamor and intoxication of wrongdoing vanish, and the evildoer stands alone before the verdict of his own soul. What seemed an adventure in the committing now yields its harvest of plain facts. (186:1.6)

Sin was bewitching and adventurous in the committing, but now must the harvest of the naked and unromantic facts be faced. (186:1.6)

It is a heavy record: a man who had once been an ambassador of the kingdom of heaven ended his life forsaken and alone. It reads less as a condemnation of one person than as a warning of where self-centered calculation and nursed resentment can lead a human being. (186:1.7)

II. The Master’s Attitude — When to Speak and When to Be Silent

When he was arrested, Jesus knew that his work on earth in the flesh was finished, and he took little interest in the details of his trials. Yet he was not silent without exception. He always answered questions about the nature and divinity of his mission, whoever asked them, and he spoke when he thought a sincere person might learn more of the truth. (186:2.1, 186:2.2)

Soldiers and servantsMocked and struck, he showed patience and self-control, without resentment

The text says that he had taught the futility of casting pearls before swine, and now he had the courage to practice it. His conduct joined the patient submission of human nature with the majestic silence of the divine. Nor did he try to change the outcome by persuasive eloquence, for he was convinced it was the Father’s will that he follow the natural course of human events. (186:2.2, 186:2.3)

He could not be intimidated. (186:2.5)

When the false testimony before Caiaphas had collapsed, Jesus answered the high priest’s question without hesitating, and so himself supplied the ground his enemies wanted. Truthful witness to his mission mattered more to him than protecting himself. Even when they blindfolded him, struck him in the face, and taunted him to prophesy, he did not become angry. His love for ignorant men was shown most clearly in just this patience and self-control. (186:2.7, 186:2.10)

He was not interested in Pilate’s halfhearted efforts to release him, yet he pitied Pilate and tried to enlighten his darkened mind. The text records that the watching celestial hosts reported this trial to the universe as a scene of “Pilate on trial before Jesus.” (186:2.6, 186:2.8)

And as Pilate spoke, there echoed throughout all Nebadon, “Behold God and man!” (186:2.11)

All his life Jesus never failed to reveal God to man, and now, in these final episodes and in his death, he made a new revelation of man to God. The few words he spoke during his trials were enough to show the whole universe what kind of character a human being can attain in partnership with God. (186:2.9, 186:2.11)

III. The Dependable David Zebedee — Keeping the News Moving

When the temple guards went out to Gethsemane to arrest the followers, they found the camp deserted. David had expected the enemy to return and had moved some of the tents farther up the ravine; the guards burned the empty camp and went back. The Sanhedrin, satisfied that the followers had been thoroughly cowed, adjourned. (186:3.1)

Within five minutes of Jesus’ being handed over to the Roman soldiers, David’s messengers were on their way to Bethsaida, Pella, Sidon, Damascus, Alexandria, and elsewhere. All that day he sent messengers every half hour to bring news to the apostles, the Greeks, and Jesus’ family. (186:3.2, 186:3.3)

David was the only one of the leading disciples who took literally Jesus’ statement that he would rise on the third day. Knowing the others did not expect it, he said little about his belief, but he quietly arranged for his messengers to gather at the home of Nicodemus early on Sunday morning. A simple and steady faith became the most practical preparation on the darkest of days. (186:3.4)

That the scattered community could follow events throughout that day was owing to the organization and faithfulness of this one man. Service of this kind, easily overshadowed by great events, shows what actually holds a community of faith together in a crisis. (186:3.3)

IV. Preparation for the Crucifixion — The Son of Man Left Alone

Pilate washed his hands in an effort to escape the guilt, yet in fact he delivered an innocent man to be crucified. The soldiers took off the robe Herod had put on Jesus and dressed him again in his own clothes; they mocked him, but inflicted no further physical abuse. His friends were now in hiding, his enemies had gone their way, and not even John was beside him. (186:4.1)

From the time he was handed over, a little after eight, until the procession started out shortly before nine, Jesus said nothing. The text tells us that during this time the executive business of a universe was virtually at a standstill. The soldiers began to be impressed by his composure, his dignity, and his uncomplaining silence. The departure was delayed because the captain decided at the last moment to take along two thieves already condemned to die. One of them had several times heard Jesus teach, both in the temple and at the Pella camp. (186:4.2, 186:4.3, 186:4.4, 186:4.5)

V. Jesus’ Death in Relation to the Passover — Not a Sacrifice

It is true that Jesus died at about the time the Passover lambs were being slain in the temple, but the text states plainly that his death had nothing to do with the Jewish sacrificial system. The events that led to that hour show that his death was purely natural and man-managed. (186:5.1)

It was man and not God who planned and executed the death of Jesus on the cross. (186:5.2)

The Father did not interfere with the march of human events, but neither did he decree, demand, or require the death of his Son in this way. The experience needed for the bestowal had already been completed at his baptism; the manner of his later life and death was his personal ministry for the welfare of his creatures. (186:5.2, 186:5.3)

A common ideaWhat the text says
Jesus’ death is bound up with the Passover sacrificeThere is no direct connection; the timing was coincidental (186:5.1)
God required the death of his SonMan planned and carried it out; the Father did not require it (186:5.2)
His death atoned for the guilt of the raceMankind has no racial guilt; guilt is a matter of personal, deliberate rebellion (186:5.7)
The gospel depends on Jesus’ deathThe gospel was illuminated far more by his life than by his death (186:5.4)
The Father loves us more because of the bestowalThe Father’s love is unchanged; celestial intelligences came to know man in a new way (186:5.6)
True, indeed, all this gospel of the kingdom has been tremendously illuminated by the Master’s death, but even more so by his life. (186:5.4)
Jesus is not about to die as a sacrifice for sin. (186:5.7)

The text adds that sin and rebellion have nothing to do with the fundamental bestowal plan of the Sons of God; the plan of salvage appears only as an incidental feature of the bestowal. So when we look at the death of Jesus, the emphasis falls not on a transaction in which a price is paid but on the love he revealed in following the Father’s will to the end. (186:5.8)

Salvation would have been just as certain had Jesus not died at cruel hands. The love of God and the fact of sonship are relationships that have existed from eternity; they do not depend on the bestowal drama. Only one thing is needed to make them real in personal experience. (186:5.5, 186:5.9)

You mortals are the sons of God, and only one thing is required to make such a truth factual in your personal experience, and that is your spirit-born faith. (186:5.9)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 186 lights the hours before the cross through three figures: Judas, who collapsed in despair; Jesus, who never lost his dignity; and David, who kept the news moving to the end. And it asks us not to see Jesus’ death as something God demanded, but to distinguish the deeds of men from the unchanging love of God. (186:5.2, 186:5.6)
