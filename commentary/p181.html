Introduction — Hearing a Promise the Way We Want

After the farewell discourse, Jesus visited informally with the apostles. At last it began to dawn on these Galileans that their Master was really going to leave them. Their hope grasped at the promise that he would be with them again after a little while, but they easily forgot that this return, too, would be only for a little while. (181:0.1)

The text pinpoints the reason sharply. Many took the promise to mean that the Master was going to the Father for a brief visit and would soon return to establish the kingdom, because that reading fit both their lifelong beliefs and their ardent hopes. This observation, that people readily find an interpretation that justifies their own longings, serves as a mirror for the reader of the whole paper. (181:0.1, 181:0.2)

I. Last Words of Comfort — A Peace the World Cannot Give

Jesus said that while he was in the flesh he could be but one individual, but once delivered from mortal nature he could return as a spirit indweller of every believer. So the Son of Man would become a spiritual incarnation in the souls of all true believers. He would guide them not only through this life but through the many abodes of the heaven of heavens. (181:1.1, 181:1.2)

Life in the Father’s eternal creation is not an endless rest of idleness and selfish ease but rather a ceaseless progression in grace, truth, and glory. (181:1.2)

He told them that to follow him they should not imitate the natural life he had been required to live in the flesh, but live according to the spirit of his teachings and the ideal of his life: doing the Father’s will. The distinction matters. Discipleship does not mean copying Jesus’ trade, dress, or way of living, but inheriting the devotion to the Father’s will that he carried. (181:1.3)

Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you. I make these gifts not as the world gives—by measure—I give each of you all you will receive. (181:1.5)

He said that when persecution scattered them and they felt alone in the world, he would know of their isolation, just as they would know of his when they scattered and left the Son of Man in the hands of his enemies. Yet he was never alone; the Father was always with him. To be alone and yet not alone is the real shape of the peace he leaves. For those who receive the Spirit of Truth, it becomes a well of living water springing up into eternal life. (181:1.4, 181:1.6)

In this world you will have tribulation, but be of good cheer; I have triumphed in the world and shown you the way to eternal joy and everlasting service. (181:1.6)

The narrator explains this peace by contrasting it with two kinds of worldly comfort. All that unbelieving materialists can hope for is stoicism, which resolves to endure the worst, or optimism, which longs for a peace that never really comes. Both are of some use in earthly life, but neither has anything to do with the peace Jesus gives. (181:1.7, 181:1.8)

Jesus was thoroughly devoted to his mission, yet he was not an unfeeling stoic; he always looked for the cheerful side of life, yet he was not a blind optimist. He knew all that was to befall him, and he was unafraid. His peace is the assurance of a son who fully believes that his career for time and eternity is safely in the keeping of an all-wise, all-loving, and all-powerful spirit Father. It passes the understanding of the mortal mind, but the believing heart can enjoy it to the full. (181:1.9, 181:1.10)

II. Farewell Personal Admonitions (1) — John, Simon Zelotes, and Matthew

Having finished his admonitions to the group, Jesus went around the table and spoke to each one in turn, and each man rose as the Master addressed him. The scene shows that the Master knew and loved the eleven not only as a group but as individuals, each with a different temperament, weakness, and mission. (181:2.1)

To John, the youngest, he entrusted the care of his earthly family, since John would very likely outlive the others. To the “son of thunder” who once wanted fire called down on unbelievers, he said that he should become the apostle of the new commandment. When John asked how he could learn to love his brethren more, Jesus answered that he would learn it when he first loved their Father in heaven more and became truly interested in their welfare. (181:2.2, 181:2.3, 181:2.4, 181:2.5)

And all such human interest is fostered by understanding sympathy, unselfish service, and unstinted forgiveness. (181:2.5)

Jesus paused for a moment with a sad countenance beside Judas’s empty seat, but no one except possibly Andrew guessed its meaning. To Simon Zelotes, the fiery patriot, he said that he did not doubt his loyalty, but that Simon was still set on making the kingdom come according to his own liking. No one would be more disheartened after the Master’s departure. Yet he would always be the Master’s apostle, and he was promised that the Master would be with him through all his season of discouragement. (181:2.6, 181:2.7, 181:2.10, 181:2.11)

once more I warn you that they who fight with the sword perish with the sword, while they who labor in the spirit achieve life everlasting in the kingdom to come with joy and peace in the kingdom which now is. (181:2.11)

To Matthew, the former publican, Jesus said that he knew of his anxieties and labors to keep the treasury replenished, which his brethren did not know. The apostles would soon be scattered and go out alone to the ends of the earth, and Andrew would no longer direct them. Matthew’s mission was to show the world what the Father could do for a hated tax-gatherer, and to dedicate his life to showing that God is no respecter of persons. (181:2.12, 181:2.13, 181:2.14)

II. Farewell Personal Admonitions (2) — The Other Eight

To James he said that true wisdom embraces discretion as well as courage, and sagacity along with aggressiveness. There would come supreme moments when his disciples must lay down their lives, but in ordinary circumstances it would be far better to placate the wrath of unbelievers and live long to win souls. Andrew he commended for his fair leadership and released from responsibility as administrative head. Yet his moral responsibility to hold his brethren together with a loving hand in the trying time ahead remained. (181:2.15, 181:2.16, 181:2.17)

Our kingdom is a realm of order, and where two or more will creatures act in co-operation, there is always provided the authority of leadership. (181:2.16)

The last charge to Andrew brings out the worth of an unseen place. If his brethren wished, he was to promote peace and harmony among the groups of believers and show impartial love to the Greeks in the West and to Abner in the East. Though the great works seen by men might not fall to him, he was to be content to be the teacher and counselor of those who did them. These words comfort all who serve behind the scenes. (181:2.18)

To the Alpheus twins Jesus said not to be discouraged when there were no multitudes to manage. They would show how cheerfully and courageously a person called for a season to God’s special service can return to the labors of former days. At the heart of this charge is the realization that to one who knows God there is no such thing as common labor or secular toil. (181:2.19)

To you who have worked with me, all things have become sacred, and all earthly labor has become a service even to God the Father. (181:2.19)

To Philip he first read aloud the last question in his heart: what would he ever do if the Master went away? The world, Jesus said, is full of people who look at life as Philip did, so he had a great mission. To Nathaniel he said that the expression of even a good thought must be adjusted to the state of the hearer. When Nathaniel confessed that he could not understand the full meaning of the teaching, Jesus answered that he must now learn from the master of all teachers, actual experience. (181:2.20, 181:2.21, 181:2.23, 181:2.24)

Sincerity is most serviceable in the work of the kingdom when it is wedded to discretion. (181:2.21)
what you have not been able to get from my teachings and my life, you must now prepare to acquire at the hand of that master of all teachers—actual experience. (181:2.24)

To Thomas he said that his doubts had perplexed his brethren but had never troubled the Master. Last of all, he told Peter he was distressed that Peter had not learned to think before he spoke. When Peter declared that even if all the others stumbled he would die for the Master, Jesus foretold that before the cock crowed that night Peter would deny him three or four times. What he had failed to learn in peaceful association, he would learn through much trouble and many sorrows. (181:2.26, 181:2.27, 181:2.28, 181:2.29)

Jesus told all the apostles not to be dismayed that they could not grasp the full meaning of the gospel, for they were finite mortals and what he had taught was infinite and eternal. He promised again that when he was raised up he would tarry with them for a season, and he told them he loved them with the love wherewith the Father loved him. Then they sang a hymn and left for the camp on the Mount of Olives. (181:2.25, 181:2.30, 181:2.31)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 181 records how the departing Master left peace to the whole group and a word meant only for each one. He did not cover up their weaknesses but named them honestly, while making clear that those weaknesses did not lessen his affection in the least. Reading the eleven admonitions in turn, the reader finds himself standing in the place of the apostle nearest his own temperament and hearing the word meant for him. (181:2.27)
