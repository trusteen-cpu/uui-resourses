Introduction — After Coming Down from the Hills

In Paper 136 Jesus settled, in the isolation of the hills, on the methods of his ministry. Paper 137 follows the four months from February 23, A.D. 26, when he came down from the hills and rejoined John’s company at Pella, to June 22, when he preached his first public sermon on the kingdom. The opening scene is a humble one. He spent the whole day mingling with the multitude, and when a lad hurt himself in a fall, Jesus cared for him and took him safely home to his parents. (137:0.1)

The paper can be read as a story of crossed expectations. His family and his new disciples waited for a dazzling Messianic debut, while Jesus asked them again and again to wait on the Father’s will. In between came the unlooked-for event at Cana, and the tension it created was gradually resolved, through four quiet months of training, into the proclamation of a spiritual kingdom.

I. Choosing the First Four Apostles — Andrew, Peter, James, and John

Of all John’s followers, Andrew was the most deeply impressed with Jesus. He walked with him on the trip to Pella, asked him many questions, and confessed that although he did not understand all the teaching, he had made up his mind to follow. Jesus welcomed him as the first of the twelve apostles. Andrew soon brought his brother Simon, and the two went first to consult John. John was saddened to lose them, but he answered bravely that this was only the beginning. (137:1.1, 137:1.3)

Simon, your enthusiasm is commendable, but it is dangerous to the work of the kingdom. I admonish you to become more thoughtful in your speech. (137:1.3)

James and John, the sons of Zebedee, had spent many days searching the hills for Jesus. When they returned and learned that others had been chosen first, their hearts were heavy. Jesus’ answer turns a question of order into a question of the heart. They had belonged to the kingdom even before they asked; and had they not been away on a well-intentioned but self-appointed search, they might have been first before men as well. (137:1.5, 137:1.6)

You should learn to search for the secret of the new kingdom in your hearts and not in the hills. (137:1.6)

The two brothers took the rebuke in good grace and never again envied Andrew and Simon. From that day the word “apostle” set the chosen family of Jesus’ advisers apart from the great multitude of believing disciples who came later. The lesson of the scene is that who was called first matters far less than whether the heart is set on the Father’s will. (137:1.7)

II. Choosing Philip and Nathaniel — “Follow Me”

On February 24 Jesus took leave of John the Baptist, never again to see him in the flesh. That same day the first great division broke out among John’s followers. Ezra could not accept a carpenter as the Deliverer and went off south with many disciples; the text tells us that a remnant of this sect persists in Mesopotamia even today. (137:2.1, 137:2.2)

On the road to Galilee they met Philip of Bethsaida. Without a moment’s warning, by the roadside, the most momentous decision of his life confronted him. When Philip put his question to Jesus, the answer was short. Philip then hurried back to his friend Nathaniel, who sat under a mulberry tree, deep in thought. (137:2.3, 137:2.4, 137:2.5, 137:2.6)

So Jesus gathered half of his future corps of intimate associates. That night, in his boyhood home at Nazareth, he destroyed every trace of his writing that remained about the house. The disciples never afterward saw him write, except upon the dust or in the sand, and this made a deep impression on them. (137:2.8, 137:2.9)

III. The Visit to Capernaum — Jesus Lighthearted Again

The family, too, was stirred by the news from the new associates. Joseph said that perhaps Mother had been right after all, and Mary encouraged her sons to have faith in their brother as the deliverer of Israel. Jesus’ friends in Capernaum saw a great and pleasant change in him. Through the years before his baptism and the isolation periods around it, he had grown serious and self-contained; now he seemed cheerful and natural again, much as he had been in the early Nazareth years. (137:3.2, 137:3.3, 137:3.4)

There was about him something of majestic import and exalted aspect, but he was once again lighthearted and joyful. (137:3.4)

Yet the expectations were already off course. Mary believed that Gabriel’s promise was about to be fulfilled and that her son would be revealed as the supernatural king of the Jews. The disciples were confident that he would show his Messianic power at the wedding in Cana. Jesus repeatedly warned them to tell no one about him until the Father’s hour should come. (137:3.5, 137:3.6, 137:3.7)

IV. The Wedding at Cana — A Wonder That Was No Miracle

Almost a thousand guests gathered at Cana, more than four times the number invited. When Mary and James asked when and how he would reveal himself, Jesus only told them to tarry with him while he waited on the Father’s will. Their request was, in effect, a proposal that he display his divinity in exactly the way he had decided in the hills not to do. When the wine ran out, Mary appealed to her son again. Jesus refused, yet before her tears his human heart was overcome with compassion. (137:4.1, 137:4.4, 137:4.5, 137:4.9)

Most gladly would I do what you ask of me if it were a part of the Father’s will— (137:4.9)

In the moment he broke off, he realized that he had already said, or rather desirefully thought, too much. The necessary personalities were gathered near at hand, and in the face of the expressed wish of the Universe Creator Sovereign there was no escaping the appearance of the wine. The Personalized Adjuster had signified that this was in no way contrary to the Father’s will. Of everyone present, Jesus was the most surprised. (137:4.9, 137:4.12)

Now everyone believed in him as the Messiah, but Jesus was sorely perplexed, knowing that they believed only because of an unusual occurrence they had happened to witness. He saw that he must constantly be on guard lest his sympathy and pity lead to more episodes of this kind. Even a good heart, the scene teaches, can cloud a mission when it chooses the wrong means. (137:4.16, 137:4.17)

V. Back in Capernaum — A Night by the Lake

Without taking leave of anyone, Jesus left for Bethsaida with his six apostles and warned them to say nothing about the water turned into wine. That evening at Zebedee’s house he tried to make clear who he was, what his mission was, and how it might end, and the apostles were stunned into silence. His brother Jude confessed that though he did not understand, he believed, and went home to Magdala. (137:5.1, 137:5.2)

That night Jesus did not sleep; he sat by the lake thinking until dawn. He came to see that he would never be able to make his followers see him as anything other than the long-expected Messiah. He was not the Davidic type of Messiah, yet he truly fulfilled the words of the more spiritually minded seers of old. From then on he never wholly denied that he was the Messiah, and he left the untangling of this complicated situation to the outworking of the Father’s will. (137:5.3)

VI. The Events of a Sabbath Day — A Kingdom That Comes Without Noise

On the Sabbath of March 2, his first public appearance since his baptism, the Capernaum synagogue was crowded to overflowing. The audience expected some display of supernatural power, but Jesus read from Isaiah and left them only a short word about patience. That afternoon, in a boat, he told the apostles to go on with their regular work until the hour of the kingdom came and to spend three hours every evening in study. (137:6.1, 137:6.3, 137:6.5)

my kingdom is not to come with noise and glamor, but rather must it come through the great change which my Father will have wrought in your hearts and in the hearts of those who shall be called to join you in the councils of the kingdom. (137:6.5)

He promised them joy, but he also warned of trouble, and he told them that a generation of sign seekers would be slow to accept the revelation of the Father’s love as the credentials of his mission. Praying at the water’s edge, he gave thanks for “these little ones” who believed in spite of their doubts. Here the central theme of the paper comes into focus: the kingdom is not an outward event but an inward change. (137:6.5, 137:6.6)

VII. Four Months of Training — Belonging to No Party

From March through June Jesus held more than one hundred sessions with his six associates and his brother James. Not one miracle happened, and Mary’s faith sank to new low levels. Yet the seven were learning that it was really easy to be friends with the God-man. To the impatient Peter, Jesus’ reply was always to be patient and make progress. (137:7.1, 137:7.2, 137:7.3)

We are seeking to win all of them, but we are not of any of them. (137:7.5)

All these groups looked for a national deliverer, but Jesus made it plain that he would ally himself with none of these schools. From the start he taught that the kingdom of heaven was a spiritual experience, the enthronement of God in human hearts. In later years the apostles remembered these four months as the most precious time of their whole association with the Master, because he taught them only as much as they could assimilate. (137:7.12, 137:7.13, 137:7.14)

VIII. Sermon on the Kingdom — The First Full Proclamation

When he heard that John had been arrested, Jesus laid down his tools and said that the Father’s hour had come. Tuesday, June 18, was his last day at the carpenter’s bench. On the Sabbath of June 22, in the Capernaum synagogue, he made the first pretentious public effort of his career. (137:8.2, 137:8.3, 137:8.4)

The close of the sermon shows clearly how Jesus differed from John. John preached repentance to prepare people; Jesus proclaimed faith, the gift of God, as the price of entrance into the kingdom. Along with this came the word that whoever would be great must first become the server of all. (137:8.11, 137:8.17)

If you would but believe that my Father loves you with an infinite love, then you are in the kingdom of God. (137:8.17)

The response divided three ways. About a third believed, even without fully understanding; about a third prepared in their hearts to reject so purely spiritual a concept of the kingdom; and the rest could not grasp the teaching, many thinking he was beside himself. (137:8.18)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 137 is a story of expectations that keep missing the mark. People waited for signs and a coronation; Jesus gave them waiting, daily work, and study, and at last proclaimed a kingdom established in the heart. What he taught was not a dramatic event but an inward change that grows through faith and service. (137:6.5, 137:7.13)
