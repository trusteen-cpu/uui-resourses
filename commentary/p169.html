Introduction — A Peak Week Amid Gathering Opposition

Late on Monday evening, March 6, Jesus and the ten apostles reached the Pella camp. The week that followed was to be his last there. Every afternoon he preached to the crowds, and every night he answered questions from the apostles and the more advanced disciples. Word of the resurrection of Lazarus had arrived two days ahead of him, and the whole camp was astir; the narrator remarks that nothing since the feeding of the five thousand had so fired the imagination of the people. (169:0.1, 169:0.2)

Yet at this very height of the second phase of the public ministry, the Pharisees and the chief priests were beginning to formulate their charges. Their objections fall under four heads, and the list shows which parts of Jesus’ teaching most troubled the religious leaders of his day. (169:0.3)

Source 169:0.4

The paper records four scenes from that week: the stories of three lost things told to the multitude, the parable of the steward given in answer to the apostles, an old allegory recited by Peter in the midst of an uproar, and the narrator’s summary of the names and ways by which Jesus revealed God. What stands out is that, just as the accusations grew darker, the message Jesus pressed hardest was not judgment but the Father’s searching love. (169:1.1, 169:4.1)

I. Parable of the Lost Son — The Father Who Seeks First

On Thursday afternoon Jesus preached on the “Grace of Salvation.” He retold the stories of the lost sheep and the lost coin and then added his favorite, the parable of the prodigal son. He granted that the prophets had always urged men to seek God, but he added something new to their counsel. (169:1.1, 169:1.2)

But I have come to show you that, while you are seeking to find God, God is likewise seeking to find you. (169:1.2)
The fact that souls are lost only increases the interest of the heavenly Father. (169:1.2)

People had been taught that divine acceptance follows repentance and comes as the fruit of sacrifice and penitence. Jesus reversed the order. The Father accepts us even before we repent, and he sends the Son and his associates to find the lost and bring them home. This declaration—that grace is not the reward for merit but the starting point—still brings deep relief to readers today. (169:1.3)

but I assure you that the Father accepts you even before you have repented (169:1.3)

The two sons in the parable have opposite temperaments. The younger is cheerful but shirks responsibility; the elder is hard-working but self-centered and conceited. The younger squanders his inheritance, ends up feeding swine, comes to himself, and turns for home. His father, watching for him, sees him while he is still far off and runs out to meet him, and the son never gets the chance to finish his confession. (169:1.6, 169:1.8, 169:1.9)

for this my son was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found. (169:1.10)

The story then turns to the elder brother’s anger. For years, he protests, he has never transgressed a single command, and yet he was never given so much as a kid to make merry with his friends. Because the father loves both sons, he goes out to reason with the elder and reminds him that all he has is already his. The narrator explains why Jesus liked to tell the three stories together. (169:1.12, 169:1.13, 169:1.15)

Source 169:1.15

The elder brother mirrors a trap that long and faithful service can set. He stayed at his father’s side, yet he did not share his father’s joy. Once service becomes a matter of reckoning rewards, a feast for a returning brother looks like injustice. The father does not scold him; he calls him “my son” and invites him into the rejoicing. (169:1.12, 169:1.13)

Jesus told this story of the prodigal son many, many times over the years of his teaching. The narrator tells us that this parable and the story of the good Samaritan were his favorite ways of teaching the love of the Father and the neighborliness of man. (169:1.16)

II. Parable of the Shrewd Steward — Foresight for Eternity

One evening Simon Zelotes asked what Jesus had meant by saying that the children of the world are wiser in their generation than the children of the kingdom. Jesus reminded the apostles that some of them had been very shrewd in business before they entered the kingdom. If they had been so diligent in making gains for themselves, why should they be less diligent in gaining souls, now that they were stewards of God? (169:2.1, 169:2.2)

The steward of the parable had oppressed his master’s clients and squandered his master’s funds, and he was about to be dismissed. So he reduced the bonds of his master’s debtors, making friends who would welcome him once he was put out. Even his master had to admit that he had shown sagacity in providing for the future. What Jesus commended was not the dishonesty but the farseeing diligence. (169:2.3, 169:2.4)

Source 169:2.4

I affirm that he who is faithful in little will also be faithful in much, while he who is unrighteous in little will also be unrighteous in much. (169:2.6)

The parable neither praises nor condemns wealth as such. Jesus’ question is how we handle what has been entrusted to us. If we have shown no foresight and integrity in the affairs of this world, how can we be faithful when entrusted with the true riches of the heavenly kingdom? The question applies just as directly to our workplaces and households today. (169:2.6)

Jesus urged his hearers to live so as to make eternal friendship with the forces of righteousness, and he closed with the saying that no one can serve God and mammon. The Pharisees, much given to acquiring riches, sneered and tried to draw him into argument, but he refused to debate with his enemies and withdrew to his tent. His refusal to be drawn into unprofitable dispute is itself a lesson. (169:2.5, 169:2.7, 169:2.8)

III. The Rich Man and the Beggar — An Old Parable Retold by Peter

When the meeting grew too noisy, Simon Peter stood up and took charge. What the Master had said, he told them, was no new doctrine, and he recited the old allegory of the Nazarites that John the Baptist used to thunder at those who loved riches. Peter made it clear, however, that this parable was not according to the gospel they preached. (169:3.1)

In the story a rich man named Dives lives in splendor every day, while at his gate lies Lazarus, a beggar covered with sores. After death their positions are reversed, and when Dives begs that someone be sent to warn his five brothers, the request is refused. (169:3.2)

The allegory served as a warning against a life that enjoys wealth while failing to see the suffering at its own door. Peter said the people would do well to heed its lessons until they came to comprehend the new light of the kingdom of heaven. The story was offered, in other words, not as a substitute for the gospel but as a stepping-stone for those who had not yet reached its new light. (169:3.1)

If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded even if one were to rise from the dead. (169:3.2)

Jesus’ own attitude is worth noticing. The apostles and disciples often asked him about this parable, but he never consented to comment on it. The narrator sets the story apart as an ancient parable of the Nazarite brotherhood, not as Jesus’ own teaching. Readers do well to keep this distinction in mind and to take from it, with care, only its warning about riches. (169:3.3)

IV. The Father and His Kingdom — Not a King but a Father

The apostles proclaimed the kingdom of God, yet they could hardly grasp that the Father in heaven was not a king. People then knew of kings and emperors as rulers, and the Jews had long awaited a coming kingdom. So the Master thought it best to call the spiritual brotherhood of man the kingdom of heaven and its spirit head the Father in heaven. Never once did he refer to his Father as a king. (169:4.1, 169:4.6)

Jesus gave no systematic lessons about the Father, nor did he offer arguments to prove the Father’s reality. His teaching centered on the declaration that he and the Father are one. The way to learn of God is not to analyze his words but to observe the divinity of his life. (169:4.2, 169:4.3)

You can know God, not by understanding what Jesus said, but by knowing what Jesus was. (169:4.4)

Except when quoting the Hebrew scriptures, Jesus referred to Deity by only two names: God and Father. Instead of Yahweh, which stood for the tribal God of the Jews, he used the Hebrew word signifying the plural God, and he exalted the deity of a race into the Father of all men. He taught, moreover, that the God of universes and the Father of all men are one and the same Paradise Deity. (169:4.5, 169:4.8)

Jesus never claimed to be the manifestation of Elohim in the flesh. He proclaimed himself instead the revelation of the Father in the flesh. In fact, he taught only two things about God: that God in himself is spirit, and that in all his relations with his creatures he is a Father. (169:4.9, 169:4.11)

To hear Jesus’ teaching is not equivalent to knowing God, but to see Jesus is an experience which in itself is a revelation of the Father to the soul. (169:4.12)

The paper likens Jesus to a spiritual lens in human likeness that makes the invisible One visible to material creatures. Yet all of this must take place in the personal experience of the individual believer, for God who is spirit can be known only as a spiritual experience. (169:4.13)

The God of universes rules the far-flung creation, but it is the Father in heaven who sends forth his spirit to dwell within your minds. (169:4.12)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 169 begins with people seeking God and moves to the Father who seeks people first. The parable of the prodigal son teaches the order of grace; the parable of the steward teaches diligence for eternity; and the final section teaches that the way to know God lies less in doctrine than in the experience of beholding the life of Jesus. (169:1.3, 169:4.4)
