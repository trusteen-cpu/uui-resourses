Introduction — The State Is an Evolved Institution

Paper 71 rounds off the series on human institutions by asking how the state arose and where it should be heading. Sponsored by a Melchizedek of Nebadon, it does not stop at recording the past; it looks ahead, setting out the stages of representative government and the marks of the ideal state. (71:0.1, 71:8.16)

The starting point is a sober definition. The state is society’s net gain from the ravages and sufferings of war; it was neither divinely founded nor deliberately designed by anyone. Along with it arose the moral myth of the citizen’s absolute obligation to live and die for the state. The paper can be read as a call not to idolize the state but to use it rightly as a tool. (71:0.1, 71:0.2)

But the state is not of divine genesis; (71:0.2)

I. The Embryonic State — From Blood Ties to Territory

The state is a territorial social regulative organization, and it is strongest and most enduring when made up of a single nation sharing a common language, mores, and institutions. The early states were small and all the result of conquest, so they were stratified from the start. The embryonic state became possible when the blood bond gave way to the territorial bond. (71:1.1, 71:1.2, 71:1.23)

The author contrasts two cases. The Iroquois federation of North America had good government but lacked certain essentials of national life and never quite functioned as a state. The text says they were too democratic, and that they would eventually have evolved a state had they not prematurely encountered a more advanced civilization. Rome, by contrast, built a successful state on the foundations listed below. (71:1.3, 71:1.12, 71:1.13)

Rome too collapsed in the end. The author points to one factor: the sudden and extensive manner in which supposedly liberal reforms were adopted. The harm lay not in the reforms themselves but in their speed, and Rome shows what happens when internal degeneration is joined to too rapid expansion. When states fail to integrate, they may slip back into prestate conditions, as in the feudalism of the European Middle Ages. (71:1.22, 71:1.24)

II. The Evolution of Representative Government — Ten Steps

Democracy is an ideal, but it is a product of civilization, not of evolution. So the author urges, “Go slowly! select carefully!” and names its dangers: the glorification of mediocrity, the choice of base and ignorant rulers, universal suffrage in the hands of uneducated majorities, and slavery to public opinion. The majority is not always right. (71:2.1, 71:2.6)

Public opinion slows society, yet it is valuable because it preserves civilization. The answer, then, is not to ignore public opinion but to educate it. The advance of society is measured by how far public opinion can control personal behavior and state regulation without violence, and cultural growth accelerates as bullets give way to ballots. (71:2.7, 71:2.8)

Education of public opinion is the only safe and true method of accelerating civilization; (71:2.7)
Popular elections may not always decide things rightly, but they represent the right way even to do a wrong thing. (71:2.8)

III. The Ideals of Statehood — What It Does, Not What It Is

The form of government matters little so long as it provides liberty, security, education, and social co-ordination. A state is judged not by what it is but by what it does. And no state can rise above the moral values of its citizens. If we want a good state, good citizens must come first: this is where the section begins. (71:3.1)

no state can transcend the moral values of its citizenry as exemplified in their chosen leaders. (71:3.1)

The chosen people doctrine and national egotism have helped build nations, but no state can reach ideal levels until every form of intolerance is mastered. Intolerance is best combated by the co-ordination of science, commerce, play, and religion. The ideal state moves under three drives: love loyalty born of human brotherhood, intelligent patriotism based on wise ideals, and cosmic insight interpreted in terms of planetary facts. (71:3.2, 71:3.3, 71:3.6)

The laws of the ideal state are few, and instead of taboo they foster the positive liberty that comes from self-control. Leisure must produce as well as consume, and political service is esteemed as the highest devotion of the citizenry. The author sums it up: the best state governs least and co-ordinates most. (71:3.7, 71:3.9, 71:3.12)

That state is best which co-ordinates most while governing least. (71:3.9)

The ideals of statehood come by evolution, not revolution—by the slow growth of civic consciousness. At first people take up the burdens of government reluctantly, as a duty; later they seek such service as a privilege and the greatest honor. The author says the status of any civilization is faithfully portrayed by the caliber of the citizens who volunteer for the responsibilities of statehood. (71:3.10)

IV. Progressive Civilization — Brotherhood and God-Consciousness

On an evolutionary world, standing still means decay. The author lists the program of an expanding civilization: preserving individual liberty, protecting the home, economic security, preventing disease, compulsory education and employment, profitable use of leisure, care of the unfortunate, science and art, philosophy, and finally cosmic insight—spirituality. The list begins with material things and ends with spirit. (71:4.1, 71:4.2, 71:4.14)

Static conditions on an evolutionary world are indicative of decay; (71:4.1)

All this progress leads to two goals: socially, the brotherhood of man, and personally, God-consciousness, revealed in the supreme desire to do the will of the Father in heaven. Genuine brotherhood means a social order in which all delight in bearing one another’s burdens and truly desire to practice the golden rule. (71:4.15, 71:4.16)

But the author warns against naive idealism. So long as some would exploit peaceful dispositions, the “golden rulers” must live by their ideals while keeping an adequate defense. The great test of idealism is to resist the temptation to use that military strength for aggression. (71:4.16, 71:4.17)

Only love, brotherhood, can prevent the strong from oppressing the weak. (71:4.17)

V–VI. The Evolution of Competition; The Profit Motive — Toward Co-operation and Service

Competition is essential to social progress and is gradually displacing war, but unregulated it breeds violence. In early evolution competition stimulates, but in advanced civilizations co-operation is more efficient than competition. Even so, no attempt to eliminate the waste of competition should be allowed if it abrogates even the slightest of the individual’s basic liberties. (71:5.1, 71:5.3, 71:5.4)

The verdict on the profit motive has the same balance. An economy driven only by selfish profit is doomed unless profit motives are augmented by service motives, and it is incompatible with the teachings of Jesus. Yet the profit motive must not be suddenly removed before higher motives are firmly in place. (71:6.1, 71:6.2, 71:6.3)

VII–VIII. Education; The Character of Statehood — From the Cradle to the Grave

The enduring state is founded on culture, dominated by ideals, and motivated by service. Education must move from being localistic and success seeking to being world-wide and cosmic grasping, and it must be the business of a whole lifetime. The author sets out seven ascending levels of wisdom that education should lead toward, and says that through them many will rise to God-consciousness. (71:7.1, 71:7.3, 71:7.5, 71:7.13)

In the ideal state philosophy will one day be the chief pursuit of its citizens. Control of education has passed from the clergy to lawyers and businessmen, but it must finally be given over to the philosophers and scientists, and teachers must be free beings and real leaders. (71:7.2, 71:7.4)

The purpose of education should be acquirement of skill, pursuit of wisdom, realization of selfhood, and attainment of spiritual values. (71:7.1)

The final section names the only sacred feature of any human government: the division into executive, legislative, and judicial domains, the plan by which the universe itself is administered. On that basis the author lists twelve marks of progressive government, including the abolition of slavery, universal education, recognition of sex equality, a universal language, the ending of war through continental courts and a supreme planetary tribunal, and the evolution of a world religion presaging light and life. (71:8.1, 71:8.10, 71:8.13, 71:8.14)

Urantia is far from the realization of these exalted ideals, but the civilized races have made a beginning—mankind is on the march toward higher evolutionary destinies. (71:8.15)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 71 neither sanctifies nor despises the state. The state is an evolved tool, and its quality finally depends on the intelligence, morals, and spirit of service of its citizens. So the paper’s last message is addressed to people more than to politics. The ideal state draws nearer as we educate public opinion, keep learning all our lives, and enlarge profit into service, competition into co-operation, and patriotism into brotherhood.

The series on human institutions that began in Paper 68 here gathers into a single vision: the long road on which bonds born of fear grow into brotherhood, and law born of taboo grows into the liberty of self-control. Paper 72 then makes the vision concrete by describing the government of a neighboring planet.
