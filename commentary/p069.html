Introduction — Institutions Are Accumulated Mores

Paper 68 asked why society arose; Paper 69 looks at the institutions that society built. The author, a Melchizedek of Nebadon, notes what sets man apart from his animal ancestors: emotionally, the ability to appreciate humor, art, and religion; socially, the fact that he is a toolmaker, a communicator, and an institution builder. (69:0.1, 69:9.19)

Institutions grow naturally out of long-lasting social groups, and most of them have saved labor while adding to group security. Yet the author cautions against too much pride in them. Every institution is, in the end, the accumulated mores of the past, a legacy preserved by taboo and dignified by religion. (69:0.2, 69:0.3)

all human institutions are merely the accumulated mores of the past as they have been conserved by taboos and dignified by religion. (69:0.3)

I. Basic Human Institutions — Three Classes

Every institution serves some social need, but when overdeveloped it overshadows personality, diminishes initiative, and lowers the worth of the individual. So people must govern their institutions rather than be governed by them. The author sorts institutions into three classes according to the urges from which they spring. (69:1.1, 69:1.2)

Man should control his institutions rather than permit himself to be dominated by these creations of advancing civilization. (69:1.1)

The three classes are closely interwoven and work as a single social mechanism. Notably, the author remarks that civilization has never evolved distinctive institutions of self-gratification. The distinction between self-maintenance and self-gratification from the previous paper carries on here. (69:1.5, 69:1.6)

II–III. The Dawn of Industry; The Specialization of Labor

Primitive industry grew slowly as insurance against the terrors of famine, and man learned from animals that store food against lean days. Poverty is man’s natural estate; wealth comes from labor, knowledge, and organization. Association led to organization, and the first result of organization was the division of labor, saving both time and materials. (69:2.1, 69:2.2, 69:2.3)

Wealth is not a natural gift; it results from labor, knowledge, and organization. (69:2.2)

What drove the naturally inactive early races to work was the double demand of the struggle for existence and rising standards of living. Labor—effort by design—distinguishes man from the beast, and the author reminds us that the Prince’s staff worked and that Adam was a gardener. After a long struggle between the devotees of magic and those who exercised foresight, magic slowly gave way to self-denial and industry. (69:2.4, 69:2.5, 69:2.7)

The necessity for labor is man’s paramount blessing. (69:2.5)

The story of the smiths is intriguing. Their skill with metals won them both fear and privilege, but they abused their privileges, became universally hated, and lost out to the medicine men, who drove them from the villages. The author calls this the first contest between science and religion—and superstition won. Driven out, the smiths kept the first inns on the outskirts of the settlements. (69:3.5, 69:3.6)

IV–V. The Beginnings of Trade and Capital — The Great Civilizer

Barter followed seizure by raids. At first it was silent barter, armed traders leaving their goods at a neutral spot, and women held the first markets. These market places became the first sanctuaries, later known in some lands as “cities of refuge.” Modern writing itself grew out of trade records; man’s first literature was a salt advertisement. (69:4.1, 69:4.2, 69:4.4, 69:4.6)

Commerce has been the great civilizer through promoting the cross-fertilization of culture. (69:4.8)

Capital is labor applied as a renunciation of the present in favor of the future. Hoarding food developed self-control and also created the first problems of capital and labor. The author lists eight urges that led to the accumulation of capital, and the list reveals both the bright and the dark side of human motives. (69:5.1, 69:5.3)

The author gives a balanced verdict on capital. Its abuse by unfair capitalists does not alter the fact that it is the basis of modern industrial society, and through capital and invention the present generation enjoys more freedom than any before it. But he adds clearly that this is a statement of fact, not a justification of capital’s misuse. (69:5.15)

Man’s technique varies, but his disposition remains quite unchanged. (69:5.13)

VI–VII. Fire and Animals — What Set Man Apart from the Beasts

Primitive society rose through four instruments: fire, animals, slaves, and property. Of these, fire building by a single bound separated man from animal forever. Fire let man stay on the ground at night, encouraged evening fellowship, and, through cooking, reduced the energy spent on digestion and left strength for culture. (69:6.1, 69:6.2, 69:6.7)

The loveliest meaning of fire lies in sharing. Because one could give live coals to a neighbor without losing one’s own, fire became man’s first means of being altruistic without loss. The early home was not a building but the family gathered about the fire. Andon, the discoverer of fire, did not worship it, but many of his descendants made it a fetish, and that superstition lasted long. (69:6.3, 69:6.4)

The early home was not a building but the family gathered about the fire, the family hearth. (69:6.3)

Animals were at first man’s enemies, and domestication began by accident when hunters surrounded and penned herds. The dog was the first domesticated animal, and watchdogs first made it possible for a whole clan to sleep at night. But when domestication coincided with the Caligastia confusion, many tribes treated women much as they treated their animals, and the author records this as one of the darkest chapters of human history. (69:7.1, 69:7.4, 69:7.5)

VIII. Slavery as a Factor in Civilization — A Bridge and a Malady

This section treats an uncomfortable subject from a historical viewpoint. Early captives were eaten, tortured, or sacrificed, so the text judges enslavement to have been an advance over massacre and cannibalism. Slavery compelled the invention of regulative machinery and so gave rise to the beginnings of government; it was the bridge over which society passed from chaos to order. (69:8.2, 69:8.6, 69:8.7)

Yet the author does not glorify slavery. It was oppressive, and it soon attacks society from within as the gravest of all destructive social maladies. Machinery is making the slave obsolete, but his remark that thousands today let ambition enslave them to debt applies just as well to modern readers. (69:8.8, 69:8.9, 69:8.10)

IX. Private Property — From Common Holding to Ownership

Primitive society was virtually communal—not as a theory, but as a practical adjustment that prevented pauperism. Yet this primitive communism put a premium on idleness, stifled industry, and destroyed ambition, and it gave way to a higher order because it ran counter to four strong human proclivities. (69:9.1, 69:9.2)

Source 69:9.2
Proclivity opposed to primitive communismWhat it meant
The familyThe wish to leave property to one’s children; in communal society the inheritance tax was one hundred per cent
Religious tendenciesThe wish to save property for the next existence; the origin of burying belongings with the dead
The desire for liberty and leisureResistance to a system in which the worker was made slave to the idler
The urge for security and powerSecret hoarding and evasions by successful individuals

Private property began with things personally touched, sleeping space, fire sites, and wells, and with agriculture it expanded to land. Property was respected because it was believed to carry part of the owner’s personality. Along with property the home changed shape, and polygamy was gradually displaced by monogamy, which the author calls the slave-free ideal of the matchless association of one man and one woman. He also notes that the teachers of revealed religion were the first to proclaim that the poor could have salvation on equal terms with the rich. (69:9.4, 69:9.7, 69:9.9, 69:9.14)

The right to property is not absolute; it is purely social. (69:9.17)

The paper’s last counsel concerns our attitude toward change. The present social order is not sacred, but when we change it we must make certain we change for the better, and we must not go back to formulas our forefathers discarded. (69:9.18)

Go forward, not backward! Let evolution proceed! Do not take a backward step. (69:9.18)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 69 shows that the workplaces and markets, savings and possessions, and family hearths we take for granted were all won through long trial and error. Deep shadows lay along the way: superstition, exploitation, and cruelty toward women and captives. The author records the light and the shadow together and urges us to govern our institutions rather than serve them, and to move forward without haste.

Paper 70 traces how human government evolved on the foundation of these institutions. Keeping in mind how slavery and property were bound up with the beginnings of government in this paper will make the next one easier to follow.
