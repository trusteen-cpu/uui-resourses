Introduction — The First Tour Without Restraint

The first public preaching tour of Galilee began on Sunday, January 18, A.D. 28, and lasted about two months, ending with the return to Capernaum on March 17. Jesus and the twelve, assisted by the former apostles of John, tarried, taught, and baptized believers in eleven cities. (146:0.1)

This was the first time Jesus permitted his associates to preach without restraint. He cautioned them only to stay away from Nazareth and to be discreet when passing through Capernaum and Tiberias, and the apostles threw themselves with great joy into preaching, ministering to the sick, and baptizing. Yet throughout, the paper also shows the steady tension between crowds expecting miracles and a Master wary of them. (146:0.2)

I. Preaching at Rimmon — Old Beliefs and the New Gospel

Rimmon had once been dedicated to Ramman, a Babylonian god of the air, and its people still held many Babylonian and Zoroastrian teachings. Jesus and the twenty-four therefore spent much time making plain the difference between these older beliefs and the gospel. Many became believers, but the narrator notes how hard it is to bring nature worshipers into full fellowship with a spiritual ideal within a single lifetime. (146:1.1, 146:1.2)

The narrator explains that the better Babylonian and Persian ideas of light and darkness, good and evil, were later taken into Christian doctrine and made it more acceptable to the peoples of the Near East, just as Plato’s theories, adapted by Philo, eased the way for the western Greeks. Todan, who first heard the gospel at Rimmon, became one of the first to preach it beyond the Euphrates. (146:1.3, 146:1.4)

II. At Jotapata — Sixteen Teachings on Prayer

On the second evening at Jotapata, Nathaniel, confused about the Master’s teaching on prayer, thanksgiving, and worship, asked for help, and Jesus explained at great length. The narrator summarizes the discourse in modern terms as sixteen points. Grouped by theme, their core runs as follows. (146:2.1)

The time, manner, and degree of the answer are determined by the spiritual wisdom and universe consistency of the petition. A wise father does not literally grant the foolish requests of inexperienced children, though the children may find real soul satisfaction in making them. To be sure of being heard, then, is not the same as being sure of getting what we ask. (146:2.6)

Prayer does not change the divine attitude toward man, but it does change man’s attitude toward the changeless Father. (146:2.8)

Jesus taught that a thoroughly selfish soul cannot truly pray, and that prayer is no means of escaping the delays of time or the handicaps of space. Nor should we expect prayer for the sick to take the place of loving and intelligent ministry to their needs. Prayer does not replace action; it changes the one who acts. (146:2.9, 146:2.11)

There is but one form of prayer which is appropriate for all God’s children, and that is: “Nevertheless, your will be done.” (146:2.12)

Jesus warned that ornate repetitions, fasting, penance, or sacrifices do not make prayer more effective, and he deplored how little of the spirit of thanksgiving was found in his followers’ prayers. He urged them to use prayer as a way of leading up through thanksgiving to true worship. (146:2.15)

Worship is a transforming experience whereby the finite gradually approaches and ultimately attains the presence of the Infinite. (146:2.17)

III. The Stop at Ramah — The Philosopher and the Way of Assurance

At Ramah Jesus listened with patience and sympathy to an aged Greek philosopher who held that science and philosophy were enough to satisfy human experience, and he granted the truth of much that the man said. Then he pointed out that the discussion had left out “whence, why, and whither,” and that religion begins where philosophy leaves off. Sincerely honest of heart, the old philosopher soon believed the gospel. (146:3.1)

Where you leave off, we begin. (146:3.1)

When the apostles were disconcerted at seeing Jesus openly agree with the philosopher, he explained that truth does not fear honest criticism. (146:3.2)

intolerance is the mask covering up the entertainment of secret doubts as to the trueness of one’s belief (146:3.2)

Thomas asked how a new believer could really be certain of the truth of the gospel. Jesus answered that assurance is wholly a matter of personal experience and described it almost as a sum. He also gave a practical measure of how far one has yielded the soul to the indwelling spirit. (146:3.4, 146:3.6)

Since Jewish teaching had been confused about the survival of the righteous, these definite words about the eternal survival of believers were a great comfort. At this time each of Jesus’ apostles took one of John’s apostles as an associate. At Zebulun Jesus discussed the difference between personal religious experience and social religious obligations, one of the few times he did so, for he gave very little instruction about the socialization of religion. (146:3.8, 146:3.9, 146:3.10)

IV. The Gospel at Iron — Among the Miners, and the Leper

At this time every synagogue in Galilee and Judea was still open to Jesus. In the mining town of Iron, since he had never shared the life of a miner, he spent most of his time working underground with the laborers. He helped many of the sick, but he performed no so-called miracle of healing except for the leper. (146:4.1, 146:4.2)

On the third day a leper knelt before him, believing he could not enter the kingdom unless he were made clean. Jesus’ human heart was touched, and the divine mind was moved with compassion. (146:4.3)

I will—be clean. (146:4.3)

The narrator states that this was the first so-called miracle Jesus had deliberately performed, and that it was a case of real leprosy. But the man ignored the charge to tell no one and spread the news through the town, so that Jesus, thronged by the sick, had to leave early the next day. In Chorazin and Madon that followed, the message was largely rejected, and the apostles, blaming Jesus’ refusal to let them present him as a healer, longed for another display of power. The Master was unmoved. (146:4.4, 146:4.5, 146:4.6)

V–VI. Back in Cana; Nain and the Widow’s Son — A Wonder-Seeking Generation

At Cana, Titus, a prominent citizen of Capernaum, begged Jesus to heal his dying son. Jesus lamented that except they see signs and wonders, people refuse to believe, then said, “Your son will live.” The fever left the boy at that very hour, and Titus and his whole household believed. Yet the narrator explains that this was no miracle of curing physical disease, but a case of preknowledge of the course of natural law. (146:5.2)

At the gate of Nain Jesus met the funeral procession of a widow’s only son. Lifting the cover of the bier, he found that the young man was not really dead; he told the mother that her son was sleeping and then awakened him. Jesus tried again and again to explain that he had not raised the dead, but the people took his words as the modest way he always hid his miracles. (146:6.2, 146:6.3)

Weep not. Your son is not dead; he sleeps. He will be restored to you. (146:6.2)

The narrator sums it up: these people believed in signs; they were a wonder-seeking generation. Jesus could not make even all his apostles fully understand that the boy had not been dead, and whenever a rumor of miracles spread, he had to leave quickly. The section quietly warns that a heart hungry for the dramatic may close the ears that should be hearing the truth. (146:6.1, 146:6.4)

VII. At Endor — Teaching About Spirits

At Endor, escaping the crowds for a few days, Jesus told the story of King Saul and the witch of Endor. He explained that the supposed spirits of the dead had been impersonated by stray and rebellious midwayers, and that after he returned to the Father and the spirit was poured out on all flesh, these so-called unclean spirits could no longer possess mortals. He also taught that the spirits of departed human beings do not come back to communicate with the living. After two days’ rest, Jesus proposed returning to Capernaum. (146:7.1, 146:7.2, 146:7.3)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 146 holds together a widening ministry and a deepening teaching. The discourse at Jotapata shows that prayer is not a means of moving God but a process that brings us into line with the Father’s will; the conversation at Ramah shows that assurance is confirmed through experience and love. And amid a generation chasing wonders, Jesus kept truth ahead of signs to the end. (146:2.8, 146:3.6)
