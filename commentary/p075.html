Introduction — A Story of Impatience, Not Malice

Paper 75 tells the tragedy of Eden. After more than a hundred years of effort Adam could see very little progress outside the Garden, and the situation seemed so desperate as to call for some relief not embraced in the original plans. The text first makes clear that Adam and his mate were loyal. They were isolated from their kind and sorely distressed by the sorry plight of their world. (75:0.1)

The narrator of this paper is Solonia, the very seraphim who reproved Adam and Eve in the Garden that night. So the account reads not as a verdict pronounced from afar but as the testimony of one who stood close by. What the paper stresses again and again is not evil intent but what happens when good intentions meet wrong methods. (75:4.2, 75:8.8)

I. The Urantia Problem — Adam on the Hardest of Worlds

On a normal planet the first work of Adam and Eve would be the co-ordination and blending of the races. On Urantia that project seemed just about hopeless, for the races had never been purged of their retarded and defective strains. They could not take counsel with their superiors, and the early departure of the Melchizedek receivers deepened their loneliness. (75:1.1, 75:1.2, 75:1.4)

Slowly their courage weakened, their spirits drooped, and sometimes their faith almost faltered. (75:1.4)

The narrator states plainly that they would have sometime met with success had they been more farseeing and patient. But both of them, especially Eve, were altogether too impatient and unwilling to settle down to the long endurance test. They wanted immediate results, and they got them—but those results proved most disastrous both to themselves and to their world. (75:1.6)

II–III. Caligastia’s Plot; The Temptation of Eve

Caligastia often visited the Garden and proposed compromises, but Adam and Eve, having seen enough of the results of rebellion, were adamant. He had no power to influence anyone against his will. So he gave up on Adam and tried a flank attack on Eve, using as his channel suitable persons from the upper strata of the Nodites, descendants of his onetime staff. (75:2.1, 75:2.3)

Two things should be remembered here. Though misguided, Caligastia was still the titular Planetary Prince of Urantia, a high Son of the local universe, and was not finally deposed until the times of Christ Michael on Urantia. And from the early days of Eden the Garden dwellers had received much valuable help and co-operation from the Nodites. The danger came not from an enemy but from within a trusted partnership. (75:2.2, 75:2.5)

Before leaving, the Melchizedeks had especially warned Eve never to stray from the side of her mate—to attempt no personal or secret methods of furthering their common work. Eve kept these instructions scrupulously for more than a hundred years. Yet it did not occur to her that any danger could attach to her increasingly private and confidential visits with a Nodite leader named Serapatatia. (75:2.4)

The whole affair developed so gradually and naturally that she was taken unawares. (75:2.4)

Serapatatia was no villain. Impressed by the righteousness of Adam’s cause, he brought his people into affiliation with the work of the Garden and became one of Adam’s ablest lieutenants. The text twice insists that he was entirely honest and sincere and never knew he was being used as a tool of Caligastia. But he too was impatient; he wanted to see results within his own lifetime. (75:3.2, 75:3.3, 75:3.6)

IV. The Realization of Default — Commingling Good and Evil

The celestial life of the planet was astir, and Adam called Eve aside and heard for the first time the whole story: a plan to speed world improvement in two directions at once, pursuing the divine plan alongside Serapatatia’s enterprise. Then “the voice in the Garden”—the narrator herself—announced that they had transgressed the Garden covenant, disobeyed the Melchizedeks, and defaulted on their oaths of trust to the sovereign of the universe. (75:4.1, 75:4.2)

Source 75:4.3

This definition is the heart of the paper. Eve did not act from rebellious intent. Cano assured her that men and women with good motives could do no evil, but the text overturns exactly that idea. However high the motive, an attempt to reach righteous ends by the wrong way, departing from the divine plan, is evil. (75:4.5, 75:4.6)

it constituted evil because it represented the wrong way to achieve righteous ends, because it departed from the right way, the divine plan. (75:4.6)

That night the narrator spoke with the two and gave them counsel; some of it they followed and some they disregarded. This conference survives in the records as the Lord God calling to Adam and Eve in the Garden, “Where are you?” Later generations tended to attribute everything extraordinary to the direct intervention of the Gods. (75:4.8)

V–VI. Repercussions of Default; Adam and Eve Leave the Garden

Heartbroken as he was, Adam held only pity and sympathy for Eve. The next day he sought out Laotta, a Nodite woman, and committed the same folly. The text makes clear that he was not beguiled but deliberately chose to share Eve’s fate; he could not bear the thought of a lonely vigil on Urantia without her. (75:5.1, 75:5.2)

he deliberately chose to share the fate of Eve. (75:5.2)

The consequences followed one upon another. The infuriated Garden dwellers destroyed the nearby Nodite settlement, and Serapatatia, overcome with remorse, drowned himself. Adam wandered alone for thirty days while Eve and the children knew nothing of his fate. Only when the Melchizedek receivers returned seventy days after the default did Adam know for certain that they had failed. A long and bitter war with the Nodites began at this time. (75:5.3, 75:5.4, 75:5.5, 75:5.8, 75:5.9)

The consequences of the follies of misguided parents are so often shared by their innocent children. (75:5.6)

Those thirty days, the text says, were as long years of suffering to Eve. She did not know whether her mate had destroyed himself in sorrow or been removed from the world in retribution, and no later hardship ever compared with those nights of loneliness. So when Adam returned, her joy and gratitude were never effaced through all their long partnership. Even after the misstep, love was not broken. (75:5.7)

As the Nodite host advanced, Adam, who had no liking for war, left the first garden unopposed with some twelve hundred loyal followers. On the third day the seraphic transports arrived, and the children of the age of choice were given the option of staying or going to Edentia. Two thirds chose Edentia, and all the younger children were taken there as well. Before finding a new home, the pair had lost more than three fourths of their children. (75:6.2, 75:6.3, 75:6.4)

VII. Degradation of Adam and Eve — Default, Not Rebellion

Gabriel appeared to pronounce judgment: the Planetary Adam and Eve were adjudged in default, having violated the covenant of their trusteeship. They were reduced to the status of mortals and must henceforth look to the future of the world races for their own future as man and woman of Urantia. Yet the judges on Salvington absolved them of all charges of “contempt of the universe government.” (75:7.1, 75:7.2, 75:7.3)

VIII. The So-Called Fall of Man — Perfection Is the Goal, Not the Origin

The final section corrects an old theological notion. Adam and Eve did fall from the high estate of material sonship to the status of mortal man, but that was not the fall of man. The history of the human race is one of progressive evolution, and the Adamic bestowal left the world’s peoples biologically far better off than before. The superior stocks of Urantia now carry inheritance factors from four sources: Andonite, Sangik, Nodite, and Adamic. (75:8.1, 75:8.2)

There has been no “fall of man.” (75:8.2)

Adam, the text says, should not be regarded as the cause of a curse on the human race; justice demands recognition that he faced a well-nigh hopeless task. And from this story it draws a lesson for every ascender: nothing is gained by impatiently trying to circumvent the divine plan through short cuts or personal inventions. (75:8.3, 75:8.4, 75:8.5)

Never, in all your ascent to Paradise, will you gain anything by impatiently attempting to circumvent the established and divine plan by short cuts, personal inventions, or other devices (75:8.5)
Perfection is our eternal goal, not our origin. (75:8.6)

The narrator even rejoices that mistakes and disagreements are possible, because they show that this universe is dominated not by mechanism but by personality. Because the universe is personal and progressive, failure can happen—and for the same reason we can be confident of growth, experience, and adventure. (75:8.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 75 is a record of failure, but not of condemnation. In isolation, loneliness, and discouragement, good people took a short cut for good ends, and the result wounded everyone. Yet the text says that humanity was not fallen but uplifted, and that ours is a personal universe able to hold even mistakes. The paper warns us against impatience while teaching us to look on those who fail with compassion. (75:1.6, 75:8.1, 75:8.7)

Paper 76 tells of the second garden that Adam and Eve founded in the Euphrates valley after leaving Eden, and of the rest of their lives as mortals. Reading of the service they continued after their failure shows how the tragedy of this paper leads on into a story of recovery.
