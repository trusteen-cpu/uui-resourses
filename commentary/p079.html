Introduction — Asia, the Homeland of Humankind

Paper 79 carries the Andite story of Paper 78 eastward. It is presented by an Archangel of Nebadon. Asia was where Andon and Fonta were born, where the Sangik peoples branched off, and where the civilizations from Dalamatia to the Andites arose. This paper compares the roads taken by three parts of that continent: central Asia, India, and China. (79:0.1)

Asia is the homeland of the human race. (79:0.1)

This paper often assesses racial mixture and relative superiority. The commentary presents those statements only within the text’s own scope and does not use them as grounds for judging any people today. Indeed, the conclusion the paper keeps returning to is that the higher power deciding a civilization’s destiny is true religion and the pursuit of truth.

I. The Andites of Turkestan — The Rise of Traders and Cities

For over twenty-five thousand years the heart of Eurasia was predominantly Andite. From the lowlands of Turkestan they turned west into Europe; from the highlands they moved through Sinkiang and Tibet into China, and through the Punjab and Baluchistan into India. These early movements were no conquests but a steady drifting. For almost fifteen thousand years the Tarim basin was the easternmost outpost of Andite culture, rivaling Mesopotamia itself, until the rain winds shifted and it perished. (79:1.1, 79:1.2)

As central Asia dried out around 8000 B.C., the Andites were driven to the valleys of the Nile, Euphrates, Indus, and Yellow rivers. Instead of turning to herding like the older races, they built cities devoted to manufacture and trade, and Adonia, near present-day Ashkhabad, became the commercial metropolis of central Asia. Environmental pressure produced not regression but a new way of life. (79:1.3, 79:1.4)

A new class of men, the traders, began to appear in large numbers. (79:1.3)

Drought and the southward push of the Andonites diluted the Andites of central Asia nearly to the vanishing point, yet their traces endured. The text sees the Mongols under Genghis Khan in A.D. 1200 as the last manifestation of their military genius, adding that the early breakup of that empire delayed contact between East and West and hindered the growth of monotheism in Asia. (79:1.7, 79:1.9)

these warriors proclaimed the existence of “one God in heaven.” (79:1.9)

II–III. The Andite Conquest of India; Dravidian India

India is the only place where all the Urantia races were blended. From about 15,000 B.C., for more than fifteen centuries, Andites poured in through Baluchistan and spread over the Indus and Ganges valleys. The text finds the main reason India did not lead Eurasia in its topography: people were crowded south into the Deccan, surrounded by sea. Despite the conquerors’ restrictions on intermarriage, the Andites were largely absorbed by 10,000 B.C. (79:2.1, 79:2.4, 79:2.5, 79:2.6)

This section includes the narrator’s assessment of the gains and losses of racial mixture. The text holds that mixture is advantageous because it favors versatility of culture, but that its achievements need certain conditions to endure. This is offered as a diagnosis explaining India’s history in that age, and the commentary adds no further generalization. (79:2.7)

The blending of the Andites with the native stock produced the Dravidians, who soon built the most versatile civilization then on earth. They were among the earliest city builders; by 7000 B.C. their camel trains and ships reached Mesopotamia and the East Indies, and they brought an alphabet and the art of writing from Sumeria. Their civilization survives in the Deccan to this day. (79:3.1, 79:3.7, 79:3.8)

In religious history, too, India had a special beginning. The thread of monotheism in Indian religion stems from the teachings of the second garden, and as early as 16,000 B.C. one hundred Sethite priests entered India and very nearly won the western half of it. But their teaching did not last. (79:3.3, 79:3.4)

Within five thousand years their doctrines of the Paradise Trinity had degenerated into the triune symbol of the fire god. (79:3.4)

IV. The Aryan Invasion of India — What Caste Could Not Preserve

In the middle of the third millennium before Christ, the Aryan invasion—the last great migration out of Turkestan—went on for nearly five hundred years. Too few to complete the conquest, the Aryans were absorbed by the Dravidians of the south, and except in the north their influence was chiefly cultural and religious. On the Gangetic plain the two streams mingled to produce a high culture. (79:4.1, 79:4.2, 79:4.3, 79:4.4)

Of the four castes, the first, the teacher-priests, stems from the Sethites; the rest were set up in a futile attempt to prevent amalgamation. The Aryans brought concepts of Deity preserved from the religion of the second garden, but the Brahman priests could not withstand the pagan momentum of sudden contact with the religions of the Deccan. This, the text says, is why India failed to produce the high civilization once foreshadowed. (79:4.6, 79:4.7)

for religion, true religion, is the indispensable source of that higher energy which drives men to establish a superior civilization based on human brotherhood. (79:4.9)

What institutional walls sought to protect was not protected; the power that lifts a civilization comes in the end from true religion. That is the conclusion of this section. And the text leaves a hope. (79:4.8, 79:4.9)

someday a greater Gautama may arise to lead all India in the search for the living God (79:4.8)

V. Red Man and Yellow Man — The Long Struggle for Asia

The story of eastern Asia belongs to the primary Sangiks, the red and yellow races. The red man was first to form tribal organization and reigned in eastern Asia for almost a hundred thousand years before the yellow tribes arrived. The yellow race, entering China along the coast more than three hundred thousand years ago, pushed northward, and the two races fought for more than two hundred thousand years. At first the red men generally prevailed, but the yellow man learned the art of war and showed a marked ability to live peaceably with his own people. (79:5.1, 79:5.2, 79:5.3, 79:5.5)

the Chinese were the first to learn that in union there is strength. (79:5.5)

The red tribes, torn by internal conflict, left Asia when the Bering isthmus opened, and the last pure red men departed eighty-five thousand years ago. The red and yellow races are the only stocks that achieved a high civilization apart from Andite influence, and the peoples of the Western Hemisphere remained nearly isolated from the rest of the world until the end of the first millennium after Christ. From the same starting point, the results diverged between those who co-operated and those who divided. (79:5.6, 79:5.8, 79:5.9)

The civilizations of the Americas were evolutionary products of the Sangiks. The oldest Amerindian culture was the Onamonalonton center in California, and more enduring civilizations later arose in Mexico, Central America, and the mountains of South America. The text reveals that the original Melchizedek plan stipulated that one million pure-line descendants of Adam should go to upstep the red men of the Americas—a glimpse of the distance between the original plan and the history that actually unfolded. (79:5.8, 79:5.9)

VI–VII. Dawn of Chinese Civilization; The Andites Enter China

After driving out the red man, the Chinese had by twenty thousand years ago built a dozen centers of culture along the Yellow River and the Yangtze. The text sums up the reasons the ancient yellow race moved ahead under four factors. (79:6.5, 79:6.6)

In 15,000 B.C. the Chinese numbered fewer than twelve million, yet as a compact body speaking a common language they formed a nation more united than any of their political unions in historic times. From 25,000 to 5000 B.C. the highest mass civilization on Urantia was in central and northern China. But for the mountain barriers and a later decline in spiritual culture, the text adds, the yellow race would quickly have dominated world civilization. (79:6.8, 79:6.9, 79:6.13)

About fifteen thousand years ago Andites crossed the pass of Ti Tao into the upper Yellow River valley. Though few, their mixture produced a more versatile stock, and the Yellow River settlements forged ahead of the Yangtze villages. The text judges that this limited infusion did not disturb the stability of the Sangik type. Cities and manufacture began after 10,000 B.C., and Chinese merchants traveled to Mesopotamia even in Sumerian days, to the benefit of both. (79:7.1, 79:7.3, 79:7.5, 79:7.6)

VIII. Later Chinese Civilization — The Light and Shadow of Ancestor Veneration

With external threats gone, the old military state dissolved and a peaceful society of agriculture and family took its place. Pride in past achievements, the conservatism of a farming people, and a well-developed family life gave birth to ancestor veneration. Then the search for new truth became overshadowed by a tendency to venerate what was already established. (79:8.2, 79:8.3, 79:8.4)

Slowly the genius of the yellow race became diverted from the pursuit of the unknown to the preservation of the known. (79:8.4)

Truth, the text says, is relative and expanding and lives always in the present. At the same time it praises the amazing stability of Chinese culture as the fruit of the paramount place given the family. Vestiges of a real concept of God survived in the imperial worship of Shang-ti, and in family loyalty, group ethics, and personal morality no civilization has excelled the Chinese. (79:8.7, 79:8.9, 79:8.16)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 79 sets three regions side by side and shows that civilization is not decided by bloodline or terrain alone. India, trying to keep its identity through institutions, lost its way as its religion declined; China, which moved ahead through the worship of truth, came to rest in veneration of the past. Yet the text closes by saying that this civilization is now reawakening. (79:4.9, 79:8.4, 79:8.17)
