Introduction — A Creator Living an Ordinary Human Life

Paper 128 covers Jesus’ twenty-first through twenty-sixth years. The narrator first reminds us that he came into the world as other children do, without selecting his parents, and grew up wrestling with the vicissitudes of his environment just like other mortals. He then sets out two purposes we must always keep in mind when reading of this bestowal. (128:0.1, 128:0.2)

Source 128:0.3

2. Revealing the FatherRevealing the Universal Father to the mortals of time and space and leading them to a better understanding of him

These two purposes are the thread running through the whole paper. Each ordinary scene—caring for the family, working, declining offers, training his brothers—was at once experience that earned sovereignty and a life that revealed the Father. (128:0.5, 128:7.6)

I. The Twenty-First Year (A.D. 15) — A True Man Among Men

Having reached adulthood, Jesus began in earnest, fully aware of his dual nature, to complete the experience of living the life of his lowest form of intelligent creatures. He labored, grew weary, rested, and slept; he hungered and thirsted and experienced the full range of human feelings. He chose “the Son of Man” as his first title because he knew full well that he was a mortal man, born of woman. (128:1.1, 128:1.2)

Until after his baptism he availed himself of no supernatural power. (128:1.3)

As to the attributes of his prehuman existence he emptied himself, and before his public work his knowledge of men and events was wholly self-limited. So, as one who was actually tested and tried, he is abundantly able to understand and minister to the confused and distressed. That we have a ruler who knows our weakness is the first comfort this paper offers its readers. (128:1.4, 128:1.5)

He was a true man among men. (128:1.4)

His human nature was self-evident to him, but the self-realization of his divine nature was a slow and natural evolutionary revelation. It began in Jerusalem when he was nearly thirteen and was completed at his baptism in the Jordan, and nothing supernatural happened in between. The narrator testifies that, apart from his guardian seraphim, he did not use the guidance of celestial personalities. (128:1.8, 128:1.9)

This does not mean he was not divine. After he began his public career, he made no objection when he was called by many great titles. There was only one title he refused. (128:1.10, 128:1.11)

When he was once called Immanuel, he merely replied, “Not I, that is my elder brother.” (128:1.11)

At Passover this year he took his brother Joseph to Jerusalem, because he never showed partiality in his family. When Joseph asked about his mission along the way, he usually replied only, “My hour has not yet come.” (128:1.14, 128:1.15)

II. The Twenty-Second Year (A.D. 16) — James Installed as Acting Head

As his brothers and sisters passed one by one through the trials of adolescence, Jesus grappled with their problems alongside them. A family conference decided that not all the boys should become carpenters, and Simon went to work with Jacob the stone mason. When carpentry was slack, Jesus went to Sepphoris and spent six months learning the smith’s trade. (128:2.1, 128:2.2, 128:2.3)

Before leaving he held a family conference, solemnly installed James, just past eighteen, as acting head of the family, and exacted promises of obedience to James from everyone. He could have walked home each night, but he purposely stayed away; his true motive was to train James and Joseph to bear the family responsibility. (128:2.4)

Never again did Jesus take the reins out of James’s hands. (128:2.4)

In Sepphoris he worked and lived with gentiles and made a close study of the gentile mind. But the city’s moral standards were far below those of Nazareth, and he was unwilling to be employed on public work under Herod Antipas, so he returned to Nazareth. Even then he did not take back the direction of the household, leaving it with James. (128:2.5, 128:2.6)

III. The Twenty-Third Year (A.D. 17) — A Journey with Simon, and a Meeting with Stephen

With four now working, the family’s finances eased a little, and Jesus took Simon to Jerusalem by way of the Decapolis, returning by the coast road. These three weeks were the longest break from daily toil he had had since his father’s death. At Philadelphia they met a merchant from Damascus who owned over four thousand camels, and this meeting led to the episode that follows. (128:3.1, 128:3.2, 128:3.3)

In Jerusalem Jesus spent four hours with a young Hellenist named Stephen, talking about the way of life and the true God and his worship. Stephen never forgot his words; years later his bold preaching of the gospel led to his being stoned to death. The emotions aroused in Saul of Tarsus, who stood by, eventually led him to become Paul. (128:3.5, 128:3.6)

Meanwhile Mary was upset to hear that in Jerusalem Jesus had spent his time visiting with strangers, especially those from far countries. The family could never comprehend his urge to know people and learn their thoughts, and Mary was slowly giving up the idea that he was a child of promise. (128:3.8, 128:3.9)

IV. The Damascus Episode — One of the Greatest Temptations

Jesus spent the last four months of the year in Damascus as the merchant’s guest. The merchant proposed to found a school of religious philosophy that would outrival Alexandria, asked Jesus to head it, and brought twelve merchants and bankers to back it. The text calls this one of the greatest temptations Jesus ever faced in his purely human career. (128:4.1, 128:4.2)

He well knew that his mission on earth was not to be supported by institutions of learning; he knew that he must not obligate himself in the least to be directed by the “councils of men,” no matter how well-intentioned. (128:4.2)

He never told his family about the offer and returned to his daily duties in Nazareth as if nothing had happened. The narrator explains that he deliberately kept the episodes of his life separate so that people would never connect them as the doings of one individual, for the following reasons. (128:4.4, 128:4.5)

As the years passed, it became harder and harder to recognize that this man was a Son of God. He seemed just another man among men, and the Father in heaven had ordained that the bestowal should unfold in exactly this way. (128:4.9)

V. The Twenty-Fourth Year (A.D. 18) — The Invitation from Alexandria

In his first year of comparative freedom from family responsibility, five prominent Jews of Alexandria met him at Caesarea and urged him to become a religious teacher in their city. They warned that any Palestinian uprising would be national suicide and that Jerusalem would be destroyed. Jesus thanked them but declined, saying in substance, “My hour has not yet come,” and he also refused the purse they offered. (128:5.1, 128:5.2, 128:5.3, 128:5.4)

“The house of Joseph has never received alms, and we cannot eat another’s bread as long as I have strong arms and my brothers can labor.” (128:5.4)

The six months that followed were the most uneventful of his whole career. He communed much with his Father in heaven and made tremendous progress in mastering his human mind. In December James asked to marry Esta, and Miriam to marry Jacob the stone mason; Jesus consented on condition that each first train a successor. (128:5.6, 128:5.7, 128:5.8)

VI. The Twenty-Fifth Year (A.D. 19) — Jude’s Arrest, and Uncle Joshua

With all debts paid, Jesus took his youngest brother Jude on his first visit to the temple. But Jude burst out in anger at a Roman guard who had insulted a Jewish girl and was arrested, and Jesus stayed with him in prison for two days. Before the magistrate he pleaded the case judiciously and won his brother’s release. He said nothing to the family, but three weeks later he had a long talk with Jude. (128:6.3, 128:6.5, 128:6.6, 128:6.7, 128:6.8)

This was the last Passover Jesus attended with any member of his family. Around this time he put sand, blocks, and stones beside the repair shop, and children flocked there and begged “Uncle Joshua” to come out and tell them a big story. His friends could hardly understand how he could swing so completely from profound discussions of politics and philosophy to the joyous play of little children. (128:6.9, 128:6.11, 128:6.12)

The Roman magistrate warned him to keep an eye on the lad, and indeed Jude caused trouble again and again through thoughtless patriotic outbursts. Yet Jude never forgot the patience and forbearance his brother-father showed throughout that ordeal. The time spent sharing his confinement lasted longer in Jude’s memory than any rebuke could have. (128:6.7, 128:6.8)

The children loved Jesus, and Jesus loved the children. (128:6.11)

VII. The Twenty-Sixth Year (A.D. 20) — His Father’s House Given to James

This year Jesus became strongly conscious of possessing a wide range of potential power, but he was fully persuaded that it was not to be used until his hour should come. The conclusion of his thinking was expressed in a prayer on the hilltop. (128:7.1, 128:7.2)

“Regardless of who I am and what power I may or may not wield, I always have been, and always will be, subject to the will of my Paradise Father.” (128:7.2)

The stumbling block in the family was Jude. When James and Joseph wanted to cast him out, Jesus counseled patience and persuasion by example, and that counsel prevented a break in the family. When Jude ran away, Jesus placed him in the care of a fisherman relative at Magdala, and from then on Jude worked fairly steadily. (128:7.3, 128:7.4, 128:7.8)

“Be patient. Be wise in your counsel and eloquent in your lives, that your young brother may first know the better way and then be constrained to follow you in it.” (128:7.4)

In November, the day after the double wedding, Jesus solemnly abdicated as head of the household and established James as “head and protector of my father’s house,” while promising to send something each month until his hour should come. In this way he made ready to enter the second, home-detached phase of his adult life before beginning his public work. (128:7.10, 128:7.13, 128:7.14)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 128 is a record of preparing to leave. Jesus did not abandon his family; he trained them to move slowly from dependence to independence. At the same time he declined honor, money, and institutional backing one by one, guarding his mission from becoming a religion that exalted a man. (128:4.6, 128:7.5)
