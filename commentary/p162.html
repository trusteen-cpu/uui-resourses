Introduction — Turned Away by a Samaritan Village

Jesus and the ten apostles meant to travel to Jerusalem by way of Samaria. But a village on the slopes of Mount Gilboa refused them lodging because Jesus was a Jew, and drove off Philip and Matthew, who had shown their anger, with sticks and stones. James and John then asked permission to bid fire come down from heaven to devour the villagers. (162:0.1, 162:0.2)

Vengeance savors not of the outlook of the kingdom of heaven. (162:0.2)

Jesus did not argue; he simply turned toward another little village by the Jordan ford. The narrator notes that because of sectarian prejudice those Samaritans denied themselves the honor of showing hospitality to the Creator Son of a universe. The response to insult was not revenge but a change of road, and in that sense this brief episode sets the tone for the whole paper. (162:0.2, 162:0.3)

I. The Dangers of the Visit to Jerusalem — Protected by Boldness

His followers had long urged Jesus to proclaim the gospel in Jerusalem, but now that he meant to go, they feared for his life. They knew the Sanhedrin intended to bring him to trial, and he had repeatedly told them he must meet death. Where he had once said, “My hour has not yet come,” he now answered only that his hour had come. (162:1.1, 162:1.2)

Several reasons combined to allow him to teach openly in the temple courts and still leave alive. The text’s explanation can be summed up as follows. (162:1.5)

Those who heard him were divided. Some called him a good man, a prophet, the true Messiah; others denounced him as a mischief-maker leading the people astray. His enemies feared to denounce him openly because of the believing crowd, and his friends feared to acknowledge him openly because of the leaders. Yet even his enemies, who knew he had never studied in the rabbinical schools, marveled at his teaching. (162:1.10)

His mere appearance in Jerusalem at the feast of tabernacles put an end forever to the rumor that he had fled to Phoenicia out of fear. The text says that this teaching in Solomon’s Porch and the temple courts was in effect the formal announcement of Jesus’ divinity to the Jewish people and the world. As the apostles heard him speak more boldly day by day, their fears only grew. (162:1.3, 162:1.9, 162:1.11)

II. The First Temple Talk — Eber Returns Empty-Handed

Asked how he could teach so fluently when he had never studied under the rabbis, Jesus answered that his teaching was not his own but belonged to the One who sent him. And he placed the test of it not in knowledge but in the will. (162:2.1)

If any man really desires to do my Father’s will, he shall certainly know about my teaching, whether it be God’s or whether I speak for myself. (162:2.1)

He explained that the rulers sought to kill him because of a gospel that frees people from formal, ceremonial religion and heavy tradition, and he appealed to them to judge not by appearances but by the true spirit of his teaching. Then he spoke without malice to Eber, the Sanhedrin officer sent to arrest him, inviting him not to be afraid to come closer. (162:2.2, 162:2.6, 162:2.7)

I know the Father, for I have come from the Father to declare and reveal him to you. (162:2.3)

Eber came back without Jesus and reported that he had never heard a man speak like this man. When the leaders rebuked him, he asked in turn whether the law did not require fairness, and whether they would condemn a man before hearing him. A man who went out to do his duty listened to the teaching and ended by following the voice of his conscience. (162:2.9)

He cheers the downhearted, and his words were comforting even to our souls. (162:2.9)

III. The Woman Taken in Adultery — A Distorted Record and What Really Happened

The text states that the record of this episode as it has come down to us is distorted. Jesus knew the scribes and Pharisees were blinded by tradition, but he also knew they were among the most thoroughly moral men of their day. In fact, it was agents hired by the Sanhedrin who brought the woman before him. (162:3.1, 162:3.2)

Source 162:3.3
Jesus’ choiceThe trap his enemies had prepared
If he said, “Stone her”A death sentence without approval of the Roman courts would bring him into conflict with the Roman rulers
If he said, “Do not stone her”They would accuse him before the Sanhedrin of setting himself above Moses and the Jewish law
If he kept silentThey would accuse him of cowardice

The woman was the wife of a man who had been a troublemaker in Nazareth in Jesus’ youth; her husband had made her a source of income and had handed her over in a deal with the rulers’ hirelings. Jesus went to where the husband stood behind the crowd and wrote in the sand, and the husband was the first to leave. Then the accusers, and finally the woman’s companion in evil, read what he wrote and went away one by one. (162:3.4, 162:3.5)

I know about you; neither do I condemn you. Go your way in peace. (162:3.5)

The woman’s name was Hildana; she left her wicked husband and joined the disciples of the kingdom. Jesus did not publicly shame the plotters, yet he let the trap collapse under its own weight. The scene shows that justice and mercy need not stand opposed; they can stand together. (162:3.3, 162:3.5)

IV. The Feast of Tabernacles — The Feast of Feasts

The feast of tabernacles, which drew people from all over the known world, from Spain to India, was an ideal occasion for Jesus to proclaim his full gospel publicly. The cool autumn weather brought more Jews than either Passover or Pentecost, and sacrifices missed at other feasts could all be offered at this one. (162:4.1, 162:4.2)

Only the Roman tower of Antonia looked down on the brilliant festivities in grim contrast. Jesus’ two discourses that followed take hold of these very symbols of light and water and point to the spiritual realities behind them. (162:4.2)

V–VI. Sermon on the Light of the World; Discourse on the Water of Life

I am the light of the world. He who follows me shall not walk in darkness but shall have the light of life. (162:5.2)

On the evening before the last day, while the candlesticks and torches lit the scene, Jesus stood up and declared that he was the light of the world. He said he knew where he came from, who he was, and where he was going, and that his hearers judged only by the appearances of the flesh and did not perceive the realities of the spirit. He judged no one, he said, and if he did judge, he would judge together with the Father. (162:5.1, 162:5.2)

my Father loves even his erring children. (162:5.3)

He foretold that when the Son of Man was lifted up, all would know who he was. He did nothing of himself but did as the Father had taught him, and because he did what was pleasing in the Father’s sight, he was not alone. Many who heard believed, and no one dared to lay hands on him. (162:5.4, 162:5.5)

On the great day of the feast, just after the priests had poured the water and wine on the altar, Jesus called out that whoever was thirsty should come to him and drink. He did not interrupt the ceremony; he spoke in the brief interval while the Hallel was finished and the offerings prepared. What he promised was the Spirit of Truth, to be poured out on all flesh when his work on earth was done. (162:6.1, 162:6.2)

Those who receive this spirit shall never know spiritual thirst. (162:6.1)

Jesus granted that their fathers had done well to symbolize the outpouring of the spirit, yet he asked why they would water their souls with the traditions of men, poured from the broken pitchers of ceremonial service. The point of the discourse is to honor the symbol but press on to the reality it points to. (162:6.3)

VII. The Discourse on Spiritual Freedom — Before Abraham Was

On the afternoon of the last day, Jesus told the believers in Solomon’s Porch that the truth would make them free. The freedom he meant was not outward release from another’s rule but the liberty of the soul. (162:7.1, 162:7.2)

If, therefore, the Son shall make you free, shall make you sons, you shall be free indeed. (162:7.2)

He said that it is the Father who would glorify him, and that he had come to show people how to become truly sons of God. When an uproar broke out, he said only, “Before Abraham was, I am,” and slipped away to a secret meeting place near Bethany, where Martha, Mary, and Lazarus were waiting for him. (162:7.5, 162:7.6)

VIII–IX. The Visit with Martha and Mary; At Bethlehem with Abner

In the home of his friends at Bethany, Mary sat at Jesus’ feet listening to his teaching while Martha busied herself preparing the evening meal. The text explains that Martha was given to being needlessly distracted by many unnecessary tasks. Jesus’ answer did not merely take Mary’s side; it called both sisters to co-operation. (162:8.2, 162:8.3)

But when will both of you learn to live as I have taught you: both serving in co-operation and both refreshing your souls in unison? (162:8.3)

During the feast Jesus divided his time almost equally between Bethany and Bethlehem. At Bethlehem he taught Abner and the former apostles of John the Baptist, who, moved by the courage they had seen in Jerusalem and the sympathy they experienced in his personal teaching, wholeheartedly accepted the kingdom. Early in November, Abner and his eleven associates joined Jesus and the twelve in a single organization. (162:9.3, 162:9.5)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 162 shows Jesus speaking most boldly in the very midst of danger. Yet his boldness was not aggression. He took no revenge on the village that insulted him, bore no ill will toward the officer sent to arrest him, and did not condemn the woman used as bait in a trap. The courage to proclaim truth and the mercy with which he treated people lived together in one man. (162:0.2, 162:2.7, 162:3.5)
