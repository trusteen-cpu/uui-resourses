Introduction — The History of a Vanished Cradle

Paper 78 covers the span from about 35,000 B.C., soon after Adam’s default, to about 2000 B.C., when the violet race finally disappeared from its Mesopotamian homelands. It is presented by an Archangel of Nebadon. Its central stage is the second garden between the Tigris and Euphrates, and its protagonists are the Adamites and the Andites they became as they blended with other peoples. (78:0.1, 78:0.2)

The second Eden was the cradle of civilization for almost thirty thousand years. (78:0.1)

This paper has much to say about races and bloodlines. This commentary traces that course within the bounds of what the text says, and its aim is not to exalt or belittle any group. The text itself portrays the rise and fall of cultures as the outcome not of bloodlines alone but of tradition, resources, climate, and leadership working together.

I. Racial and Cultural Distribution — The World Thirty-Five Thousand Years Ago

When Adam arrived, the physical evolution of the races had gone on unaffected by the rebellion, but minds and morals were at a low level. Though partially a failure, Adam’s contribution greatly upstepped the peoples. Still, thirty-five thousand years ago most of the world had little culture, with centers of civilization scattered here and there. (78:1.1, 78:1.2)

Having drawn this map, the text calls it the world just before the great expansions of the violet race some twenty-five thousand years ago. The hope of future civilization lay in the second garden between the rivers, where the ideas and ideals salvaged from Dalamatia and Eden had the potential to spread to all the world. The progeny Adam and Eve left behind was limited in number, but potent. (78:1.12, 78:1.13)

II. The Adamites in the Second Garden — The Fate of a Borrowed Civilization

For thousands of years Adam’s descendants worked on irrigation and flood control to the south and on defenses to the north, striving to preserve the traditions of the first Eden. Never losing sight of the purpose of their mission, they sent their choicest sons and daughters as emissaries to the other races. At times this depleted their home culture, but always they recovered. (78:2.1, 78:2.2)

But the civilization of the second Eden was an artificial structure—it had not been evolved—and was therefore doomed to deteriorate until it reached a natural evolutionary level. (78:2.3)

Adam left a great intellectual and spiritual culture but no advanced mechanical appliances. Every civilization, the text explains, is limited by natural resources, inherent genius, and leisure for invention. A culture resting on Adam’s presence and the traditions of Eden settled, as those traditions faded, to a level in balance with its surroundings. It invites us to consider that what is given from above lasts longest when a foundation grown from within supports it. (78:2.4)

This does not mean the second garden was small or weak. Around 19,000 B.C. the Adamites were a real nation of four and a half million, and they had already poured millions of descendants into the surrounding peoples. The decline was not a sudden collapse but a gentle descent over thousands of years. (78:2.5)

III. Early Expansions of the Adamites — Sending Teachers Instead of Conquering

The violet race long kept the peaceful traditions of Eden, so even as their population grew they did not make war for land. The cultural effect of these early migrations did not last, but those who went out as teachers, traders, and explorers brought biologic vigor to the peoples around them. (78:3.1)

instead of making war to secure more territory, they sent forth their excess inhabitants as teachers to the other races. (78:3.1)

The main current of migration went north, circled the Caspian, and turned west into Europe. But the farther they went, the more the Adamic traits thinned, and their culture was largely lost in transit. Only the later Andites moved fast enough to carry the culture of Eden far from Mesopotamia. (78:3.2, 78:3.3, 78:3.10)

IV. The Andites — A Versatile People Born of Blending

The Andites were the primary blends of the violet race and the Nodites plus the evolutionary peoples; the term generally designates peoples whose inheritance was from one-eighth to one-sixth violet. They arose on the outer edge of the concentric circles surrounding the second garden and spread northward as they blended with the inhabitants of Turkestan. (78:4.1, 78:4.2)

These early Andites were not Aryan; they were pre-Aryan. (78:4.4)

The text adds that they were not white, and neither Occidental nor Oriental. We may read this as a warning not to project today’s racial categories onto the past. It also records that as the peaceful violet tradition joined with the belligerent Nodite stock, their movements became increasingly military. The character of a culture is not fixed; it changes with blending and with choices. (78:4.4, 78:4.5, 78:4.6)

The Andites were adventurous and had roving dispositions. Mixture with other stocks steadied them somewhat, yet their descendants never stopped until they had circumnavigated the globe and found the last remote continent. That persistent movement toward new places laid out the paths along which civilization later spread. (78:4.6)

V–VI. The Andite Migrations; The Last Andite Dispersions

Around 15,000 B.C. the revival of the Sethite priesthood made the Garden shine again, and great waves of civilization spread over Eurasia and North Africa. In the highlands of Turkestan languages blended to form the so-called Aryan mother tongue. One hundred and thirty-two Andites sailed from Japan in small boats, crossed the Pacific, and reached South America, and Easter Island was long a center of one of these lost groups. (78:5.1, 78:5.3, 78:5.7)

And to every nation to which they journeyed, they contributed humor, art, adventure, music, and manufacture. (78:5.8)

By 12,000 B.C. three quarters of the world’s Andites lived in northern and eastern Europe. They went also to northern China and India, and as missionaries, teachers, and traders to the ends of the earth. The text identifies them as the so-called Dravidian and later Aryan conquerors of India, and records that some went to China by way of Sinkiang and Tibet. (78:5.4, 78:5.5, 78:5.6)

Between 8000 and 6000 B.C., pressed by the hill tribes to the east and the plainsmen to the west, the last three waves left Mesopotamia. They divided as follows. (78:6.1)

These migrations drained the biologic reserves of the homeland while greatly strengthening the surrounding peoples. The cultural age of the second garden came to an end, and civilization moved west to the Nile and the Mediterranean islands. (78:5.8, 78:6.8)

VII. The Floods in Mesopotamia — The Real Story of Noah

From about 5000 B.C. the northern highlands rose rapidly and snowfall increased, bringing unprecedented floods to the Euphrates valley every spring. For almost a thousand years scores of cities were deserted, and these floods completed the disruption of Andite civilization. (78:7.2, 78:7.6)

There has never been a universal flood since life was established on Urantia. (78:7.4)

The text explains that the story of a world-wide flood was devised by Hebrew priests in the Babylonian captivity to connect their genealogies. Yet it affirms that Noah himself was real. He was a wine maker of Aram near Erech who kept a yearly record of the river’s rise and was ridiculed for urging that houses be built like boats. When one year unusually heavy rains swept away the village, only his family survived in their houseboat. (78:7.3, 78:7.5)

But Noah really lived (78:7.5)

VIII. The Sumerians—Last of the Andites

The small minority who remained at the river mouths were the Sumerians. Even when horse-riding invaders from the northeast overran the whole valley, they kept their integrity for a long time thanks to superior intelligence, better weapons, their system of military canals, and a uniform group religion. The invaders soon came to trust these peaceful people as teachers and administrators. (78:8.3, 78:8.5, 78:8.6)

Each city believed its municipal god to be superior to all other gods, and therefore they refused to subordinate themselves to a common leader. (78:8.7)

After their confederation broke up, the city-states were ruled by apostate descendants of the Sethite priests and, jealous over their city gods, could not unite. Sargon of Kish ended that era, but struggles for supremacy went on, and about 2500 B.C. Lagash fell. By the time of Hammurabi the Sumerians had been absorbed by the northern Semites. The contrast is striking: a people once protected by a united faith lost its strength along with that unity. (78:8.7, 78:8.8, 78:8.10)

The northern tribes who took the valley willingly absorbed what remained of Mesopotamian civilization, adopting Sumerian culture and the arts of the valley. They even tried to build a third tower of Babel and took its name for their nation. What the barbarians failed to do to ruin Mesopotamia, the text adds, later climatic changes accomplished. (78:8.4, 78:8.11)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

The homeland of the violet race vanished amid emigration, immigration, flood, and invasion. Yet the text does not end on mere decline. Long before the horsemen conquered the valley, the culture of the Garden had already spread across three continents, becoming the leaven that shaped today’s civilization. (78:8.12)
