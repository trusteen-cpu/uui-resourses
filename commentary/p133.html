Introduction — The Scribe Who Left Without Farewells

When Jesus left Rome, he said good-bye to none of his friends. The scribe of Damascus had appeared without announcement and disappeared in the same way. The Stoics, Cynics, and mystery cultists who had known him formed small groups drawn together by their interest in his teachings, and they kept meeting until the first preachers of Christianity arrived in Rome. (133:0.1)

The three travelers walked across Italy along the Appian Way to Tarentum. On the road Ganid asked about India’s caste system, and Jesus replied that although human beings differ in many ways, before God all stand on an equal footing. (133:0.2, 133:0.3)

There are only two groups of mortals in the eyes of God: those who desire to do his will and those who do not. (133:0.3)

I. Mercy and Justice — The Work of the Individual and of the Group

Near Tarentum a rough youth was brutally attacking a smaller lad. Jesus rescued the boy and held the attacker until the little one had escaped. But when Ganid began to thrash the bully, Jesus promptly stopped him. Ganid asked: if mercy requires rescuing the smaller boy, does not justice demand punishing the offender? Jesus answered as follows. (133:1.1)

Mercy ministry is always the work of the individual, but justice punishment is the function of the social, governmental, or universe administrative groups. (133:1.2)

Asked what he would do if he himself were attacked, Jesus said he would defend himself against a creature lacking moral judgment, but would not punish a fellow man of sonship status in advance and without judgment. He was absolutely assured that the entire universe was friendly to him. The scene invites us to ask whether, when we are angry, we are trying to make ourselves the judge. (133:1.4)

Ganid was never fully satisfied, but after hearing how Jacob, the stone mason’s son, had defended Jesus in boyhood, he reached his own conclusion: few would want to attack so kindly a person, and if anyone did, someone would likely rush to help. Jesus would say only that when such a test came, he would abide by the Father’s will. On another occasion he did give the opinion that organized society had every right to use force in executing its just mandates. (133:1.5)

II. Embarking at Tarentum — Husband and Wife

At the landing a man was mistreating his wife. Jesus did not rebuke him, but tapped him gently on the shoulder and asked to speak with him privately. He first acknowledged the man’s sense of justice and then asked what had happened. It was not so much his words as his kindly look and sympathetic smile that touched the man’s heart, and he regretted his lack of self-control. (133:2.1)

The loving care and consideration which a man is willing to bestow upon his wife and their children are the measure of that man’s attainment of the higher levels of creative and spiritual self-consciousness. (133:2.2)

Jesus said that men and women are partners with God, since they cooperate to create beings with the potential of immortal souls. Hearing this, Gonod resolved to reorganize his home when he returned to India. (133:2.2, 133:2.3)

What stands out here is Jesus’ approach. He stopped the violence without shaming the man in public. He drew him aside, called out the good in him first, and gave him the chance to name his own fault. As a result the man promised to live up to his former pledge, and the couple stood in a tearful embrace. On the boat to Nicopolis, Ganid began speaking with the ship’s steward about religion, and when he got into deep water he called on Joshua for help. The spirit of personal ministry was taking hold of him. (133:2.1, 133:2.3, 133:2.4)

III–IV. At Corinth; Personal Work in Corinth — Courtesans and People of Many Trades

In Corinth Jesus held more than twenty sessions with Crispus, the chief ruler of the synagogue, while Ganid observed Jewish family life in his home. Years later, when Paul was driven out of the synagogue, Crispus and his entire family embraced the new religion. Of Aquila and Priscilla, who welcomed Paul, Aquila was one of the Cynics Jesus had met in Rome. (133:3.1, 133:3.2, 133:3.12)

One evening two public women accosted them, and Ganid spoke sharply and rudely motioned them away. Jesus stopped him and asked whether he knew the circumstances that had led them to this way of making a living. (133:3.6)

Who are we that we should sit in judgment on these women? (133:3.6)

Jesus saw in their faces not wickedness but sorrow, and Ganid apologized. He took the two women to the home of Justus and entrusted them to his wife, Martha, to plan a new life with them. The elder died soon afterward with bright hopes of survival, and the younger became a lifelong member of the first Christian church in Corinth. Asking about someone’s circumstances before condemning them is what lifts people up. (133:3.7, 133:3.10)

More than half of the people with whom they had intimate conversations during those two months in Corinth later became members of the Christian community. Paul had not intended a long stay in Corinth, but without knowing how well the Jewish tutor had prepared the way, he remained eighteen months. These brief conversations were fitted exactly to each person’s trade and situation. The truth was one, but the words that carried it were translated into the language of each listener’s life. (133:3.3, 133:3.11, 133:3.12)

V. At Athens — Discourse on Science

At their inn in Athens, after a Greek philosopher had lectured for almost three hours, Jesus spoke of the place and limits of science. Science can measure energy manifestations but cannot tell what they are. Logic and mathematics are valid in the material world, but they should not be regarded as infallible when applied to the problems of life. (133:5.3, 133:5.4, 133:5.5)

If one man can shear a sheep in ten minutes, ten men cannot shear it in one minute. Yet a group of people working in coordinated harmony stands for a force far greater than the simple sum of its parts. It is an everyday lesson that the worth of people working together cannot be reckoned by arithmetic alone. (133:5.5, 133:5.6)

The real universe is friendly to every child of the eternal God. (133:5.8)

VI–VII. At Ephesus; The Sojourn at Cyprus — Discourses on the Soul and on Mind

At Ephesus a Greek thinker asked what Jesus meant by “soul.” Jesus described it as the self-reflective, truth-discerning part of man that elevates him above the animal level. Self-consciousness alone is not the soul; moral self-consciousness is its foundation. The divine spirit arrives with the mind’s first moral activity, and that is the occasion of the birth of the soul. (133:6.4, 133:6.5)

The soul of man cannot exist apart from moral thinking and spiritual activity. A stagnant soul is a dying soul. (133:6.5)

Jesus explained that the saving or losing of a soul depends on whether the moral consciousness attains survival status through eternal alliance with the immortal spirit. All soul conflict arises from a lack of harmony between the moral, or spiritual, self-consciousness and the purely intellectual self-consciousness. The soul cannot be proved by material science or by pure spirit-testing, yet every morally conscious mortal knows of its existence as a real personal experience. (133:6.6, 133:6.7)

In the mountains of Cyprus Ganid fell gravely ill with fever, and Jesus nursed him with skill and tenderness. During his recovery Jesus taught about the human mind. Ideas are sensations plus the reflective interpretation of the self, and the self is more than the sum of its sensations. And the mind does not well stand the conflict of double allegiance. (133:7.3, 133:7.7, 133:7.12)

The supremely happy and efficiently unified mind is the one wholly dedicated to the doing of the will of the Father in heaven. (133:7.12)

Peace of mind is not won by compromising ideals but by the stalwart assertion of the triumph of what is true, overcoming evil with the force of good. The teaching suggests that clearly choosing a side, rather than folding up our conscience to avoid conflict, is the real road to peace. (133:7.12)

VIII–IX. At Antioch; In Mesopotamia — The Farewell of Joshua the Teacher

In Antioch Jesus became quieter and more reflective. Ganid sought out a disgruntled Indian employee who was about to be dismissed, told him of the Father in heaven, and helped him with a Hebrew proverb: whatever your hand finds to do, do it with all your might. The pupil had begun to practice what he had learned. (133:8.2, 133:8.3)

After crossing the desert and reaching Ur, the day of parting finally came. Ganid said that the Father in heaven must be something like his teacher. Jesus saw them off at Charax, and in this world they never learned that Jesus of Nazareth was their teacher Joshua. (133:9.4, 133:9.5)

At Ur Ganid had a long talk about the difference between knowledge, wisdom, and truth, and he was charmed by the Hebrew wise man’s saying that wisdom, if embraced, will bring one to honor. Gonod and Ganid extended their stay three weeks in hope of persuading Jesus to come with them to India, but his path lay in Palestine. (133:9.2, 133:9.3)

I will remember your teaching, but most of all, I will never forget you. (133:9.4)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 133 closes the chapter of Joshua the teacher. On this journey Jesus offered truth suited to the moment to people he met by the roadside, at the landing, and in the inn. He asked about circumstances instead of condemning, showed mercy instead of punishing, and even when speaking of science, the soul, and the mind, pointed to a unified life given to doing God’s will. (133:9.6)
