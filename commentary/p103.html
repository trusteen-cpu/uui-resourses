Introduction — The Cosmic Window

Where Paper 102 dealt with the foundations of faith, Paper 103 enters the place where that faith is actually experienced. The author, a Melchizedek of Nebadon, begins by naming in turn the influences that sponsor man’s religious responses: the adjutants of worship and wisdom, the Holy Spirit of the Creative Spirit, the Spirit of Truth that follows the bestowal Sons, and the Thought Adjuster. (103:0.1, 103:9.13)

The Thought Adjuster is the cosmic window through which the finite creature may faith-glimpse the certainties and divinities of limitless Deity, the Universal Father. (103:0.1)

Religious tendencies are innate in mankind, and primitive religions are always evolutionary in origin. Periodic revelations of truth punctuate their slow progress. As a result, four kinds of religion exist side by side on Urantia today. (103:0.2, 103:0.3)

I. Philosophy of Religion — Spiritual Unity, Philosophic Diversity

Religious experience has a unity because the same God fragment indwells each person. But since every personality is unique, no two people interpret its leadings alike. That is why twentieth-century theologians and philosophers produced more than five hundred definitions of religion. (103:1.1)

A group of mortals can experience spiritual unity, but they can never attain philosophic uniformity. (103:1.1)

So it matters to know the varied religious experiences of others; otherwise one’s own religion hardens into something egocentric and unsocial. And contrary to the assumption of rationalism, religion begins not with belief but with the pursuit of values. This is why people agree far more easily on goals than on creeds, and why experience persists even when beliefs change greatly. (103:1.3, 103:1.4)

Theology does not produce religion; it is religion that produces theologic philosophy. (103:1.4)

The experience of realizing spiritual values cannot be named exactly in any human language, and the paper chooses to call it God-consciousness. The Adjuster is prepersonal, yet it exudes a flavor of divinity that is personal in the highest sense. (103:1.6)

II. Religion and the Individual — A Child’s First Unselfish Choice

Religion functions in the mind before it appears in consciousness. Just as a baby is born after nine months, spiritual birth is gradual, yet sooner or later it has its “birth day.” Some pass through anguish like a stormy labor; others grow naturally. Those raised by their parents to know themselves as children of a loving heavenly Father should not look askance at those who reached that awareness only through crisis. (103:2.1, 103:2.2)

The soil in which revealed religion germinates is the moral nature. A child’s first moral promptings concern not guilt but justice, fairness, and kindness—helping others. The psychology of a child is naturally positive; so many people are negative because they were trained to be. (103:2.3, 103:2.5)

When a moral being chooses to be unselfish when confronted by the urge to be selfish, that is primitive religious experience. (103:2.8)

People tend to identify the selfish urge with the self and the altruistic will with an influence from outside—God—and the paper says this judgment is right, for such unselfish desires originate in the leadings of the indwelling Adjuster. Yet when a personality is not unified, altruism can become so overdeveloped that it injures the self, and a misguided conscience can cause endless unhappiness. (103:2.10)

III–IV. Religion and the Human Race; Spiritual Communion

Belief in spirits, dreams, and superstitions, along with clan solidarity, shaped primitive religion, but the social group is not the source of religious experience. The true religious impulse comes from spirit presences that activate the will to be unselfish. Religion evolves favorably as magic gives way to morals, and it is destined at last to become the service of God and of man. (103:3.1, 103:3.2, 103:3.4)

What sets a religious gathering apart from a social one is the atmosphere of communion. A shared meal was the earliest form of communion, and the Lord’s Supper continues it. Communion gives a refreshing truce in the conflict between the self-seeking ego and the altruistic urge of the indwelling spirit, and it is the prelude to true worship, the practice of the presence of God. (103:4.1)

our ideals tend to grow by geometrical progression, while our ability to live up to them is enhanced only by arithmetical progression. (103:4.2)

So a sense of guilt arises in the gap between ideal and practice. The way out is to realize that one’s highest moral ideals are not necessarily the will of God, and to stay true to the purpose of becoming more like him. Jesus swept away the ceremonies of sacrifice and atonement and placed the creature-Creator relationship on a child-parent basis. The Father deals with his children not according to their worthiness but according to their motive and intent. (103:4.3, 103:4.4, 103:4.5)

V. The Origin of Ideals — Between Self and Neighbor

Unlike the sense of duty born of fear, the ideal of altruism comes from the direct impulse of the indwelling spirit. The circle of “neighbor” widened from those close at hand to clan and nation, and Jesus enlarged it to all humanity, even to our enemies. Yet the self has rights as well as the neighbor, and neither can claim exclusive service. (103:5.1, 103:5.2, 103:5.4)

Happiness comes when the desires of the self and the altruistic urge of the higher self are reconciled by a unified will. This conflict is not fully resolved within one short life, but every good thing done in this life directly enriches the life to come. Taught that altruistic impulses are merely herd instinct, idealism is fatally wounded; knowing that they come from the spirit, it is mightily energized. (103:5.5, 103:5.7, 103:5.8)

Man is most truly the architect of his own eternal destiny. (103:5.10)

Spiritual growth springs from within the soul, not from pressure, and it is greatest where external pressures are least. The church, then, should let its members interpret their own experience freely. (103:5.11, 103:5.12)

The security of a religious group depends on spiritual unity, not on theological uniformity. (103:5.12)

VI. Philosophic Co-ordination — The Morontia Gap

Theology is the study of your own religion; the study of another’s religion is psychology. Seen from outside, the universe looks mechanical, energy and matter; seen from within personality, all creation appears spiritual. Materialism and spiritism each see only a universe turned one way, so neither can build a consistent picture of the cosmos. (103:6.1, 103:6.3, 103:6.4, 103:6.5)

Science and religion are hard to reconcile because we know nothing of the intervening domain, the morontia world. The local universe has three stages of reality—matter, morontia, and spirit—and each level has its own technique of knowing. (103:6.7)

VII. Science and Religion — Two Paths Resting on Assumptions

Faith is not founded on reason, yet it is reasonable; it is independent of logic, yet encouraged by sound logic. Ascending man reaches inward toward God and at the same time outward toward an understanding of energy, so the progress of science does not end with earthly life. The two are united through the logic of a philosophy strengthened by revelation. (103:7.1, 103:7.3, 103:7.6)

The paper asks both science and religion for more fearless self-criticism; teachers on both sides are often too self-confident and dogmatic. Neither can prove its own starting point. (103:7.7)

In the mortal state, nothing can be absolutely proved; both science and religion are predicated on assumptions. (103:7.10)

The presence of the Adjuster is real proof of spiritual reality, but it cannot be demonstrated to the outside world—only to the one who experiences that indwelling. Science discovers the material world, religion evaluates it, and philosophy seeks to interpret its meanings. (103:7.14, 103:7.15)

VIII–IX. Philosophy and Religion; The Essence of Religion

Philosophy can only assume the probability of God; certainty is affirmed only by the experience of a spirit-led person. The experience may be wholly valid while talk about God remains divergent. The paper’s illustration makes the point: a man who loves his wife completely may fail a written examination on the psychology of marital love, yet that does not make his love any less real. (103:8.1, 103:8.2, 103:8.3)

If you know and love God by faith, the paper urges, do not let that experience be diminished by the doubts of science, the caviling of logic, or the clever arguments for a religion without God. It is rather the unbeliever’s doubt that should be challenged. The most helpful philosophy avoids both materialism and pantheism and recognizes the reality of personality, permanence in the presence of change. (103:8.4, 103:8.5, 103:8.6)

No matter how illusory and erroneous one’s theology, one’s religion may be wholly genuine and everlastingly true. (103:9.2)

The paper names original Buddhism as one of the best religions without a God, yet says that religion without faith is a contradiction. It also warns that when theology dominates religion, religion becomes a doctrine instead of a life. (103:9.3, 103:9.6)

When theology masters religion, religion dies (103:9.6)

When reason recognizes right and wrong, it shows wisdom; when wisdom chooses between truth and error, it shows spirit leading. Mind, soul, and spirit always work together in this way. Faith leads not merely to a mystical feeling but to knowing God, and it must not be overmuch swayed by its emotional consequences. (103:9.10, 103:9.11)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 103 pictures religious experience not as theology or emotion but as a living reality that begins with the choice to look past self toward others. Interpretations differ, yet the experience can be true, and its convictions are unassailable. A reader of this paper can be generous toward the faith of others while holding his own experience with courage. (103:9.12)
