Introduction — The Levant, Cradle of Western Faith

Where Paper 94 followed the Salem gospel eastward, Paper 95 traces its western road. As India gave rise to the religions of eastern Asia, so the Levant was the homeland of the faiths of the Occidental world. The Salem missionaries spread through Palestine, Mesopotamia, Egypt, Iran, and Arabia, but the results differed from land to land. (95:0.1)

Sometimes their failures were due to lack of wisdom, sometimes to circumstances beyond their control. (95:0.1)

I. The Salem Religion in Mesopotamia — Attempting Too Much

By 2000 B.C. Mesopotamia had almost lost the teachings of the Sethites. The Salem teachers reduced the chief gods to seven—Bel, Shamash, Nabu, Anu, Ea, Marduk, and Sin—and then exalted Bel, Ea, and Anu as a supreme triad of earth, sea, and sky. These triads were based on the Salemites’ belief in Melchizedek’s insignia of the three circles. (95:1.1, 95:1.3, 95:1.4)

Things went wrong when Nabodad, leader of the school at Kish, launched a concerted attack on temple harlotry. When the social reform failed, the spiritual teachings went down with it, and the cult of Ishtar and astrology enjoyed a great revival. The author names the cause of the failure plainly. (95:1.5, 95:1.6, 95:1.7)

But it has often been the error of the teachers of new truth to attempt too much, to attempt to supplant slow evolution by sudden revolution. (95:1.8)

The missionaries had been commissioned to preach a definite gospel, but they became entangled in the apparently worthy cause of reforming the mores. The lesson is to hold to the central mission and not let side causes drain one’s strength. Even so, scattered bands kept faith in the one Creator, and the psalms they inscribed on stone later found their way into the Hebrew collection of hymns. The text adds that the Book of Job fairly reflects the teachings of the Salem school. (95:1.8, 95:1.9, 95:1.10)

II. Early Egyptian Religion — From Tombs to Conscience

The original Melchizedek teachings took their deepest root in Egypt. Egypt fostered the most thoroughly blended religious philosophy, and the Jews took their idea of creation from Babylon but their concept of divine Providence from Egypt. Egypt was more favorable to the Salem teaching because each dynasty exalted its tribal god as creator of all others, accustoming the people to the idea of a supergod. (95:2.1, 95:2.2)

Seeing that bodies buried in the sand were preserved, the Egyptians began to embalm the dead, and burial statues led to great advances in art. Magical texts such as The Book of the Dead appeared, but in the Nile valley ritual early became bound up with conscience and character. So, in time, the Egyptians came to rely for salvation on ethical ideals rather than elaborate tombs. (95:2.4, 95:2.5)

When Melchizedek appeared, the Egyptians had a religion far above that of their neighbors. They believed the soul came to the judgment hall of Osiris and, if innocent of murder, robbery, falsehood, adultery, theft, and selfishness, entered the realms of bliss. The idea of judgment after death for one’s earthly life passed from Egypt into Hebrew theology. (95:2.9, 95:2.10)

III. Evolution of Moral Concepts — The Humanism of the Nile

Much of Egypt’s social and ethical idealism grew up in the Nile valley as a purely evolutionary development. Here the author states an important principle: moral evolution does not depend wholly on revelation; because a divine spirit indwells him, man can derive spiritual values and cosmic insight from his own experience. (95:3.1, 95:3.2)

The message of one of the great teachers of this epoch was: “Do right and deal justly with all.” (95:3.3)
Of all the purely human religions of Urantia none ever surpassed the social ideals and the moral grandeur of this onetime humanism of the Nile valley. (95:3.3)

Yet Egypt was intellectual and moral but not overly spiritual. Of the four great prophets who arose in six thousand years, Amenemope was followed for a season, Okhban was murdered, Ikhnaton was accepted halfheartedly for one generation, and Moses was rejected. Even so, the teachers of the Nile valley were the first to proclaim conscience as the mandate of God. (95:3.5)

IV. The Teachings of Amenemope — A Link to the Hebrews and Greeks

Amenemope, whom many called the “son of man,” exalted conscience as the highest arbiter between right and wrong. He believed God-consciousness to be the determining factor in all conduct and that every moment should be lived aware of God’s presence and of responsibility to him. The text says his instruction to his son on honesty in public office would do honor to any modern statesman. (95:4.1, 95:4.2)

In substance he taught: Man proposes but God disposes. (95:4.3)

(95:4.4)

V. The Remarkable Ikhnaton — A Reformer Too Far Ahead

As Amenemope’s teachings were losing their hold, a woman of the royal family, influenced by a Salemite physician, persuaded her son Ikhnaton to accept the doctrine of One God. The text says that no one since Melchizedek had possessed so clear a concept of the Salem religion, and that Ikhnaton kept open the philosophic monotheistic channel needed for Michael’s bestowal. The child Jesus was taken to Egypt partly in recognition of this achievement. (95:5.1, 95:5.2)

Under the guise of sun-god worship, he led people to worship the One God, creator of Aton. He wrote a thirty-one-chapter book, “The One God,” and one hundred and thirty-seven hymns, and made “righteousness” the supreme word of daily life. In that generation neither social position nor wealth gave any Egyptian an advantage before the law. (95:5.6, 95:5.7, 95:5.8)

His son-in-law changed his name to Tutankhamen and went back to the old gods. The repercussions of his work persisted for centuries in Palestine and Greece. (95:5.10, 95:5.11, 95:5.13, 95:5.14)

Never did the concept of monotheism die out of the hearts of men in Egypt and in the world. (95:5.11)

VI. The Salem Doctrines in Iran — Zoroaster

The Salem teachers made headway in Iran for more than five hundred years, until a change of rulers brought persecution that all but ended their teaching. Then, in the sixth century before Christ, Zoroaster appeared to revive the smouldering embers. On a pilgrimage to Ur he had learned the traditions of Caligastia and the Lucifer rebellion and of the Seven Master Spirits, and he created a galaxy of seven supreme gods with Ahura-Mazda at its head. (95:6.1, 95:6.2)

His was a religion of action—work—rather than prayers and rituals, a militant philosophy that dared to battle evil, inaction, and backwardness. Zoroaster did not teach fire worship but used the flame as a symbol of the wise Spirit; his later followers worshiped the symbol. Nor was the original teaching a pure dualism. (95:6.3, 95:6.4, 95:6.5)

VII. The Salem Teachings in Arabia — Failure Close to Home

Not even in China or Rome did the Melchizedek teachings fail more completely than in the Arabian desert, so very near Salem itself. Each tribe worshiped its fetish and each family its household gods, and rival concepts of deity contended for ages. Some clans clung to a hazy idea of the one God, and many centers might have responded to the gospel of Jesus, but the Christian missionaries of the desert were austere and unyielding. (95:7.1, 95:7.2, 95:7.3)

The author says that had the followers of Jesus been more gracious in their preaching and less stringent in social requirements of their own devising, many lands, Arabia among them, would gladly have received the simple gospel of the carpenter’s son. Instead, the tribes’ common reverence for the black stone at Mecca became the point of contact for the later establishment of Islam. The text finds Islam’s strength in its clear presentation of Allah as the one Deity, and its weakness in spreading by military force and in its degradation of woman. (95:7.3, 95:7.5, 95:7.6)

“For whenever as many as three speak together, God is present as a fourth,” (95:7.6)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 95 shows through many examples that the way truth is shared matters as much as the truth itself. Mesopotamia failed by attempting too much, Ikhnaton by moving too fast, Arabia by being too rigid. Egypt’s moral culture, on the other hand, testifies that people can reach high ideals through experience even before revelation.
