Introduction — The Lull Before the Storm

From Friday evening, the day they reached Bethsaida after returning from Jerusalem, through Sabbath morning, Jesus was deep in thought; he ate almost nothing and said little. The apostles and their associates gathered in small groups about the house, in the garden, and along the seashore, and an uneasy foreboding hung over them all. This paper records the epoch-making sermon Jesus preached that afternoon in the Capernaum synagogue and the events that ran on into that night. (153:0.1, 153:0.3)

The narrative pauses to note how each apostle reacted, in his own way, to the same tension. Their varied responses mirror their temperaments and their expectations, and a reader may find among them a face that looks much like his or her own. (153:0.2)

I. The Setting of the Stage — The Turning-Point Crisis

At three o’clock in the afternoon, in the new synagogue where Jairus presided, sat fifty-three Pharisees and Sadducees from Jerusalem, more than thirty leaders of the neighboring synagogues, and the official observers of Herod Antipas. This orthodox vanguard, acting under orders from the Sanhedrin, had come to inaugurate open warfare. Jesus knew it, and he chose boldly to assume the offensive. (153:1.1, 153:1.2)

The text calls this sermon the outward turning of the tide of popular fame. From then on the work of the kingdom would be more and more concerned with winning lasting spiritual converts rather than great crowds. The crisis, moreover, spanned different stretches of time for Jesus and for his apostles. (153:1.2, 153:1.4)

Just before the sermon, one question filled the minds of everyone in the synagogue: why had he so deliberately turned back the tide of popular enthusiasm? The feeding of the five thousand had made the greatest appeal to the Jewish idea of the Messiah, but his prompt refusal to be made king had immediately offset that advantage. Disappointment grew into unconscious opposition and eventually into hatred, and it was after this sermon that Judas Iscariot first consciously thought of deserting. (153:1.5, 153:1.6)

Jesus fully understood how men prepare themselves for the decisions of a crisis and the performance of sudden deeds of courageous choosing by the slow process of the reiterated choosing between the recurring situations of good and evil. (153:1.3)

This insight reminds us that great decisions are not made overnight. Jesus put his apostles through repeated rehearsals in disappointment, trusting that in the final test their habitual spirit reactions would carry them through. Meanwhile, despite the long and earnest pleading of the Jerusalem leaders, Jairus kept his word. (153:1.3, 153:1.7)

I have granted this request, and I will not violate my word. (153:1.7)

II. The Epochal Sermon — The Bread of Life

Jesus read from Deuteronomy the curses that would overtake a disobedient people, and from Jeremiah the story of the prophet let down into a filthy dungeon for his warnings. If that was what they did to a prophet who warned of political downfall, he asked, what would they do to a teacher who warned of spiritual doom? He told them they already had sufficient evidence to decide, and he called on them to choose that very day whom they would serve. (153:2.1, 153:2.2, 153:2.3, 153:2.5)

Jesus said that he had left them undisturbed in their positions of influence and power and had only proclaimed new liberty for man’s fear-ridden soul. His purpose was to reveal the Father and to establish the spiritual brotherhood of the sons of God. And the Father had granted them, beyond material wonders, the more evidential signs of spiritual transformation and regeneration. (153:2.4)

He then laid bare the motives of those who had pursued him across the lake. They had come not for truth or service but for more bread for which they had not labored, because they had long been taught that the Messiah would make life pleasant and easy. (153:2.6)

I have come to proclaim spiritual liberty, teach eternal truth, and foster living faith. (153:2.6)

Pointing to the pot of manna carved on the lintel of the synagogue, Jesus said that the manna their forefathers ate was the bread of earth. The bread of heaven is that which comes down from God and gives eternal life—and he himself is that bread. (153:2.8, 153:2.9)

And this bread of life which I give to all who will receive is my own living and combined nature. (153:2.12)

III. The After Meeting — Not Hands but Hearts

Even after the congregation was dismissed, the questions went on for more than three hours. One Pharisee climbed onto a lampstand and mockingly asked how Jesus could give them his flesh to eat and his blood to drink. Jesus explained that he had not been teaching about literal flesh and blood, but about a reality of experience: the Word of God bestowed in the flesh and a life lived in subjection to the Father’s will. (153:2.13, 153:3.2)

You cannot eat my flesh nor can you drink my blood, but you can become one in spirit with me even as I am one in spirit with the Father. (153:3.2)

A Jerusalem spy then accused him of breaking the tradition of washing hands. Jesus pointed to the tradition that let undutiful children say the money that should have helped their parents had been “given to God,” and he charged them with making void the commandment of God by their own tradition. The spies had raised the question because a saying of Jesus had been reported to them. (153:3.3, 153:3.4, 153:3.6)

Salvation is a matter of clean hearts rather than of clean hands. (153:3.6)

Even the apostles did not grasp his meaning, and Peter asked him to explain, lest some of the hearers be unnecessarily offended. Jesus replied that no one can compel men to love the truth, so they should turn their attention to those who would know it. He added the warning that when the blind lead the blind, both fall into the pit. For anyone worn out with trying to persuade others, these words show where to spend one’s strength. (153:3.5)

The text adds that such beliefs, once they become part of one’s religion, are hard to get away from. Even Peter remained in bondage to fears about things clean and unclean until many years later, when he was finally delivered through a vivid dream. Knowing that an open rupture with these leaders could no longer be prevented, Jesus spoke out with less reserve and exposed the folly of the whole system of the oral law. (153:3.6, 153:3.7)

IV. Last Words in the Synagogue — Our Choices Make Us

A Pharisee brought forward a youth possessed of an unruly and rebellious spirit, and Jesus, moved with compassion, took him by the hand and sent the spirit out. The text explains that this was the first case in which Jesus really cast an evil spirit out of a human being, and that such possession became forever impossible after Pentecost, when the Master’s spirit was poured out upon all flesh. The Pharisees then charged that he did these things by the power of Beelzebub. (153:4.1, 153:4.2)

Jesus answered that a kingdom divided against itself cannot stand, and he solemnly warned those who would knowingly and with premeditated malice ascribe the works of God to devils. Such persons will never seek nor receive forgiveness, and so they reject divine forgiveness themselves. He then made plain the weight of choice with the figure of the tree and its fruit. (153:4.3, 153:4.4)

And as you now choose, so shall you eventually be. (153:4.4)

When asked for a predetermined sign, Jesus answered that no sign would be given beyond what they already had and what they would see when the Son of Man departed. The apostles, amazed and somewhat terror-stricken at this militant manner they had never seen in the Master, led him home in silence to Bethsaida. (153:4.5, 153:4.6)

V. The Saturday Evening — The Sifting and the Pledge of Loyalty

The apostles had seen their hopes dashed many times before, but no disappointment had ever equaled this one. Near midnight Joab reported that about one third of the evangelists had deserted, and others brought word of a general revulsion of feeling toward the Master. Yet of the twelve women gathered at Peter’s house, not one deserted. (153:5.1, 153:5.2)

A little after midnight Jesus stood before about thirty of them and told them this sifting was unavoidable; why grieve when a new day was dawning? He said he had known from the beginning that the halfhearted believers would fall out by the way, and he urged each of them to look to his own faith, for one of them stood in grave danger. (153:5.3, 153:5.4)

it is the spirit that quickens; the flesh and all that pertains thereto is of little profit. (153:5.4)

When Jesus asked whether they too would desert, Peter answered that though they were sad and perplexed they would never forsake him, for he had taught them the words of eternal life. All of them nodded their approval of that pledge, and Jesus told them to go to their rest, for active days were just ahead. (153:5.4, 153:5.5)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 153 records the day when success measured in numbers collapsed, yet it is not a record of failure but of sifting. Jesus offered the bread that gives life to the soul instead of bread that fills the belly, and he asked for purity of heart instead of purity of hands. Faced with that demand, people either left or stayed, and the choice of those who stayed was the fruit of many small choices made over time. (153:1.3, 153:5.3)
