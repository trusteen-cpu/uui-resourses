Introduction — Seven Natures of Deity and Unending Growth

Like Paper 117, Paper 118 was sponsored by a Mighty Messenger temporarily sojourning on Urantia. Where the previous paper portrayed God the Supreme himself, this one asks how the Supreme and the Ultimate are bound up with time and space, causation and will, and providence. It starts from a list that sets out the several natures of Deity at a glance. (118:0.1, 118:10.24)

As God the Sevenfold is indispensable to the attainment of the Supreme, so the Supreme is indispensable to the emergence of the Ultimate. Together they form the experiential bridge linking the beginnings and completions of all creative growth in the master universe. The author explains this growth with an image from numbers: just as one can always be added to any number, growth has no end—yet it is punctuated by satisfying moments of goal attainment, and those moments become the prelude to new adventures. (118:0.9, 118:0.10, 118:0.12)

I. Time and Eternity — The Time Unit of Maturity

Absolute Deity is eternal by nature, but in the evolutionary universes eternity is experienced as the “everlasting now.” A mortal can become eternal by identifying with the indwelling spirit through choosing to do the Father’s will. The creature’s purpose then becomes fixed; a million or a billion moments make no difference. (118:1.1, 118:1.2)

A distinctive insight of this section is that it describes maturity in terms of the length of one’s unit of time. We make decisions in the present by weighing past experience and future wisdom together. As the self matures, it reaches farther into past and future, and its decisions are freed more and more from the fetters of the moving present. (118:1.3, 118:1.4, 118:1.5)

Patience is exercised by those mortals whose time units are short; true maturity transcends patience by a forbearance born of real understanding. (118:1.6)

On infinite levels the present contains all the past and all the future. To God as absolute, an ascender who has made the eternal decision is already a finaliter. Yet through the Adjuster the Father does not stop at that knowledge; he takes part in every struggle of the creature rising from animallike to Godlike levels. (118:1.9, 118:1.10)

II–III. Omnipresence and Ubiquity; Time-Space Relationships

God the Supreme may not be a demonstration of time-space omnipresence, but he is literally a manifestation of divine ubiquity. Ascenders discern God progressively through the ministry of God the Sevenfold, discover God the Supreme in Havona, find him as a person on Paradise, and as finaliters seek to know him as Ultimate. The assurance that whatever level they reach they will encounter the same Paradise Father keeps finaliters undisturbed. (118:2.2, 118:2.3)

We perceive time by analysis and space by synthesis, and we join the two by the integrating insight of personality. Of all the animal world only man has this capacity. And truth rises above the conditions of time: the more truth we know and the more truth we are, the better we understand the past and grasp the future. (118:3.1, 118:3.2)

Things are time conditioned, but truth is timeless. (118:3.2)

When the eternal truth of the Creator is linked with the factual experience of the creature, a new value of the Supreme emerges. On space, the author is humble: space comes nearer than any other nonabsolute thing to being absolute, yet it is not absolute, and whether the pattern of an idea occupies space, he confesses, is really not known. (118:3.4, 118:3.5, 118:3.7)

IV. Primary and Secondary Causation

Many theological difficulties arise from attributing infinite and absolute qualities to subordinate Divinity and to evolutionary Deity. There is a true First Cause, but there are also a host of co-ordinate and secondary causes. A first cause produces original effects that inherit nothing from antecedent causes; a secondary cause always yields effects that show inheritance from what went before. (118:4.1, 118:4.2)

In the cosmic sense, then, Creator Sons are transformative creators, yet from the finite viewpoint they certainly do create. This distinction does not diminish God’s greatness; it gives his many co-workers their rightful place. (118:4.7)

V–VI. Omnipotence and Compossibility; Omnipotence and Omnificence

Omnipotence is not the power to do the nondoable. Even the infinite God does not make square circles or evil that is inherently good, for such contradictions amount to nonentity. Omnipotence not only creates things but gives origin to the very nature of things. (118:5.1)

God cannot do the ungodlike thing. (118:5.1)

In the beginning the Father does all, but as eternity unfolds, creatures—even men—become God’s partners in realizing destiny. Consciousness also flows in opposite directions. Man moves from fact to meaning to value; the Creator moves from thought-value through word-meaning to the fact of action. That is why God always seeks man first. (118:5.2, 118:5.3)

Always must God first find man that man may later find God. (118:5.3)

God is omnipotent, but he is not omnificent. To claim that he personally does all that is done would disenfranchise nearly a million Creator Sons and their countless helpers. Human will, too, is relative: man cannot choose to be other than human, but he can choose to be more than a man. The choice to find God and be like him alone transcends finite limits. Recognizing omnipotence brings security; accepting omnificence leads into the error of pantheism. (118:6.1, 118:6.2, 118:6.7, 118:6.8)

He cannot, for instance, choose to be other than a human being except that he can elect to become more than a man (118:6.5)

VII. Omniscience and Predestination — Foreknowledge Does Not Erase Freedom

The Master Architects have set the maximum limits within which will can operate, but within those bounds the sovereignty of creature will is not diminished. A wise adult may accurately forecast a younger associate’s decision without making that decision any less free. Mistaken choice is time bound, and it reveals the range of choice that immature creatures must be given if they are to make free contact with reality. (118:7.1, 118:7.3)

Only as a creature becomes God identified, does he become truly real in the universes. (118:7.5)

True freedom carries even the possibility of self-destruction. So on the lower levels choice is narrowed for safety, and as one ascends the universes choice grows freer, until, in identification with God’s will, it approaches divine freedom. (118:7.7, 118:7.8)

VIII. Control and Overcontrol — The Time Governor and Self-Imposed Restraints

Man is a living mechanism, but he is much more than a machine: he is mind endowed and spirit indwelt. He can never escape the chemical mechanics of the body, yet by consecrating his mind to the urges of the Adjuster he can increasingly subordinate that machine to the wisdom of experience. (118:8.2)

There is a reason evolution is slow. Time is a brake on dangerous velocities of progress, and when material achievement outruns worship-wisdom, civilization carries the seeds of retrogression. The iniquity of Caligastia and of Lucifer lay in trying to break this time governor prematurely. So whenever we overcome an outward restraint, we must put in its place a voluntarily assumed restraint: concepts of justice and ideals of brotherhood. (118:8.6, 118:8.7, 118:8.9, 118:8.10)

he achieves this transformation by the force and constancy of his own personality decisions, in each of life’s situations declaring, “It is my will that your will be done.” (118:8.11)

IX. Universe Mechanisms — Time and Space That Insulate and Limit

Time and space are the devices by which finite creatures can coexist with the Infinite. Without them no creature could act, yet by them every act is limited. Mechanisms are the fixed crystallizations of Creator thought and always function true to the concept that gave them origin; their purposiveness lies in their origin, not in their function. (118:9.1, 118:9.5)

The grand universe is both mechanism and organism, a living mechanism. The author sums up God the Supreme as the personalization of all universe experience, the focalization of all finite evolution, and the consummation of cosmic wisdom. In the eternal future he will synthesize the manifold finite diversities into one meaningful whole. (118:9.4, 118:9.9)

X. Functions of Providence — A Slow and Sure Emergence

Providence does not mean that everything has been decided in advance; that would be cosmic tyranny, not love. Providence works with regard to the total, and intervention occurs not because of a creature’s importance as a person but because of the importance of its function to the growth of some whole. Nevertheless, the Father as a person may at any time put a fatherly hand into the stream of events. (118:10.1, 118:10.5, 118:10.6)

Providence does not mean that God has decided all things for us and in advance. (118:10.1)

What we call good luck may be bad luck, and an apparently cruel fate may be the tempering fire that forges character. Providence is partial and unpredictable, yet it is neither whimsical nor magical. (118:10.9, 118:10.21, 118:10.22)

When men pray for providential intervention in the circumstances of life, many times the answer to their prayer is their own changed attitudes toward life. (118:10.23)

The kingdom of God is in the hearts of men, and when it becomes real in the heart of every person on a world, the sovereignty of the Supreme Being is attained. (118:10.16, 118:10.17)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 118 is full of difficult philosophical terms, but beneath them lies a single message. We are limited by time and mechanism, yet those limits protect us, and when we identify ourselves with the will of God, true freedom and stability come together. Providence is not luck; it is the direction of the universe, revealed more and more to those who walk that way. (118:10.18)
