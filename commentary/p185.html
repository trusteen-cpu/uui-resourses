Introduction — Six O’Clock on Friday Morning

A little after six o’clock on the morning of Friday, April 7, A.D. 30, Jesus was brought, his hands bound, before Pontius Pilate, the Roman procurator who governed Judea, Samaria, and Idumea. About fifty accusers went with him, among them Caiaphas and other members of the Sanhedrist court, together with Judas, and the Apostle John also came along. Annas did not appear. (185:0.1)

The public hearing took place on the steps outside the governor’s headquarters, because the accusers refused to enter a gentile building on the day of preparation for the Passover; doing so would have made them ceremonially unclean. The narrator points out the contradiction of men who plotted a judicial murder while remaining scrupulous about ritual cleanness, and then adds that they were not the only ones to fall into it. (185:0.2, 185:0.3, 185:0.4)

And these Jews have not been the only ones to fail in the recognition of high and holy obligations of a divine nature while giving meticulous attention to things of trifling importance to human welfare in both time and eternity. (185:0.4)

This sentence is a key to the whole paper. The narrative records the failings of particular people, but it widens those failings into a trap that all human beings can easily fall into. Keeping the form while missing the substance can happen to religious people of any age. (185:0.4)

I. Pontius Pilate — Capable, but a Moral Coward

Pilate was a fairly good administrator, good enough to hold his post for ten years, yet the text calls him a moral coward. He never grasped that the people he governed had a real religion, a faith for which they were willing to die. Having lost prestige through his early blunders, he gave way again and again before the threat of revolt. (185:1.1, 185:1.2)

Although he was a fairly good administrator, he was a moral coward. (185:1.1)

The text tells us that Pilate never fully recovered from regret and guilt over having consented to the crucifixion of Jesus. His wife, Claudia Procula, had heard of Jesus through her Phoenician maid, a believer in the gospel, and after her husband’s death she gave herself to spreading the good news. (185:1.6, 185:1.7)

The narrator remarks that a good procurator would not have allowed a man he had declared innocent to be put to death, and he judges that it was a great mistake for Rome to send a second-rate governor to Judea. It is a sober reminder of how far the moral courage of a single person can reach. (185:1.9)

II. Jesus Appears Before Pilate — A Death Sentence Without a Charge

When Pilate asked what charge they brought, the accusers only replied that they would not have delivered this man if he were not an evildoer. They wanted nothing more than confirmation of a sentence. Pilate tried to send them back to judge Jesus by their own law, and he took some pleasure in making them admit publicly that they had no authority to put anyone to death. (185:2.1, 185:2.2, 185:2.5)

I will not sentence this man to death without a trial; neither will I consent to examine him until you have presented your charges against him in writing. (185:2.8)

The written charges handed over by the clerk accused Jesus of perverting the nation and stirring up rebellion, of forbidding the people to pay tribute to Caesar, and of calling himself a king and teaching a new kingdom. The text stresses that Jesus had never been regularly tried or legally convicted on any of these charges. When the indictment was read, Jesus made no reply. (185:2.10, 185:2.14, 185:2.15)

Pilate was astonished at the unfairness of the proceedings, and he was deeply stirred by the way Jesus looked down upon his accusers. There was no scorn in that look. It held deep pity and sorrowful affection. (185:2.15, 185:2.16)

III. The Private Examination by Pilate — “What Is Truth?”

Pilate took Jesus and John into a private room, had them sit down, and questioned the prisoner. He said plainly that he did not believe the first charge. On the question of tribute, John testified that Jesus and his apostles had paid taxes both to Caesar and to the temple. On the third charge — was he a king? — Jesus explained the nature of his kingdom. (185:3.1, 185:3.2)

Jesus said that his standing before Pilate in bonds was enough to show that his kingdom was a spiritual dominion. It is the brotherhood of men who, through faith and by love, have become the sons of God, and it is open to the gentile as much as to the Jew. Then he declared the purpose for which he had been born: to show the Father to all men and to bear witness to the truth of God. (185:3.3, 185:3.4)

And even now do I declare to you that every one who loves the truth hears my voice. (185:3.4)

Half in ridicule and half in sincerity, Pilate asked what truth was. He could not understand a spiritual kingdom, but he was sure the prisoner had done nothing deserving death. He went out and announced that he found no crime in him. Yet when he heard that Jesus had begun his work in Galilee, he seized the chance to escape responsibility and sent him to Herod. (185:3.5, 185:3.6, 185:3.7, 185:3.8)

Truth, what is truth—who knows? (185:3.5)

IV. Jesus Before Herod — A King Who Wanted a Show

Herod Antipas was very curious about Jesus and hoped to see a miracle with his own eyes. He did not recognize the man before him as the lad who long before had come to plead for a just settlement of the compensation owed for his father’s accidental death at Sepphoris. For about fifteen minutes he questioned and mocked Jesus, but Jesus did not answer a single word. (185:4.1, 185:4.2)

In the end Herod jeered at Jesus, put an old purple royal robe on him, and sent him back to Pilate. He knew he had no jurisdiction in Judea, and he was relieved that the responsibility for any execution would rest with Pilate. The fear that had haunted him since he killed John the Baptist also eased when he saw that Jesus was a very different kind of man. (185:4.3)

Jesus’ complete silence before Herod deserves attention. To Pilate, who asked with some sincerity, he explained his kingdom; to Herod, who wanted the spectacle of a miracle, he showed nothing at all. Truth is not a tool for satisfying curiosity; it is given to the heart that seeks it. (185:4.2, 185:3.4)

V. Jesus Returns to Pilate — Between Barabbas and Jesus

Pilate announced once more that Herod had reached the same conclusion, that Jesus had done nothing worthy of death, and that he would chastise him and release him. Just then a crowd arrived asking for the customary Passover release of a prisoner. Pilate saw this as a way out of his difficulty, but the crowd shouted the name of Barabbas, a political agitator and murderous robber. (185:5.1, 185:5.2, 185:5.3)

The text explains why the crowd turned. When Jesus drove the traders from the temple he had been a hero to the multitude, but a nonresisting prisoner on trial for his life could not be their hero. Calling him “the king of the Jews” was an unwise move on Pilate’s part, for it wounded the pride of a people who were looking for a Messiah. (185:5.5, 185:5.6)

Pilate knew Jesus was innocent of the charges brought against him, and had he been a just and courageous judge, he would have acquitted him and turned him loose. (185:5.7)

At that very moment a message arrived from Claudia, urging him to have nothing to do with this innocent and just man. The letter only made Pilate hesitate further, and meanwhile the rulers moved among the crowd, urging the people to call for the release of Barabbas and the crucifixion of Jesus. (185:5.8, 185:5.13)

VI–VII. Pilate’s Last Appeal; Pilate’s Last Interview — “Behold the Man!”

The text makes clear that those now gathered before Pilate were only Jesus’ enemies and a thoughtless crowd easily stirred up. Many of his friends either did not know of his arrest or were hiding in fear. Pilate had Jesus flogged in the hope of arousing pity — itself an illegal act before any sentence of death had been passed. (185:6.1, 185:6.2)

The soldiers mocked him with a purple robe, a crown of thorns, and a reed. Pilate led him out, bloodstained, and declared again that he found no crime in him, but nothing could move hearts held captive by hatred and prejudice. When the high priest said that Jesus must die because he claimed to be the Son of God, Pilate grew more afraid than ever. (185:6.3, 185:6.4, 185:6.7)

This sight sent a mighty shudder through the realms of a vast universe, but it did not touch the hearts of those who had set their minds to effect the destruction of Jesus. (185:6.4)
You could have no power over me except it were permitted from above. (185:7.2)

Jesus told Pilate that he was not so guilty, since he was ignorant of the gospel; the one who betrayed him and those who delivered him had the greater sin. Pilate sought to release him, but Caiaphas warned that if he set this man free he was no friend of Caesar, and threatened to report him to the emperor. Fear for his own position overwhelmed every other consideration. (185:7.2, 185:7.3, 185:7.5)

Pilate was now pressed down by a double weight: a superstitious fear of Jesus himself and his fear of the Jewish leaders. Pointing to Jesus, he cried out in mockery, “Behold your king!” and the chief priests and Sadducees publicly declared that they had no king but Caesar. The text records that even the thoughtless mob was shocked by these words. (185:7.3, 185:7.5, 185:8.1)

VIII. Pilate’s Tragic Surrender — Washing the Hands Does Not Wash Away Responsibility

The narrator sums up the whole trial in a single sentence: every step of proper procedure was missing. (185:8.1)

Pilate feared a riot during the Passover, and he had recently been reprimanded by Caesar. He released Barabbas, then washed his hands in a basin and declared himself innocent of the blood of this man. But the text has already called him an unjust and fearful judge. The responsibility for a decision cannot be washed away with words. (185:8.2, 185:5.9)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

At the center of Paper 185 stands Pilate. He knew the truth, declared Jesus innocent more than once, and even looked for a way to release him. Yet because he feared for his position and his safety, he never did what was right. The paper shows that cowardice can produce greater injustice than malice. (185:7.3, 185:5.7)
