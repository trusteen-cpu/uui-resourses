Introduction — A Planetary Prince Who Came Late

Paper 66 is presented by a Melchizedek of Nebadon, and with it the history of Urantia enters an age in which celestial administration meets human society directly. On an average world the advent of the Planetary Prince, a Lanonandek Son, is a sign that will—the ability to choose the path of eternal survival—has developed in the mind of primitive man. On Urantia, however, the Prince arrived almost half a million years after human will appeared. (66:0.1, 66:8.8)

Caligastia arrived about five hundred thousand years ago, at the time the six Sangik races appeared. Almost one-half billion primitive human beings were scattered over Europe, Asia, and Africa, and the Prince’s headquarters was set up in Mesopotamia, near the center of world population. The paper describes in detail the work done at that headquarters while quietly tracing the weakness of one personality that brought it all down. (66:0.2)

I. Prince Caligastia — A Brilliant Record and a Hidden Pride

Caligastia was a Lanonandek Son, number 9,344 of the secondary order, experienced in the administration of the local universe. Lucifer elevated him to his personal staff, and he acceptably filled five successive assignments of honor and trust. He sought a commission as Planetary Prince very early, but his request repeatedly failed to win the assent of the Constellation Fathers before he was finally assigned to Urantia. (66:1.1, 66:1.2, 66:1.3)

The author was present on Jerusem when Caligastia departed. No prince of the planets, he recalls, ever set out with a richer preparatory experience or better prospects. He leaves only one hint: a certain characteristic restlessness and a tendency to disagree with the established order in minor matters. (66:1.4, 66:1.5)

I did not then comprehend that Caligastia was insidiously falling in love with himself; I did not then so fully understand the subtleties of personality pride. (66:1.5)

II. The Prince’s Staff — The Caligastia One Hundred

The Prince did not come alone. At the head of his staff was his associate-assistant Daligastia, and with them came many angelic co-operators and other celestial beings. From our standpoint the most interesting group was the corporeal staff, the Caligastia one hundred. They were chosen from over 785,000 ascendant citizens of Jerusem who volunteered for the Urantia adventure, and each came from a different planet. (66:2.2, 66:2.3, 66:2.4)

Their bodies were prepared in a special way. The Life Carriers chose fifty males and fifty females of the best surviving strains of the Andon and Fonta stock, and a commission from Avalon extracted a portion of their life plasm and transferred it to the material bodies built for the staff. The whole repersonalization took exactly ten days. The text adds that these events gave rise to numerous legends, later confused with the traditions of Adam and Eve. (66:2.7, 66:2.8, 66:2.9)

III. Dalamatia — The City of the Prince

The headquarters lay in the Persian Gulf region of those days, in the district later known as Mesopotamia. The one great task of that age was to transform man from a hunter to a herder, and later into a peace-loving, home-abiding farmer. The city was named Dalamatia in honor of Daligastia; it was simple but beautiful, enclosed within a wall forty feet high. (66:3.1, 66:3.2, 66:3.3)

The city was laid out in ten subdivisions, each with the headquarters of one council at its center, and centermost stood the temple of the unseen Father. Buildings were made mostly of brick, and the homes and villages of the surrounding peoples were greatly improved by following this example. The staff continually gathered the superior individuals of nearby tribes, trained them, and sent them back as teachers and leaders of their own peoples. (66:3.4, 66:3.6, 66:3.8)

IV. Early Days of the One Hundred — Threefold Beings and the Primary Midwayers

The arrival of the staff made a profound impression, and much later mythology grew out of garbled legends of those days. The serious obstacle was the tendency of mortals to regard them as gods. Yet the one hundred did not resort to supernatural methods or superhuman manipulations. Even so, they were extraordinary beings in three respects. (66:4.1, 66:4.2, 66:4.3)

In the thirty-third year at Dalamatia, number two and number seven of the Danite group accidentally discovered a phenomenon attending the liaison of their morontia selves. The result was the first of the primary midway creatures, and the whole staff, following the same method, brought into being the original corps of 50,000 primary midwayers. The complement of the system circuits was also introduced into the hundred Andonites who had given their germ plasm, so that they lived on with the staff century after century and remained at headquarters as personal attendants. The tree of life grew in the central courtyard of the temple of the unseen Father. (66:4.13, 66:4.15, 66:4.16)

The one hundred were organized into ten autonomous councils of ten members each, and when two or more met together Daligastia presided. A look at the work of each council shows at a glance what the foundations of civilization are made of. (66:5.1)

VI. The Prince’s Reign — Evolution, Not Revolution

When the one hundred arrived, the whole world was caught in the stalemate of tradition-bound mores. Tradition gives stability and co-operation but stifles initiative and enslaves creative powers. The one hundred proclaimed a new gospel of individual initiative, yet they did not try to transform the primitive races suddenly. Though they knew the arts and culture of Jerusem, they did not simply transplant them. (66:6.2, 66:6.3)

The text warns that overteaching and overenlightenment bring confusion and dismay and can rob people of the little progress they have gained over long ages. When we help others, pace and respect matter just as much as good intentions. (66:6.6, 66:6.7)

VII. Life in Dalamatia — Home, School, and the Father’s Way

The city was beautiful but modest, and the land within its walls could provide pasture and gardens for about twenty thousand people. No methods were used at this headquarters that did not naturally belong on Urantia. The staff had no children of their own, but their fifty pattern homes sheltered no fewer than five hundred adopted children, who after the Prince’s schools were sent as emissaries to the tribes of their own races. (66:7.1, 66:7.2, 66:7.5)

The order of settled family life also dates from this time. The home never succeeded as a social unit until people learned to love and plan for their grandchildren and their grandchildren’s children. The school Fad sponsored was an industrial school where pupils learned by doing, valuing both individual training and socialization in groups. (66:7.4, 66:7.6)

Savage man loves his child, but civilized man loves also his grandchild. (66:7.4)

VIII. Misfortunes of Caligastia — Pride That Ended in Betrayal

Looking over Caligastia’s long career, the author finds only one outstanding feature: he was ultraindividualistic and mildly resented all forms of supervision. Yet whenever a test came he proved loyal, so no real fault was found in him before his betrayal. Both Lucifer and Caligastia were patiently instructed and lovingly warned about their critical tendencies and pride, but they misconstrued this help as unwarranted criticism and interference with their liberty. (66:8.1, 66:8.2)

They judged their unselfish advisers by their own evolving selfishness. (66:8.2)

For almost three hundred thousand years after the Prince arrived, planetary civilization progressed in a fairly normal manner, but at the Lucifer rebellion Caligastia went into darkness. He was deprived of sovereign authority, his power was greatly curtailed by the incarnation of Machiventa Melchizedek, and in the days of Michael he was finally shorn of all authority. He now awaits the final adjudication of the Ancients of Days. (66:8.3, 66:8.4, 66:8.5, 66:8.7)

What the paper stresses last is the dignity of human free will. The teaching that a personal devil can overpower the normal human mind against its own choosing is, the text says, wholly fictitious. Even the indwelling Adjuster refuses to compel a single thought or act against man’s own will. (66:8.6)

The free will of man is supreme in moral affairs; (66:8.6)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 66 shows how concrete the foundations of civilization are: wells and food storage, domestic animals and the wheel, letters and schools, hygiene and law, and homes where grandparents love their grandchildren. It also shows that all of this can be undone by the hidden pride of a single leader. Yet nothing can replace or take away the moral choices human beings make for themselves. (66:7.4, 66:8.6)
