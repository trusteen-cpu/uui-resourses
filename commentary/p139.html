Introduction — Twelve Human Temperaments

Paper 139 is a gallery of portraits, one for each of the twelve apostles. The narrator begins by stressing one fact. Jesus repeatedly dashed the apostles’ hopes and personal ambitions, yet only one of them deserted him, and this is itself a testimony to the charm and righteousness of his life. (139:0.1)

The apostles learned from Jesus about the kingdom of heaven, and Jesus learned much from them about the kingdom of men, human nature as it lives on Urantia and on the other evolutionary worlds of time and space. (139:0.2)

We should not think of the apostles as ignorant men. All but the Alpheus twins were graduates of the synagogue schools, and seven came from the Capernaum synagogue schools, the best in Galilee. To call them “unlearned” means only that they were laymen, untrained in the lore and interpretive methods of the rabbis. Readers of this paper will likely find, among the twelve, a face much like their own. (139:0.3, 139:0.4)

I. Andrew, the First Chosen

Andrew was the oldest and the ablest of the twelve. Except in oratory, he was the equal of his associates in almost every ability, a fine organizer and an even better administrator. He was the best judge of men among them, and he sensed trouble brewing in Judas’ heart before anyone else, though he told no one. His handicap was a lack of enthusiasm that made him sparing with praise, and this came from his abhorrence of flattery. (139:1.2, 139:1.8, 139:1.10)

He and Peter were very different in temperament, yet neither was ever jealous of the other. What Andrew said to his brother on the evening of Pentecost, when Peter’s preaching had added two thousand souls to the kingdom, captures this brotherhood well. (139:1.6)

I could not do that, but I am glad I have a brother who could. (139:1.6)

Soon after the ascension Andrew began writing a record of the Master’s sayings and doings. It was edited and altered many times, and the last copy was destroyed by fire at Alexandria. He was crucified at Patrae in Achaia and went on proclaiming the gospel for two full days before he died. (139:1.9, 139:1.12)

II. Simon Peter — A Riddle of Courage and Cowardice

Peter was impulsive and optimistic, a man who spoke before he thought. He stayed out of worse trouble only because he learned early to talk over his plans with his brother Andrew before making them public. He was the outstanding preacher of the twelve, and he asked more questions than all the other apostles put together. (139:2.3, 139:2.4, 139:2.9)

But, after all, Jesus knew that Peter’s faults were of the head and not of the heart. (139:2.6)

He could withstand direct assault but shrank before ridicule. So he let a servant girl tease him into denying his Lord, and later at Antioch he drew back before ridiculing Judaizers. Yet once he was sure that Jesus had forgiven him, he became a saving light to thousands. To the day of his death he remained confused between the concept of Jesus as the Jewish Messiah and Jesus as the revelation of the Father, and he was crucified in Rome. (139:2.6, 139:2.7, 139:2.10, 139:2.13, 139:2.15)

III–IV. James Zebedee; John Zebedee — From Sons of Thunder to the Apostle of Love

James had a fiery temper, which he liked to excuse as righteous indignation, and his great weakness was spells of unaccountable silence. Yet he had a rare ability to see all sides of a question, and of the twelve he came nearest to grasping the real meaning of Jesus’ teaching. He was the first apostle to be martyred, put to the sword by Herod Agrippa, and he bore himself so well that his own accuser, deeply moved, went off to join the disciples of Jesus. (139:3.2, 139:3.3, 139:3.4, 139:3.8, 139:3.9)

John was the youngest apostle. The text says plainly that it cannot truthfully be said he was “the disciple whom Jesus loved,” for so magnanimous a personality as Jesus would hardly show favoritism. His weakness was a hidden conceit, but the Master’s love and unselfishness reshaped the rest of his life. He was the one apostle who followed right along with Jesus on the night of the arrest, and the first of the twelve fully to believe in the resurrection. (139:4.2, 139:4.5, 139:4.6, 139:4.10)

My little children, love one another. (139:4.6)

In exile on Patmos he wrote the Book of Revelation, which survives only in greatly abridged and distorted form. At ninety-nine he directed Nathan in writing the Gospel according to John, and he died a natural death at Ephesus in A.D. 103, at the age of one hundred and one. (139:4.14, 139:4.15)

V–VI. Philip the Curious; Honest Nathaniel — “Come and See” and Honest Humor

Philip was an ordinary, matter-of-fact man who lacked imagination, yet through methodical thoroughness he had food on hand for the apostolic family on nearly every day of four years. Jesus never once rebuked his apparently foolish questions. He knew that a rebuke would wound this honest soul so deeply that he would never again feel free to ask. (139:5.2, 139:5.4, 139:5.7)

Even parents may learn from Philip the better way of saying to their children not “Go do this and go do that,” but rather, “Come with us while we show and share with you the better way.” (139:5.8)

Nathaniel’s virtue was an honesty “without guile”; his weakness was a pride that led him to prejudge people. Yet he was not obstinate, and he changed his mind the moment he looked into Jesus’ face. A philosopher and a storyteller, he eased the tensions among the apostles with humor. When Judas complained about him, Jesus answered that no one is competent to judge his brother, and that memory lingered long in Judas’ heart. (139:6.3, 139:6.4, 139:6.5, 139:6.6)

VII. Matthew Levi — The Hidden Benefactor

Matthew, the customs collector, was the only man of any means among the apostles. His presence among the twelve kept the doors of the kingdom wide open to outcast souls who had thought themselves beyond the reach of religious consolation. He liked to call the kingdom “this business of finding God.” (139:7.1, 139:7.4, 139:7.7)

Matthew gave practically his whole fortune to the work, but he gave much of it in the names of other believers, fearing that his associates might regard his money as tainted. Even when the disdain shown to a publican became plain, he always managed to keep still. (139:7.8)

He little realized that the Master knew all about it. (139:7.9)

VIII. Thomas Didymus — The Scientist Who Overcame Doubt

An unhappy childhood had left Thomas quarrelsome and suspicious. Yet he had the one truly analytical mind among the twelve; he was the real scientist of the group. In council he always argued for caution, but when he was outvoted he was the first to say “Let’s go!”—a good loser. (139:8.2, 139:8.3, 139:8.8)

Thomas’s membership in the twelve was a standing declaration that Jesus loved even honest doubters. (139:8.6)

What drew Thomas to Jesus was not any single trait but his matchless balance. The text describes it through a series of contrasts. (139:8.7)

Had Jesus and his work not been genuine, they could never have held a man like Thomas from start to finish. He learned that when he was downhearted it was better to stay at his work beside his associates than to go off alone, and he was put to death in Malta. (139:8.11, 139:8.12, 139:8.13)

IX–X. James and Judas Alpheus — The Least of the Apostles

The twins were ordinary fishermen with neither strong nor weak points. They understood little of the philosophical discussions, but they loved their Master and gladly took on the work of ushering the crowds and running errands. Common people took heart at seeing two men like themselves made apostles. After the crucifixion they returned to their families and their nets. (139:9.2, 139:9.3, 139:9.4, 139:9.11)

Jesus does not look down upon littleness, only upon evil and sin. (139:9.8)

XI. Simon the Zealot — From Rebel to Ambassador

Simon, once a fiery agitator, had an inspiring loyalty that could bring a wavering person to faith in about fifteen minutes. His great weakness was his material-mindedness. Jesus told him often that it was proper to want the social order improved, but that this was not the business of the kingdom; still, he never fully made an internationalist of him. Crushed after the persecutions, Simon rallied again and ended his life in the heart of Africa. (139:11.3, 139:11.4, 139:11.9, 139:11.10, 139:11.11)

XII. Judas Iscariot — A Faith Adventure and a Son of Resentment

Judas was probably the best-educated apostle and a matchless treasurer. Money was never the motive for his betrayal. The trouble was that he did not really understand himself and was not sincere in dealing with himself. Spoiled as a child, he had exaggerated ideas of his own importance, was a poor loser, and had cultivated a lifelong habit of getting even. (139:12.2, 139:12.5, 139:12.6)

To Jesus, Judas was a faith adventure. (139:12.7)

Jesus received him knowing the danger, because it is the way of the Sons of God to receive even a doubtful candidate fully. But light that is not honestly received and lived up to tends to become darkness within the soul. On the day his protest over the costly incense was publicly set aside, a lifetime of accumulated resentment was mobilized, and he went on to betrayal and suicide. Jesus regarded him only with pity. (139:12.7, 139:12.8, 139:12.10, 139:12.14)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

The portraits of the twelve show that Jesus did not choose finished men. The impulsive, the doubting, the unimaginative, the ordinary, the man with a questionable past: all were among them, and each was drawn to a different side of Jesus. Jesus was not afraid to identify himself with any kind of person. (139:1.11, 139:11.8)
