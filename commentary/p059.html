Introduction — A Quarter of the Planet’s History

Paper 59 is a Life Carrier’s chronicle of the marine-life era. The author begins by dividing Urantia’s history of about one billion years into five major eras, giving for each the name used by geologists. The marine-life era treated in this paper is the third of them, and it covers about one quarter of the planet’s history. (59:0.1, 59:0.7)

The marine-life era is further divided into six long periods, each marked by well-defined developments in both geology and biology. As the era opens, the sea bottoms and continental shelves are already covered with abundant vegetation, and simple animals have spread along the coast lines until the inland seas teem with life. The author describes this as the moment when the opening chapters of the great “stone book” of life’s record are about to be written. (59:0.7, 59:0.8)

North America serves as an especially good textbook for this chronicle. The continent is wonderfully rich in fossil-bearing deposits from the whole marine-life era, and its oldest layers are clearly set apart from the preceding period by extensive erosion deposits. That is why North American place names appear so often in this paper. (59:0.9)

I. Early Marine Life in the Shallow Seas — The Trilobite Age

By 400 million years ago marine life, both plant and animal, was well distributed over the whole world, and the climate grew warmer and more even. Vegetation for the first time crawled out onto the land. And then, with no gradual line of ancestors, the first multicellular animals appeared: the trilobites. (59:1.2, 59:1.3, 59:1.4)

Suddenly and without gradation ancestry the first multicellular animals make their appearance. (59:1.4)

Trilobites were poor swimmers; they floated sluggishly or crawled along the sea bottom, curling up for protection when attacked. They developed into four groups—carnivorous, herbivorous, omnivorous, and “mud eaters”—and the last group’s ability to live largely on inorganic matter explains its great increase and long survival. Traces of the three original implantations also remain: the early animals of the Western Hemisphere differed slightly from those of Eurasia and Australasia. Geologists call this period the Cambrian. (59:1.14, 59:1.19, 59:1.20)

The world climate in these times was oceanic, not continental. There were no great ice sheets, and the warm southern seas reached north to the polar regions. The Gulf Stream flowed across central North America and turned east to warm the shores of Greenland, making that now ice-covered land a veritable tropic paradise. The passage reminds us not to judge the past by today’s map. (59:1.17)

II. The First Continental Flood Stage — The Invertebrate-Animal Age

From 350 million years ago every continent except central Asia was flooded again and again. These risings and sinkings were gradual and undramatic, and the Asiatic mother continent followed a course of its own, becoming in recent ages the most stable of all land masses. The almost lone exception to this quiet time was the great eruption in eastern Kentucky some 330 million years ago. (59:2.1, 59:2.2, 59:2.5)

This was the great age of individual animal evolution, when every type of life below the vertebrates appears in the fossils. The ancestors of corals, jellyfish, cephalopods, and many other forms flourished. But apart from a few burrowing worms there were still no land animals, because the air held too much carbon dioxide. Geologists know this period as the Ordovician. (59:2.9, 59:2.11, 59:2.13)

Primarily, all animals except certain of the more primitive ones are directly or indirectly dependent on plant life for their existence. (59:2.9)

The trilobites were still prominent, existing in tens of thousands of patterns and serving as the forerunners of modern crustaceans; some had from twenty-five to four thousand tiny eyes. But by the end of the period they shared the seas with other invertebrates, and early in the next period they perished entirely. Even a form of life that dominates an age does not stay forever. (59:2.10)

III. The Second Great Flood Stage — The Coral Period — The Brachiopod Age

Three hundred million years ago another great submergence began. The seas teemed with lime-shelled life, laying down the first widespread limestone deposit, about a thousand feet thick on average. The deposits of this age hold much gas, oil, zinc, lead, and rock salt; the gas and oil came from plants and animals of the submerged lands. The rock over which Niagara Falls now flows also dates from this time. (59:3.1, 59:3.4, 59:3.9)

By 290 million years ago the sea had largely withdrawn, and early mountain building began on every continent. The greatest of these upheavals were the Himalayas of Asia and the great Caledonian Mountains, stretching from Ireland through Scotland to Spitzbergen. Violent eruptions followed in Europe, leaving alternating layers of lava and rock 25,000 feet thick in the region of the British Isles. (59:3.3, 59:3.6)

The trilobites declined rapidly, and cephalopods growing to fifteen feet long suddenly appeared and became masters of the seas. By the end of the epoch the seas were so salty that little life survived. Then, after the primitive water scorpions, true scorpions—the first real air breathers—suddenly appeared. This period is the Silurian. (59:3.5, 59:3.10, 59:3.11, 59:3.12)

This species of animal appeared suddenly and assumed dominance of sea life. (59:3.5)

IV. The Great Land-Emergence Stage — The Age of Fishes

For long ages the sea had held the upper hand, but now a time of land victory arrived. By 270 million years ago all the continents were above water, and the bare landscape was being clothed in green. The fern family suddenly appeared and spread everywhere, and leafless trees formed forests a hundred feet tall. (59:4.1, 59:4.2, 59:4.4, 59:4.13)

not a sound was heard, not even the rustle of a leaf, for such trees had no leaves. (59:4.17)

The greatest event of the age was the sudden appearance of the fishes. Vertebrates appeared 250 million years ago, descended from modified arthropods. Some toothed fishes reached twenty-five to thirty feet in length, and today’s sharks are their survivors. This period, called the Devonian, lasted almost fifty million years. (59:4.3, 59:4.9, 59:4.10, 59:4.11, 59:4.18)

This became the age of fishes, that period of the world’s history characterized by the vertebrate type of animal. (59:4.3)

As the land rose, North America was joined to Europe by land bridges reaching to Greenland, and Greenland still holds the remains of these early land plants beneath its ice. Volcanic activity in the St. Lawrence region 230 million years ago left Mount Royal at Montreal, and the Susquehanna River exposes over 13,000 feet of this epoch’s layers. (59:4.14, 59:4.16)

V. The Crustal-Shifting Stage — The Fern-Forest Carboniferous Period — The Age of Frogs

The appearance of fish was the high point of marine evolution; from here on, the evolution of land life grows ever more important. When the seas were at their height, about 210 million years ago, the first land animals suddenly appeared: air-breathing amphibians developed from arthropods whose swim bladders had become lungs. Snails, scorpions, and frogs crawled out of the sea onto the land, and insects soon spread over the continents. (59:5.1, 59:5.5, 59:5.6, 59:5.7)

From this point onward the evolution of land life becomes increasingly important. (59:5.1)
The life features of the coal age were ferns and frogs. (59:5.23)

Coal is the water-preserved and pressure-modified remains of the rank vegetation of bogs and swamp shores. Tree roots remain in the clay beneath coal beds just as they grew, proving that coal formed exactly where it is found. Each time the land sank and rose a new layer was laid down, so the number of coal layers records the number of those movements. (59:5.14, 59:5.16, 59:5.17)

The Carboniferous closed 180 million years ago as eastern North America rose, marking the beginning of the modern Appalachian and western mountains. The rising land began to replace the mild marine climate with a less mild, more variable continental one. The spore-bearing Carboniferous trees were often seven feet across and one hundred and twenty-five feet high, and modern ferns are relics of that vanished age. (59:5.20, 59:5.21, 59:5.22)

VI. The Climatic Transition Stage — The Age of Biologic Tribulation

The last period of the marine-life era was a time of great impoverishment of life. Its cause was not the cooling of the crust or volcanic activity, but an unusual combination of ordinary influences: shrinking seas and rising lands. The mild oceanic climate disappeared, harsh continental weather took its place, and two new climatic factors appeared—glaciation and aridity. (59:6.2, 59:6.3, 59:6.6)

Toward the close of the long marine-life era there were more than one hundred thousand species of living things on earth. At the close of this period of transition less than five hundred had survived. (59:6.2)

Yet this ordeal was not an ending but a sifting. Seed plants appeared for the first time, giving land animals a better food supply, and insects evolved resting stages to survive winter and drought. Frogs held on in drying pools and, in Africa, took the first step toward becoming reptiles. Marine animals sheltered in three refuges and later went forth from them as new species, born of adversity, to replenish the seas. (59:6.7, 59:6.8, 59:6.9)

The end of this Permian period is also the end of the Paleozoic, an era of two hundred and fifty million years. The sea nurtured life through the long ages when the land could not support it, and now it gives up that role as the second stage of evolution begins on land. (59:6.11, 59:6.12)

The vast oceanic nursery of life on Urantia has served its purpose. (59:6.12)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 59 tells how life climbed upward step by step through what looks like a tedious repetition of floods and uplifts. New orders of life appeared suddenly in age after age, and at the end came a trial that cut more than a hundred thousand species down to fewer than five hundred. Yet that trial left behind the forms with survival value to become the ancestors of the next age. Hardship can be a preparation rather than an end; this is the deepest lesson the paper leaves. (59:6.10)

Paper 60 turns to the early land-life era, when life that had left the sea flourished on land—the age of reptiles and dinosaurs. The note at the close of this paper, that a frog in Africa took the first step toward the reptile, is the thread that leads into that story.
