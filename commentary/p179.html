Introduction — A Passover One Day Early

Since the Jews reckoned the day as beginning at sunset, the regular Passover supper was due to be eaten on Friday night. Yet Jesus gathered his apostles on Thursday evening. They did not fully understand why. Some thought he knew he would be arrested before Friday; others took it as a special occasion preceding the regular Passover. (179:0.1, 179:0.2)

There was no lamb at this supper. The apostles knew that Jesus did not personally take part in any sacrificial service of the Jewish system, and that when he was the host no lamb was ever served. It had also been arranged beforehand that only the twelve would be present, so no servants were provided. This small fact sets the first scene of the evening. (179:0.3, 179:0.5)

I. The Desire for Preference — The Basin No One Picked Up

As the apostles entered the upper room, they saw the pitchers of water, the basins, and the towels just inside the door. With no servant present, each wondered who would wash their feet, and each thought it would not be he. Then they surveyed the seating at the table. Judas stepped over to the seat of honor at the left of the host, and John Zebedee claimed the place on the right. (179:1.2, 179:1.3, 179:1.4)

Peter, enraged, marched clear around the table and took the lowest couch. The text is candid about his motive. It was not only a protest; he hoped that Jesus would see him in the place of least honor, call him up to a higher one, and displace someone who had presumed to honor himself. This scene holds up a mirror to the reader: even an act that looks humble can hide a desire for position. (179:1.4)

The seating was finally settled: John on the Master’s right, Judas on his left, then Simon Zelotes, Matthew, James Zebedee, Andrew, the Alpheus twins, Philip, Nathaniel, Thomas, and at the very end Simon Peter. The narrator reminds us that they had gathered to celebrate an institution older than Moses, recalling the time when their fathers were slaves in Egypt. At a feast of liberation, they had made themselves slaves to the love of honor. (179:1.5, 179:1.6)

even in such a solemn setting, under the leadership of Judas the apostles are led once more to give way to their old predilection for honor, preference, and personal exaltation. (179:1.6)

While they were still exchanging angry recriminations, the Master appeared in the doorway. He hesitated a moment as a look of disappointment slowly crept over his face, but without comment he went to his place and did not disturb their seating. Their feet were still unwashed, and the mood in the room was anything but pleasant. (179:1.7, 179:1.8)

II. Beginning the Supper — Only One Anxiety

After a few moments of silence, Jesus relieved the tension with a smile. He told them he had wanted to eat with them once more before he suffered, and that the morrow was in the Father’s hands. After giving thanks over the first cup, he said he would not again drink the fruit of the vine with them until they sat down together in the kingdom to come. (179:2.1, 179:2.2)

as concerns the morrow, we are all in the hands of the Father, whose will I have come to execute. (179:2.1)

The text here discloses the Master’s inner life. He knew he had revealed the Father’s love, shown forth his mercy, and completed what he came into the world to do. He knew too that Judas’s betrayal also pleased Lucifer, Satan, and Caligastia. But he feared neither those who sought his physical death nor those who sought his spiritual overthrow. His one anxiety was for the safety and salvation of his chosen followers. (179:2.3)

III. Washing the Apostles’ Feet — The One Who Serves Is the Greatest

After the first cup, the Master quietly rose, removed his outer garment, girded himself with a towel, and poured water into a basin. He went to the lowest seat, where Peter reclined, and knelt in the attitude of a servant. All twelve rose as one man; even Judas for a moment forgot himself in astonishment. (179:3.1)

Peter, the first to believe wholeheartedly in Jesus’ divinity and to confess it publicly, could not bear to see his Master wash his feet like a slave. He refused outright. But when he heard that unless he accepted this service he would have no part in the Master’s work, he impulsively asked him to wash his hands and head as well. Judas, on the other hand, read this humility as proof that Jesus would never qualify as Israel’s deliverer and concluded that his own decision was right. (179:3.2, 179:3.3, 179:3.4, 179:3.5)

In silence Jesus went around the table and washed the feet of all twelve, not even passing by Judas. Then he returned to his place and asked them: if the Master has washed your feet, why were you unwilling to wash one another’s feet? Why, he asked, were they so slow to learn that the secret of greatness in the spiritual kingdom is not like the methods of power in the material world? (179:3.7, 179:3.8)

But why are you so slow to learn that the secret of greatness in the spiritual kingdom is not like the methods of power in the material world? (179:3.8)
He who would be great among you, let him become as the younger; while he who would be chief, let him become as one who serves. (179:3.9)
But you will observe that I am among you as one who serves. (179:3.9)

The Master said there could be no place of preferment at his table, for he loved each of them as he did the others. The place nearest him, as men regard such honors, meant nothing concerning their standing in the kingdom of heaven. The teaching still questions every community today. What shows a community’s spiritual level is not who sits in front, but who picks up the basin first. (179:3.9)

IV. Last Words to the Betrayer — The Sop

When the meal had become cheerful again, Jesus explained that, knowing of the plot against him, he had chosen to eat this supper a day early in this secret chamber. He said that his going to the Father was necessary, but it was not required that one of them should betray him. Disconcerted, they all asked, “Is it I?” (179:4.1, 179:4.2)

How deceitful is the intellectual pride that precedes the spiritual downfall! (179:4.2)

John leaned toward the Master and asked who it was, and Peter urged John across the table to find out. Jesus had already said plainly that it was the one to whom he gave the sop, yet they did not grasp it. Only Judas was painfully conscious of the meaning, and he feared that his brethren now knew him for the betrayer. The same words passed over those who loved the Master and pierced the one who was hiding his heart. (179:4.3, 179:4.4)

Dipping the bread in the dish of herbs, Jesus handed it to Judas on his left, saying, “You have said.” Since it was natural for the host to give a sop to the guest on his left, the others noticed nothing. Jesus said he sorrowed that this evil had come to pass and had hoped up to this hour that truth might triumph over the deceptions of evil. Then, leaning toward Judas, he told him to do quickly what he had decided to do, and Judas went out into the night. (179:4.3, 179:4.5, 179:4.6)

but such victories are not won without the faith of the sincere love of truth. (179:4.5)

The text says that this whole supper was Jesus’ last appeal to the deserting Judas. Up to that very hour he had done everything possible to save him, just as he had for the others. But when love is once really dead, even the kindest warning usually only intensifies hatred and drives the person to carry out his selfish plans to the full. This insight shows how important it is to turn back before the heart hardens. (179:4.7, 179:4.8)

V. Establishing the Remembrance Supper — Freedom Left in Symbols

Taking the third cup, the “cup of blessing,” Jesus said it would be the cup of his remembrance, the cup of a new dispensation of grace and truth. Then he broke the bread, passed it around, and said that he was the bread of life. The old Passover commemorated the emergence from slavery into freedom; the new supper symbolizes the emergence from the bondage of ceremonialism and selfishness into the brotherhood of the liberated faith sons of God. (179:5.1, 179:5.2, 179:5.3)

The text explains that Jesus deliberately used symbols rather than precise definitions. He wanted to keep later generations from binding his teaching with the dead chains of tradition and dogma, and he did not want to cramp the believer’s spiritual imagination with a fixed form. Yet over the centuries people reduced these simple symbols to a set formula of almost mathematical precision. None of his teachings became more tradition-standardized. (179:5.4, 179:5.5)

The remembrance supper is the believer’s symbolic rendezvous with Michael. (179:5.6)

The narrator also answers, briefly, the old debate about the Master’s presence at the supper. When those who are Son-believing and God-knowing partake, the Master is really present, so there is no need to attach to the symbols any puerile misinterpretations of that presence. When the believer becomes spirit-conscious, the Son’s spirit fraternizes with the indwelling fragment of the Father. What matters is not the bread and cup as objects, but the awakened spiritual consciousness of those who share them. (179:5.6)

This is the new Passover which I leave with you (179:5.9)

This great event took place in the upper room of a friend, without sacred form or ceremonial consecration, and without ecclesiastical sanction. Jesus told them that when they did this, they should first look back on his life in the flesh and then, by faith, look forward to the supper they would share in the eternal kingdom. Once more he urged them not to contend over who was greatest but to be as brethren. The company ended this bloodless Passover by singing the one hundred and eighteenth Psalm together. (179:5.7, 179:5.8, 179:5.9, 179:5.10)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 179 sets side by side the human craving for position, rising even on the most solemn night, and the Master kneeling in the midst of it. Jesus served before he rebuked, called the betrayer with love to the very end, and left even the way of remembering him in symbols, so as not to harm people’s spiritual freedom. In this paper the reader sees a faith in which service, freedom, and remembrance are woven together. (179:3.8, 179:5.4)
