Introduction — Echoes of Salem in the West

Following the Hebrew prophets of the previous paper, Paper 98 traces the path the Melchizedek teachings took in Europe and the Western world. They came chiefly by way of Egypt, were thoroughly Hellenized and later Christianized, and settled into Occidental philosophy. The Salem missionaries were gradually absorbed into many cults, but the group that kept their teachings in the purest form was the Cynics. (98:0.1, 98:0.2)

The teachings did not travel by one road only. The paper adds that much of the Salem doctrine was spread in Europe by Jewish mercenary soldiers who fought in many Western wars. Truth moves not only through the preaching of missionaries but through unexpected people and channels. (98:0.3)

The basic doctrines of Greek philosophy, Jewish theology, and Christian ethics were fundamentally repercussions of the earlier Melchizedek teachings. (98:0.4)

One question unlocks this paper: what grows from the seed of truth depends on the soil it falls into. In Greece the seed was received as philosophy, in Rome as state ceremony, and among the Mediterranean masses as mystery cult, each in its own shape.

I. The Salem Religion among the Greeks — Zeus Bound by Fate

The Salem missionaries kept the oath of ordination imposed by Machiventa: they would organize no exclusive congregations, never function as priests, and receive for their service nothing but food, clothing, and shelter. The author believes that, had they not interpreted this pledge so strictly, they might have built up a great religious structure among the Greeks. (98:1.1)

The Hellenic invaders brought anthropomorphic God concepts with them, and Dyaus-Zeus became head of the Greek pantheon. But because the Greeks kept Fate in control above the gods, they never reached a true monotheism. The intelligent Greeks neither greatly loved nor greatly feared the Olympian gods, and thanks to the antipriestcraft influence of the Salem teachers, no priesthood of any importance ever arose in Greece. (98:1.2, 98:1.3, 98:1.4, 98:1.5)

A God of final value must, himself, be the arbiter of fate and the creator of destiny. (98:1.3)

Greek mythology was more aesthetic than ethical. It helped in picturing a universe governed by a group of deities, but Greek morals, ethics, and philosophy soon far outran the god concept. The author warns that this imbalance between intellectual and spiritual growth proved as hazardous to Greece as it had been in India. (98:1.6)

II. Greek Philosophic Thought — When Believing Bows to Thinking

The Olympian religion, which promised no salvation, had nearly vanished within a thousand years. When the Orient and the Levant experienced a new awakening to monotheism in the sixth century before Christ, the Greeks looked instead for comfort in philosophy and metaphysics. They succeeded in mastering fear, but they did not see that true religion is the cure for soul hunger and moral despair. (98:2.1, 98:2.2)

They sought for the solace of the soul in deep thinking—philosophy and metaphysics. (98:2.2)

Ordinary people wanted not abstract philosophy but promises of salvation and a personal God who could hear their prayers. That longing poured into the mystery cults—the Eleusinian mysteries, Dionysus worship, the Orphic brotherhood—and the author records how quickly and deeply Greece fell from its heights of philosophy. The conclusion is that philosophy, religion, and science must be welded into one. (98:2.10, 98:2.11)

Philosophy is to religion as conception is to action. (98:2.12)

III. The Melchizedek Teachings in Rome — Religion in Service of the State

Latin religion had a strongly political character from the start. Too few Salem missionaries reached Italy, and the gods and temples of the Etruscan priesthood were organized into the Roman state religion. Rome took in the Greek gods wholesale—Zeus became Jupiter, Aphrodite became Venus—and the religious initiation of Roman youths was their solemn consecration to the service of the state. (98:3.1, 98:3.2, 98:3.3, 98:3.4)

Rome conquered politically but was conquered in turn by the cults of Egypt, Greece, and the Levant. Augustus tried to revive the old state religion, and after hearing of the earlier Salem attempts to teach one God, he ended by proclaiming himself the supreme god. The Cynics, who made the last stand for the Salem faith, were rejected by the people at large. (98:3.6, 98:3.7, 98:3.9)

The worship of Augustus flourished across the empire during his lifetime, except in Palestine. This era of human gods went on until the official cult counted more than two-score self-elevated human deities, all claiming miraculous births and superhuman attributes. Once the idea of one God fell into the hands of power, it was turned to the exaltation of men as gods. (98:3.7, 98:3.8)

The emerging Roman state conquered politically but was in turn conquered by the cults, rituals, mysteries, and god concepts of Egypt, Greece, and the Levant. (98:3.6)

IV–V. The Mystery Cults; The Cult of Mithras — A Hunger for Salvation

Having lost their family and state religions and being unable to grasp philosophy, the masses of the Greco-Roman world turned to mystery cults that promised comfort now and immortality after death. The paper names the three most popular. (98:4.1, 98:4.2)

The common people craved promises of salvation—religious consolation for today and assurances of hope for immortality after death. (98:4.1)

Mithraism spread across the empire with Roman legions recruited in the Levant. By the time it reached Rome it had absorbed many of Zoroaster’s teachings and was much improved, and it was chiefly through this cult that Zoroaster’s religion later influenced Christianity. Its followers worshiped in caves, believed that partaking of the sacrament ensured eternal life, and held their most elaborate festival on December twenty-fifth. (98:5.1, 98:5.2, 98:5.4)

The Mithraic story included water gushing from a rock, a flood, a last supper before the god ascended into the heavens, and a final judgment. At first only men could be initiated, through seven successive orders; women were later admitted to the adjoining temples of the Great Mother. This fact connects with the point that decided the contest between the two religions in the next section. (98:5.3, 98:5.5)

VI. Mithraism and Christianity — What Decided the Contest

During the third century after Christ the two religions looked very much alike. Most places of worship were underground, and both practiced baptism and partook of the sacrament of bread and wine. Yet Mithraism encouraged militarism while Christianity was ultrapacific. The deciding factor lay elsewhere. (98:6.3, 98:6.4)

But the deciding factor in the struggle between the two was the admission of women into the full fellowship of the Christian faith. (98:6.4)

Here the author adds a balanced observation about institutions. The religions of Greece and Rome lacked any powerful agency for preserving higher values. It is true that institutionalization tends to lower the spiritual quality of religion, but no religion has yet survived without some degree of institutional organization. (98:6.1)

Greek philosophy supplied the concepts of ethical value; Mithraism, the ritual of worship observance; and Christianity, as such, the technique for the conservation of moral and social values. (98:6.5)

VII. The Christian Religion — A Faith Woven from Seven Strands

The final section begins by setting the purpose of the bestowal straight. A Creator Son did not take on mortal flesh to reconcile an angry God, but to win all mankind to the recognition of the Father’s love and the realization of their sonship with God. The paper then explains that Christianity took shape as a compound of many elements. (98:7.1, 98:7.3)

Notice the place of Greek philosophy in this list. The paper holds that Greek thought harmonized with Paul’s version of Christianity better than with any other religious system of the time, and that this was an important factor in Christianity’s success in the West. The missionary zeal of Jesus’ followers equaled that of the Sethites and Salemites and of their Buddhist contemporaries. (98:7.2, 98:7.10)

The author says that well-meaning teachers like Paul must not be held accountable for the use later generations made of their writings. Even so, he observes that as Christianity became Occidentalized it began to lose its potential appeal to all races and kinds of men. (98:7.9, 98:7.11)

It has glorified Jesus as the Christ, the Messianic anointed one from God, but has largely forgotten the Master’s personal gospel: the Fatherhood of God and the universal brotherhood of all men. (98:7.11)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 98 closes nearly four thousand years of the story of Machiventa’s teachings and judges that he achieved the purpose of his bestowal. When Michael came, the God concept already lived in human hearts, and it still flames anew in living spiritual experience today. The lesson the paper leaves is balance: thought without faith, or faith whose thinking has hardened, will both lose the way. (98:7.12)
