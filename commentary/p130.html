Introduction — On the Road with Gonod and Ganid

With Paper 130 the story of the Mediterranean journey begins. On April 26, A.D. 22, Jesus left Jerusalem with Gonod and his son Ganid, natives of India, and he said good-bye to them in December of the following year at Charax on the Persian Gulf. He spent about half of each day teaching Ganid and interpreting for Gonod, and he gave the rest to close personal contacts with the people he met along the way. (130:0.1, 130:0.5)

The narrator explains that in recounting these conversations he will freely translate Jesus’ words into the modern phrasing current at the time of this presentation. This paper covers the first half of the journey, up to the arrival in Rome. (130:1.4)

I. At Joppa — Discourse on Jonah and on Evil

At Joppa Jesus became warm friends with Gadiah, a young Philistine who worked as interpreter for Simon the tanner. When a great truth seeker meets a great truth giver, the narrator says, the result is a liberating enlightenment. Standing at the landing from which Jonah was said to have sailed, Gadiah asked whether Jesus believed the story of the big fish. Jesus saw that the story had impressed on the young man the folly of running away from duty, and he said nothing that would undermine that motivation. (130:1.1, 130:1.2)

My friend, we are all Jonahs with lives to live in accordance with the will of God (130:1.2)

His answer was that flight from duty leads to struggles with the whales of selfishness, but that those who seek God with a heart hungry for truth will be delivered from whatever depths they have fallen into and cast up on the dry land of fresh opportunities for service. At their last meeting Gadiah asked why a good God permits evil. Many still believed then that God creates both good and evil, but Jesus never taught such error. (130:1.2, 130:1.5)

Gadiah was fully satisfied with the answer. Later he heard Peter preach, became a profound believer in Jesus, and had much to do with Simon the leather merchant’s decision to embrace Christianity. This view of evil, not as God’s work but as the fruit of immature choices, leads the reader to learn better choosing rather than to blame God in the face of suffering. (130:1.3, 130:1.6)

II. At Caesarea — No Man Is a Stranger

While they waited at Caesarea because the ship’s steering paddle was in danger of cleaving, Jesus helped make a new one. At the inn he had long talks with a Taoist merchant from Mongolia, who went home and taught advanced truths about the One God. The narrator takes the occasion to regret that the early church sent no one like Peter or Paul into Asia. (130:2.1, 130:2.2, 130:2.3)

Anaxand, a young Greek in the shipyard, asked why the Gods, if they cared about him, did not remove his cruel foreman. Jesus answered that perhaps the man had been brought near so that Anaxand might lead him into a better way, and he encouraged him that the good in him could overcome the evil in the other. That night Anaxand and his foreman sought Jesus’ advice together. (130:2.4, 130:2.5)

It is a marvelous and transforming experience to become the living channel of spiritual light to the mortal who sits in spiritual darkness. (130:2.4)
no man is a stranger to one who knows God. (130:2.6)

When Ganid asked why he spent so much time visiting strangers, Jesus replied that no man is a stranger to one who knows God. He also explained that doing God’s will is the progressive experience of partnership with God’s choice and of becoming more and more like him. Watching a shepherd dog, he taught that the dog has a mind and a kind of will, but lacks the power to know God, who is spirit, or to choose eternal values; after that day Ganid gave up his belief in the transmigration of human souls into animals. (130:2.6, 130:2.7, 130:2.8)

Will is that manifestation of the human mind which enables the subjective consciousness to express itself objectively and to experience the phenomenon of aspiring to be Godlike. (130:2.10)

III. At Alexandria — The Lighthouse and the Library

Before the lighthouse of Pharos, Jesus told Ganid that when he returned to India he should be like that lighthouse to those who sit in darkness, showing them the way to the harbor of salvation, and Ganid said he would. In the library, with its nearly one million manuscripts, the two discussed the religions of the world day after day. Jesus pointed out the truth in each, while always adding that Yahweh was the God developed from the revelations of Melchizedek and the covenant of Abraham. (130:3.2, 130:3.4)

Under Jesus’ direction Ganid collected the teachings of the world’s religions that recognized a Universal Deity, and he was surprised to find how much the best authors of sacred literature agreed about the eternal God. One day while Jesus was interpreting lectures at the museum, Ganid said he ought to teach the professors themselves, but Jesus smiled and declined. (130:3.5, 130:3.6, 130:3.7)

The true teacher maintains his intellectual integrity by ever remaining a learner. (130:3.7)

They hoped to hear Philo, who was trying to harmonize Greek philosophy and Hebrew theology, but he lay sick throughout their stay. Jesus commended much in Greek philosophy and Stoic doctrine to Ganid, yet he impressed on him that such systems were religions only in the sense that they led men to find God and enjoy a living experience in knowing the Eternal. (130:3.9, 130:3.10)

IV. Discourse on Reality — Knowledge, Truth, and Wisdom

On the night before they left Alexandria, after meeting a professor who lectured on Plato, Jesus gave Ganid a long account of universe reality. Material things are the time-space repercussions of the Paradise Pattern and the Universal Mind of God, and the creature becomes one with the Creator only by consistently conforming his will to the Creator’s will. Just as a one-eyed person cannot see depth, neither the material scientist alone nor the spiritual mystic alone can see the true depths of reality. (130:4.2, 130:4.3, 130:4.4)

He also explained evil as a relativity concept. Error is the penalty of imperfection, the shadow of relative incompleteness that must fall across man’s ascending path to Paradise. And he warned that all static, dead concepts are potentially evil: fixed ideas invariably retard science, politics, society, and religion. (130:4.11, 130:4.13, 130:4.15)

V–VI. On the Island of Crete; The Young Man Who Was Afraid

In Crete, when Ganid asked why he did not become a public teacher, Jesus replied that only time ripens green fruit and that his tomorrow was wholly in his Father’s hands. At Fair Havens he drew a slave girl away from a drunken man who was attacking her and held the man off with his outstretched arm until he had exhausted himself; Ganid found it hard to understand why Jesus did not strike him. (130:5.3, 130:5.4)

Time alone will ripen the green fruit upon the tree. (130:5.3)

In the mountains Jesus met Fortune, a young man gripped by fear and a sense of inferiority. First he asked the youth for directions, letting him gladly share what he knew; then he offered in turn to show him the way to the destiny he was seeking in his heart. He reminded him that he had a strong body, a clear mind, and above all an indwelling spirit. (130:6.1, 130:6.2, 130:6.3)

Fortune later became the leader of the Christians in Crete. At Cyrene, Jesus and Ganid gave first aid to a boy named Rufus, injured when an oxcart broke down; his father, Simon, never dreamed that the man whose cross he would one day carry was the stranger who had once befriended his son. (130:6.5, 130:6.6)

VII. At Carthage — Discourse on Time and Space

On the way to Carthage, Gonod and Ganid discovered that Jesus was a good storyteller and that he had been reared in Galilee. When Ganid asked how to make friends, Jesus told him to take a real interest in others, learn to love them, and watch for chances to do something for them. (130:7.1, 130:7.2)

Become interested in your fellows; learn how to love them and watch for the opportunity to do something for them which you are sure they want done (130:7.2)

At Carthage he talked with a Mithraic priest about time and eternity. Time is the stream of temporal events perceived by creature consciousness, and human personality, indwelt by the Father’s spirit, is the only physically related reality that can transcend the material sequence of events. As man ascends, what once appeared as a line of events is seen more and more as a related whole, and the time-space concept increasingly approaches the timeless and spaceless concepts of the Absolutes. (130:7.3, 130:7.4, 130:7.5, 130:7.8)

VIII. On the Way to Naples and Rome — Encounters of a Single Day

On Malta, Claudus, who had contemplated taking his life, resolved to face life again. At Syracuse the backslidden Jew Ezra poured out his despair at being unable to find God. At Messina they stayed only a day, yet Jesus encouraged a young fruit vendor to learn to feed his soul as well as his body. (130:8.1, 130:8.2, 130:8.3)

Your trouble is not that you cannot find God, for the Father has already found you (130:8.2)

In Naples they saw much poverty and gave many alms, and they spread good cheer with smiles to hundreds of men, women, and children. Then, by way of Capua, they traveled the Appian Way toward Rome, the capital of the empire. (130:8.4, 130:8.5, 130:8.6)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Most of the encounters in Paper 130 look brief and accidental. Yet Jesus began with what each person already held—a story about Jonah, a knowledge of mountain trails, a few pieces of fruit—and led them from there to higher thoughts. Several of those he met later became workers for the gospel, without ever knowing who the stranger had been. (130:6.2, 130:8.1)
