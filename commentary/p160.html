Introduction — A Philosopher Meets the Gospel

On September 18, after the Decapolis mission, most of the apostles went home for a week and Jesus enjoyed almost complete rest. Meanwhile Nathaniel and Thomas were busy in discussion with Rodan, a Greek philosopher from Alexandria. Rodan had become a disciple through the mission of one of Abner’s associates and was working to harmonize his philosophy of life with Jesus’ teachings. (160:0.1)

Jesus declined to enter the discussion himself but told the two apostles to listen to all Rodan had to say and to tell him about the gospel in return. Rodan gave ten addresses to some two dozen people, and this paper presents them condensed and restated in modern phraseology. We should therefore read these words not as Jesus’ own teaching but as the reflections of a philosopher who had embraced the gospel. (160:0.1, 160:1.1)

I. Rodan’s Greek Philosophy — Maturity and Problem Solving

Rodan saw human life as made up of three great drives: urges, desires, and lures. Strong character is acquired by raising each of these to a higher level. (160:1.2)

Source 160:1.2
Drive of lifeWhat strong character does with it
UrgesConverts the natural urge of life into the social art of living
DesiresTransforms present desires into higher longings capable of lasting attainment
LuresTransfers them from conventional ideas to unexplored ideas and undiscovered ideals

The more complex civilization becomes, the harder the art of living, and mankind must learn it anew every ten generations—perhaps every generation. Animals make no inquiry into the purpose of life, but man not only grasps meanings; he is conscious of the meaning of meanings. Rodan named discouragement, worry, and indolence as evidence of moral immaturity, and said that mature people view the immature with the love parents bear their children. (160:1.3, 160:1.5, 160:1.6)

Rodan explained social maturity by the same principle. A society’s maturity shows in how far it gives up present desires in pursuit of permanent goals. Beyond that, its true badge is the willingness to leave the ease of established beliefs to pursue undiscovered ideals. Between the comfort of familiar ideas and the discomfort of seeking new truth, maturity always chooses the latter. (160:1.4)

The first step in solving any problem is to locate the difficulty and frankly acknowledge it. Yet when a problem stirs our fears or requires us to give up conceit and prejudice, people prefer to cling to old illusions. Beyond this, one also needs the tact and tolerance that win the cooperation of others. (160:1.7, 160:1.9)

Only a brave person is willing honestly to admit, and fearlessly to face, what a sincere and logical mind discovers. (160:1.7)

The greatest method of problem solving Rodan had learned from Jesus was the habit of withdrawing alone for worshipful meditation. It gives not only strength for everyday conflicts but energy for solving moral and spiritual problems. He added that prejudice is bound up with selfishness and disappears through devotion to a cause greater than oneself. (160:1.10, 160:1.13)

Stability of personality can be experienced only by those who have discovered and embraced the living God as the eternal goal of infinite attainment. (160:1.14)

II. The Art of Living — What Friendship and Marriage Give

What distinguishes man from the animal is the ability to communicate meanings, values, and ideals through symbols. This ability develops personality and gives rise to culture and civilization. The most effective social group is the family, and personal affection is the spiritual bond that holds it together. Rodan said that relationships of friendship and mutual affection contribute four things to the art of living. (160:2.1, 160:2.3, 160:2.4, 160:2.5)

Source 160:2.6
What friendship givesRodan’s explanation
Mutual self-expression and self-understandingNoble impulses die with no one to hear them; recognition and appreciation are essential to character
Union of souls — the mobilization of wisdomPooling insight guards against prejudice and narrowness; the reason the Master always sent them two and two
Enthusiasm for livingIsolation drains the soul’s energy; friendship adds joy and stirs the imagination
Enhanced defense against evilTroubles weigh more when borne alone; association greatly lessens suffering
Fear, envy, and conceit can be prevented only by intimate contact with other minds. (160:2.7)

Rodan said he did not hesitate to glorify family life, since Jesus had made the father-child relationship the cornerstone of the gospel, and he saw marriage as the place where such association finds its ideal possibilities. Ideal marriage must rest not on fluctuating sentiment or the fickleness of sex attraction but on genuine mutual devotion. When such small units are gathered together, a civilization of maturity becomes possible. For people today worn down by isolation, the insight that deep friendship and a faithful home are the soil of spiritual growth is a practical one. (160:2.6, 160:2.10)

III. The Lures of Maturity — Worship as the Source of Energy

Maturity requires work, and work requires energy. Rodan stated flatly that this energy does not come from emotional excitement, which instead exhausts both mind and body. Pointing to Jesus, who was even then out in the hills taking in power, he said the secret lies in spiritual communion—in worship. (160:3.1)

And this interchange of strength for weakness, courage for fear, the will of God for the mind of self, constitutes worship. (160:3.1)

Meditation brings the mind into contact with spirit, and relaxation determines the capacity for spiritual receptivity. As these experiences are repeated they become habits of worship; habits form spiritual character, and character becomes a mature personality. His remark that such practices are difficult and time-consuming at first but become restful and timesaving once they are habits is a realistic encouragement for anyone beginning a life of prayer and meditation. (160:3.1, 160:3.2)

Rodan believed there are times when one must fight for what is right, but he said the Master would gain the same victory gracefully through tact and tolerance. All too often, when we battle for the right, both victor and vanquished suffer defeat. His observation that we often enter a fight merely to convince ourselves that we are not afraid is a sharp one. (160:3.3, 160:3.4)

wise man, when seeking entrance through the locked door, would not destroy the door but rather would seek for the key wherewith to unlock it. (160:3.4)

Rodan said the gospel supplies a new and richer incentive for higher living. The very concept of an eternal and divine goal calls forth the best in man and lifts him above lower irritations such as worry, jealousy, and revenge. But he cautioned that the life purpose must be guarded from the temptation of easy and transient attainment and made immune to the threats of fanaticism. (160:3.5)

IV. The Balance of Maturity — Making a Living and the Art of Defeat

The spirit is our goal, but the flesh is a fact. Rodan named the two major problems of life as making a temporal living and achieving eternal survival, and said that even making a living requires religion for its ideal solution. He listed six essentials of temporal life. (160:4.1, 160:4.2)

Source 160:4.3
Essentials of temporal lifeRodan’s emphasis
Good physical healthBody and mind are the dwelling place of the gift of the Gods
Clear and clean thinkingThe mind mediates between material things and spiritual realities
Ability and skillAbility is inherited, skill acquired; skill is a source of satisfaction
Wealth — the goods of lifeIndustry alone is not enough; wise planning and organized channels are needed
Ability to withstand defeatLearn to lose cheerfully; never hide failure
Culture — education and wisdomWisdom dominates knowledge and glorifies culture

Rodan urged his hearers to treasure in memory the strength-giving episodes of life and so build up reserve galleries of beauty and goodness. The noblest of these memories are the recollections of the great moments of a superb friendship. (160:4.12)

He laid special stress on the art of defeat. Always claiming success and hiding failure behind smiles creates a world of unreality that ends in the crash of disillusionment. Wisdom comes only from the experience of adjusting to the results of one’s failures. (160:4.13, 160:4.14)

Men who prefer optimistic illusions to reality can never become wise. (160:4.14)

Because wisdom embraces both fact and ideal, it saves us from two barren extremes: idealism that excludes facts and materialism without spiritual outlook. Rodan also called skill a real source of satisfaction in living, and advised that even as pleasure seekers we should be producers as well as consumers. (160:4.11, 160:4.14)

To such men defeat is but a new tool for the achievement of higher levels of universe reality. (160:4.15)

A life whose whole temporal enterprise appears a failure may prove a great success in the light of eternity, provided each failure yielded wisdom and spirit achievement. This attitude—neither denying failure nor being crushed by it—offers deep freedom in an age that measures people by results. (160:4.16)

V. The Religion of the Ideal — The Word God and the Reality of God

For Rodan, religion is the experience of reacting to something one regards as worthy of the devotion of all mankind. So when something has truly become one’s religion, one has already become its evangel. He called religions based on fear, emotion, tradition, and philosophy intellectual religions, and those based on true spirit experience true religions. (160:5.1, 160:5.3)

Source 160:5.6
Point of contrastLower religionHigher religion
BasisFear, emotion, tradition, philosophyTrue spirit experience
GodThe word God, the idea of GodThe ideal of God, the reality of the eternal God
DirectionShapes the idea of God to suit the natural state of the human heartDemands that the human heart be changed to meet the ideals of true religion
ResultA religion of wishful imaginationLiving spiritual experience and real spirit progression

Rodan held that Jesus’ religion transcends all earlier ideas of worship, because Jesus not only portrayed the Father as the ideal of infinite reality but declared that he is personally attainable by every mortal who enters the kingdom. Rodan confessed that he himself had now set out as a believer. (160:5.7, 160:5.8)

My brethren, I am a believer, I have embarked (160:5.8)

Rodan did not hide the cost. To enter the kingdom one must be born again and be willing to undergo the terrible and testing process of self-destruction and soul reconstruction. Only after paying the price of dedication to the Father’s will do we experience great peace on the path of consecrated living. Read alongside his statement that true religion seeks to convert the individual and transform the world, this shows that faith is a call to change before it is a comfort. (160:5.5, 160:5.10)

Just now, our focalized concept of the Father, as held in our hearts, is that God is spirit; as conveyed to our fellows, that God is love. (160:5.11)

Finally Rodan said that the impulse to be like God, the emotion of worship, the conviction to serve God, and the reasoning that this religion is best are not, in themselves, true religion. All of these must be made personal by the revelation of the Spirit of Truth. It is a call not to leave religion at the level of thought or feeling but to make it living experience. (160:5.12, 160:5.13)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 160 records how one thoughtful man absorbed the gospel into a philosophy of living. Rodan confessed that his philosophy had given him the urge to search but no driving power or direction, and that Jesus’ gospel supplied the insight, ideals, and settled goals he lacked. He shows that philosophy and faith need not exclude each other but can complete each other. (160:1.15)
