Introduction — Born of Fear, Not Reverence

Paper 87 examines how the ghost idea discussed in the previous paper hardened into a cult. Its author, a Brilliant Evening Star of Nebadon, says that primitive religious observances arose not from reverence for Deity but from anxiety about bad luck and an inordinate fear of the dead. Their rites were mostly negative, designed to avoid, expel, or coerce ghosts. (87:0.1)

The ghost cult was nothing more nor less than insurance against disaster; it had nothing to do with investment for higher and future returns. (87:0.1)

The author says that nothing is more pitiful than the sight of man in bondage to ghost-spirit fear, yet he holds that this very fear set mankind on the road of religious evolution. His remark that human imagination, having left the shores of self, will not find its anchorage until it arrives at the concept of a true Deity invites us to read the whole paper as the starting point of a long voyage. (87:0.2)

I–II. Ghost Fear and Ghost Placation — The Roots of Funeral Customs

To primitive man, death meant that one more ghost had been released from a body. Ghosts were thought to have supernatural power but no intelligence, so people used all kinds of tricks to deceive them. A house where someone had died was torn down or abandoned, and this fear delayed the appearance of solid houses and permanent villages. (87:1.1, 87:1.2, 87:1.3)

The first acts of human worship were defensive. Just as people today insure against fire, the savage thought it wise to insure against the bad luck ghosts might bring. Because ghosts had to be quickly “laid” and sent off to the land of the dead, people feared that even a small mistake in the ritual could bring disaster from an angry ghost. (87:2.1, 87:2.2)

The first acts of human worship were phenomena of defense, not reverence. (87:2.1)

This fear exacted a high price. Self-mutilation in mourning, unproductive mourning that consumed weeks or months every year, and hired professional mourners all show that mourning was a ritual, not grief. Believing that ghosts wanted their wives and servants, people buried or killed them along with the dead, and broke or burned tools and property. Civilization took a great step forward when inheritance by relatives replaced the burning and burying of property, and the Iroquois, who reformed funeral waste, became the most powerful of the northern red men. (87:2.4, 87:2.5, 87:2.7, 87:2.10)

Moderns may mourn the dead out of respect and because of bereavement, but the ancients did this because of fear. (87:2.5)

The belief that ghosts enjoyed the smell of food made funeral food offerings universal, and throwing a little food into the fire before a meal while muttering a formula was primitive man’s way of praying. Compared with today’s grace at the table, it suggests how far the same gesture has traveled, from fear to gratitude. (87:2.9)

III–IV. Ancestor Worship; Good and Bad Spirit Ghosts — The Gains and Costs of Dualism

The ghost cult inevitably led to ancestor worship, which became the link between common ghosts and the higher spirits, the evolving gods. Adopting children was a way to make sure someone would make offerings for one’s soul after death, and the Romans held twelve ghost feasts a year. (87:3.1, 87:3.3, 87:3.4)

The early gods were simply glorified departed humans. (87:3.1)

At first people believed in only one kind of ghost, which brought good luck when pleased and bad luck when angered. Later they imagined spirits that had advanced beyond ghostland to higher realms, and finally the dual spiritism of good and bad spirits arose independently all over the world. The author observes that the power of an idea over the mind lies not in its reality but in its vividness and the ease of its application. (87:4.1, 87:4.2, 87:4.3)

Dualism was a great advance in religious philosophy, but it was also a heavy burden. The idea of good and evil as cosmic equals has survived into the twentieth century, and the author says that most of the world’s religions still carry this cultural birthmark of the age of the ghost cults. (87:4.6, 87:4.7)

The tragedy of all this lies in the fact that, when these ideas were taking root in the primitive mind of man, there really were no bad or disharmonious spirits in all the world. (87:4.7)

V. The Advancing Ghost Cult — Envious Spirits and Prosperity Insurance

Primitive man believed that spirits had only rights and no duties, and that men had only duties and no rights. Even the smallest misfortune was blamed on ghosts, and after making offerings to every known spirit, people still did not feel safe, so they made one more to “the unknown gods.” Religious organization developed as a self-adjustment to the spirit environment, just as industry was an adjustment to nature and marriage to the demands of bisexuality. (87:5.1, 87:5.2)

Religion represents man’s adjustment to his illusions of the mystery of chance. (87:5.2)

Good spirits were neglected on the assumption that they would do their work anyway, while great effort went into keeping bad spirits pleased. Prosperity in particular was thought to arouse the envy of spirits, and fear of the evil eye spread almost world-wide. People disclaimed praise and gave themselves ugly names; this custom was later refined into courtesy and modesty, but for a long time it hindered the development of art. (87:5.3, 87:5.4, 87:5.7)

Early humility was not, therefore, debasement of ego but rather an attempt to foil and deceive the envious spirits. (87:5.6)

Under this system life became a gamble that depended on the favor of spirits rather than on effort and talent. People tried to discover the will of the spirits through omens, dreams, and divination, not out of curiosity but in a struggle to dodge bad luck. The author points out that remnants of these superstitions linger even among civilized races. (87:5.8, 87:5.9, 87:5.14)

Slow, very slow, is man to abandon those methods whereby he so gradually and painfully ascended the evolutionary scale of life. (87:5.14)

VI. Coercion and Exorcism — From Placating to Commanding

Once the idea of good and bad spirits took hold, religion moved beyond the negative stage of bribing spirits to avert bad luck to a positive stage of trying to compel their co-operation. To make up for his helplessness before forces he could not control, man looked for techniques to even the odds in his one-sided struggle with the cosmos. (87:6.1, 87:6.2)

Noise, foul smells, frightening images, and above all water became means of keeping ghosts away. The custom of circling the corpse with water led to holy water, and in primitive times bathing was a religious ceremony rather than a matter of hygiene. Exorcism was the use of one spirit to control another, and vows and oaths, fasting and self-denial were also regarded as ways to compel reluctant spirits, becoming the roots of asceticism. (87:6.11, 87:6.12, 87:6.13, 87:6.16)

Modern man no longer attempts openly to coerce the spirits, though he still evinces a disposition to bargain with Deity. (87:6.17)

The observation that knocking on wood, crossing one’s fingers, and the archaic style reserved for prayer come from the same roots invites readers to look at their own religious habits. It asks whether our relationship with God is turning into a bargain or an incantation. (87:6.14, 87:6.17)

VII. Nature of Cultism — Symbols Must Grow

The cult has endured because it provided symbols that preserve and stimulate moral sentiment and religious loyalty. Yet while the cult guards feeling, it has also been the greatest obstacle to social reconstruction and spiritual progress. The author regrets that so many modern believers in moral ideals have no adequate symbolism to share and lean on together. (87:7.1, 87:7.2, 87:7.3)

But a religious cult cannot be manufactured; it must grow. (87:7.3)

When the cult was flexible and its symbols could expand, truth grew and spread rapidly. On the other hand, a cult that has lost its meaning degrades religion when it tries to take the place of philosophy or to enslave reason. Modern man is collectively scientific and averse to superstition but individually hungry for mystery, so a surviving symbolism must be one the individual can practice for himself and also share with others. (87:7.5, 87:7.9)

The author judges that the early Christian cult was the most effective and enduring ritual ever devised, but that in the scientific age it has weakened as many of its fundamental tenets were lost. Every new revelation of truth has given rise to a new cult, and when the religion of Jesus is restated, new symbols expressing the fatherhood of God and the brotherhood of man must grow as well. (87:7.4, 87:7.6)

The old cults were too egocentric; the new must be the outgrowth of applied love. (87:7.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 87 does not hide how low a place religion started from: fear and self-defense. Yet the story does not end in cynicism. The same fear sent mankind out on a voyage in search of a true God, and the cult appears not as something to discard but as a skeleton that must grow anew to support living spiritual experience. The task this paper leaves us is to check whether remnants of the ghost cult still linger in our own faith.
