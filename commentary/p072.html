Introduction — Why Look at a Neighboring Planet?

Paper 72 takes a form very rare in The Urantia Book. By permission of Lanaforge and with the approval of the Most Highs of Edentia, a Melchizedek describes the social, moral, and political life of the most advanced human race on a not far-distant planet of the Satania system. It is most unusual, the text says, for the system rulers to allow the affairs of one planet to be narrated on another. (72:0.1, 72:0.2)

The permission was granted because of the likeness between the two worlds. That planet too was led astray in the Lucifer rebellion by the disloyalty of its Planetary Prince; it received a Material Son shortly after Adam came to Urantia, and he also defaulted; and since no Magisterial Son has ever been bestowed there, it remains isolated. So this paper is less a curious tale of a distant star than a case study of how far a world with similar wounds can advance on its own. (72:0.2, 72:0.3)

I. The Continental Nation — Five Hundred Years of Political Maturing

The setting is an isolated continent about the size of Australia. Its population is about 140 million, a mixed race, predominantly blue and yellow, not yet fully blended but fraternizing well. The average length of life is ninety years, fifteen per cent higher than that of any other people on the planet. Heavy rains fall eight months of the year on high central mountains, favoring water power and irrigation, and the nation is self-sustaining, able to live indefinitely without imports. (72:1.1, 72:1.2, 72:1.3)

This nation was not ahead from the beginning. Thousands of years passed between the tribal stage and the rise of kings, and abortive republics, communal states, and dictators came and went endlessly. The turning point came about five hundred years ago, when one of three ruling dictators had a change of heart and volunteered to abdicate. A charter of liberty then grew up under a unified monarchy, the kings gradually became figureheads and finally disappeared, and the present republic has lasted two hundred years. (72:1.4, 72:1.5)

This growth continued until about five hundred years ago when, during a politically fermenting period, one of the nation’s powerful dictator-triumvirs had a change of heart. (72:1.4)

II. Political Organization — Divided Powers and a Three-House Legislature

The central government is a strong federation of one hundred comparatively free states. State governors and legislators serve ten-year terms and cannot be re-elected, and no city may have more than one million inhabitants. The federal chief executive is elected every six years by universal suffrage and is advised by a supercabinet of all living ex-chief executives. Much administrative work is carried on by ten regional authorities, each an association of ten states. (72:2.1, 72:2.2, 72:2.3, 72:2.8)

The judiciary is also twofold. Alongside the law courts—minor courts, state supreme courts, and the federal supreme court—stand the socioeconomic courts: parental courts, educational courts, and industrial courts. Having specialized tribunals for disputes in the home, the school, and the workplace reflects the idea that justice must reach every area of life, not only criminal law. (72:2.9, 72:2.13, 72:2.16)

III–IV. The Home Life; The Educational System — The Basic Institution of Civilization

These people regard the home as the basic institution of their civilization. It is against the law for two families to live under one roof, and attendance at parental schools of child culture is compulsory for both fathers and mothers. Fathers give almost as much attention to child culture as mothers do. All sex instruction is given at home, religious training is the exclusive privilege of parents, and there are no public places devoted exclusively to religious assembly. (72:3.1, 72:3.2, 72:3.4, 72:3.5)

The schools have no classrooms. Only one study is pursued at a time, and after the first three years every pupil becomes an assistant teacher of those below. Books are consulted to solve problems that arise in the school shops and farms. Permission to marry is granted only a year after notice of intention and after both partners present certificates from the parental schools, and the divorce rate is only one tenth that of the civilized races of Urantia. Learning and preparation for family life are closely joined to the realities of living. (72:4.1, 72:3.8, 72:3.9)

V–VII. Industrial Organization; Old-Age Insurance; Taxation — A New Balance of Work and Leisure

The author frankly admits that the industrial situation is still far from these people’s ideals. Yet workers are increasingly becoming shareholders, violence has been outlawed as a means of settling disputes, and industrial courts pass on all conflicts. Slavery was abolished over a century ago by the gradual method of freeing two per cent each year. The most recent development is a principle for dividing earnings. (72:5.1, 72:5.2, 72:5.3, 72:5.4)

People work six hours a day on a five-day week, four days of work and one of play. The profit motive is giving way to higher driving forces, and competition has shifted to play, skill, science, intellectual achievement, and social service. Even the richest man on the continent hurries after work to the school of statesmanship to qualify for public office. A new social disgust for idleness and unearned wealth is growing, and the people are learning to use their hard-won leisure for self-realization. (72:5.9, 72:5.10, 72:5.12)

Among this people public service is rapidly becoming the chief goal of ambition. (72:5.10)

There is also an effort to replace degrading charity with dignified old-age insurance. Pension funds come from one day’s earnings each month, bequests, the excess profits of compulsory labor in the mines, and half the income from natural resources. Betrayal of public trust is punished more heavily than any crime but treason and murder. In taxation, no property may be taxed more than one per cent of its value in a year, and the federal government cannot go into debt. How a government handles its people’s money reveals its morality. (72:6.1, 72:6.3, 72:6.9, 72:7.6, 72:7.7)

This nation is making a determined effort to replace the self-respect-destroying type of charity by dignified government-insurance guarantees of security in old age. (72:6.1)

The same spirit shows in matters of health and private life. In industry health receives first attention, but individual and family health remains a personal concern, and the government increasingly refrains from interfering in purely personal matters. Here we glimpse a sense of where public responsibility ends and personal freedom begins. (72:7.1, 72:7.2)

VIII–IX. The Special Colleges; The Plan of Universal Suffrage — A Democracy That Weighs Wisdom

Every candidate for public office must be a graduate of a school of statesmanship. These schools are of three classes—national, regional, and state—and the offices a person may hold depend on the level of degree earned. Judges of the federal supreme court must hold degrees from all of them. There are also schools of philosophy, institutions of science, professional training schools, and military and naval schools. (72:8.2, 72:8.3, 72:9.1)

About fifty years ago these people saw a serious weakness in universal suffrage and modified their voting plan. Every citizen votes in two groups, one by economic function and one by social and philosophic inclination, and territorial voting was abandoned. Those who have rendered great service or shown extraordinary wisdom, and heavy taxpayers, receive additional votes, but no one may cast more than ten. Only the election of the chief executive is a nation-wide ballot of one vote each. (72:9.2, 72:9.3, 72:9.5, 72:9.6)

The ideal state is organic, and every free and intelligent group of citizens represents a vital and functioning organ within the larger governmental organism. (72:9.7)

X–XII. Dealing with Crime; Military Preparedness; The Other Nations — The Limits of Superiority

Of this people’s methods of dealing with crime and defectives, the author himself says that some are pleasing but others will no doubt prove shocking to most Urantians. The text simply records severe measures—agricultural colonies, the death penalty, the prevention of reproduction among defectives—without offering them as a model for Urantia. In keeping with the text’s own attitude, this passage is best read as a reminder that even an advanced people’s institutions mix good and ill. (72:10.1, 72:10.3)

In defense, restraint stands out. Military training is always paired with education in a trade or profession, so no professional military class can form. The nation keeps a powerful defense, yet for over a hundred years it has not used it in an offensive war; in the last two centuries it has fought only nine defensive conflicts. And it gives far more attention to training statesmen, scientists, and philosophers. (72:11.2, 72:11.4)

They have become civilized to that point where they can vigorously defend civilization without yielding to the temptation to utilize their war powers in aggression. (72:11.4)

Yet on the other continents of this planet, eleven in all, the governments are decidedly inferior to the more advanced nations of Urantia. Now, for the first time, a great religious leader has arisen in this nation who advocates sending missionaries to its neighbors, and the author fears a repetition of the old mistake of forcing a superior culture and religion on others. Instead he suggests bringing the best of the neighboring peoples to be educated and then sending them home as emissaries of culture. (72:12.1, 72:12.2)

We fear they are about to make the mistake that so many others have made when they have endeavored to force a superior culture and religion upon other races. (72:12.2)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

This paper is not a blueprint for utopia. The author reminds us that the neighboring planet has had neither magisterial nor bestowal missions of the Paradise Sons, while Urantia, a bestowal world on which the Spirit of Truth has been poured out, is far better prepared to realize a planetary government and world-wide peace under law. The achievements of another world are not meant to discourage us but to make us aware of the greater spiritual resources we already have. (72:12.4, 72:12.5)
