Introduction — The Long-Awaited Moment

Just before noon on Sunday, January 12, A.D. 27, Jesus walked along the shore and called the apostles together two by two. The twelve went up into the highlands north of Capernaum, and for the first time all of them were silent; even Peter was lost in thought. This paper records that day’s ordination and sermon, along with the conversations of the following week and the service of consecration. (140:0.1, 140:0.2, 140:0.3)

What is commonly called the “Sermon on the Mount” is the ordination sermon of this day. The text asks us to read it not as the gospel itself but as an ordination charge given to the apostles. Keeping that distinction in mind makes it clear to whom the sermon’s high demands were first addressed. (140:4.1)

I. Preliminary Instruction — The Nature of the Kingdom

Before the ordination Jesus said more about the kingdom. It is an everlasting dominion established in the hearts of those who seek to do the Father’s will. The Father is the God neither of Jews alone nor of gentiles alone, and his power lies not in armies or riches but in the glory of the divine spirit. Faith alone will pass a person through the door, but to keep ascending one must bear the fruits of the spirit. (140:1.2, 140:1.3, 140:1.4)

You are not to go hence in the proclamation of the kingdom, saying, ‘it is here’ or ‘it is there,’ for this kingdom of which you preach is God within you. (140:1.5)

He told them that this small beginning of twelve commonplace men would grow until it filled the whole earth, and that people would recognize them less by their words than by their lives. So the solemn responsibility was laid upon them: just as he now represented the Father, they would soon represent him in the world. (140:1.7)

II. The Ordination — A Great Silence

Jesus had the twelve kneel in a circle around him and laid his hands on each head, beginning with Judas Iscariot and ending with Andrew. Then he prayed, committing them to the Father. When the prayer ended, the apostles embraced him one by one, but no one said a word. (140:2.1, 140:2.2, 140:2.3)

Love them and be with them as you have loved and been with me. (140:2.2)

The text describes the scene as the Creator of a universe placing the affairs of the divine brotherhood of man under the direction of human minds. Because a divine work was being entrusted to frail human hands, that silence expressed both honor and weighty responsibility. (140:2.3)

III–IV. The Ordination Sermon; You Are the Salt of the Earth

Jesus told them that now, as enlightened citizens of another, heavenly country, more would be required of them than before. Then he gave the eight beatitudes for people in distress and called the apostles the salt of the earth and the light of the world. He went on to urge them not to resist injustice with force, to love their enemies, and to save rather than judge. (140:3.1, 140:3.12, 140:3.14, 140:3.15)

You are commissioned to save men, not to judge them. (140:3.17)

He told them to know false prophets by their fruits and added that what matters for entering the kingdom is motive. Commenting on the sermon, the narrator points out that salt serves by being spent, making other things tasty, and that light, likewise, should not shine to draw attention to itself. He names negativism as the major error of modern religions. (140:3.19, 140:4.3, 140:4.5, 140:4.7)

The happy and effective person is motivated, not by fear of wrongdoing, but by love of right doing. (140:4.6)

Personality is basically changeless, but moral character grows. Inherited urges cannot be fundamentally modified, yet our emotional responses to them can be changed. This insight offers real hope to any reader trying to improve his or her character. (140:4.7, 140:4.8)

V. Fatherly and Brotherly Love — The Shape of the Beatitudes

Jesus taught his followers to show fatherly love rather than brotherly love. Brotherly love loves the neighbor as oneself, which fulfills the golden rule. Fatherly love loves our fellow mortals as Jesus loves us. Jesus himself loved mankind both ways: as the Son of God with a fatherly love, and as the Son of Man with a brotherly love. (140:5.1, 140:5.2)

The text divides the beatitudes into two groups. The first four are faith attitudes that serve as a prelude; the last four are the supreme reactions of fatherly love, which go beyond the limits of brotherly love. (140:5.4, 140:5.5)

And so it is revealed that the beatitudes of the Sermon on the Mount are based on faith and love and not on law—ethics and duty. (140:5.23)

For each beatitude the text also offers a way of teaching it to children: preserve their simple trust, let them learn peace through play together, and help them learn sacrifice early. This part is especially practical guidance for parents and teachers. (140:5.14, 140:5.19, 140:5.22)

VI. The Evening of the Ordination — Motive Beyond the Deed

That night the apostles gathered Jesus into the garden to ask about what they had not understood. He told them it was hard for them because they tried to build the new teaching directly on the old. He had come not to destroy the law and the prophets but to fulfill, enlarge, and illuminate them, and to write a new commandment on the tablets of the heart. (140:6.2)

Asked about divorce, Jesus said he had come not to legislate but to enlighten; it was not the Father’s will to teach rules suited only to one age. When he said that those who found apostleship too hard could return to discipleship, Peter answered for all that they would go on with him to the end. Through the night the apostles went in one by one for a private talk with the Master, and when Jesus found the twins asleep by the fire, he said they were doing well and should not be awakened. (140:6.6, 140:6.9, 140:6.10, 140:6.14)

VII. The Week Following the Ordination — Two Great Purposes

Out of fear, the apostles asked to delay the departure for Jerusalem, and Jesus agreed to stay until the Sabbath. During that week the apostles took charge of teaching the visitors who came from Tyre, Sidon, Damascus, Jerusalem, and elsewhere. In these days Jesus repeatedly set forth the two purposes of his mission. (140:7.1, 140:7.2, 140:7.4)

Source 140:7.5

May your wisdom equal your zeal and your courage atone for your ignorance. (140:7.7)

VIII. Thursday Afternoon on the Lake — Attitudes Toward the World

Jesus spent four hours in a boat talking with Peter, James, and John, because the apostles still could not let go of the idea of joining the spiritual kingdom to the restoration of David’s throne. Based on what Peter later told Andrew, the conversation can be summed up under five topics. (140:8.1)

Source 140:8.2
TopicJesus’ teaching

Political attitudeAvoid political entanglements and respect civil law; he was not a political reformer
Social attitudeKindness that makes the whole world one’s neighbor, yet wary of indiscriminate kindness

But never make the mistake of identifying Jesus’ teachings with any political or economic theory, with any social or industrial system. (140:8.10)

The text says that if Jesus were on earth today he would disappoint many good men and women by refusing to take sides in political, social, or economic controversies. He knew that each age must find its own remedies for its troubles, and instead he taught the way to perfect the inner life of the people who must solve them. His religion also made no provision for introspective self-examination; he did not teach character building but character growth. (140:8.17, 140:8.27)

IX–X. The Day of Consecration; The Evening after the Consecration — Being Before Doing

On the following Sabbath Jesus returned to the highland where he had ordained them and called the apostles before him one by one to consecrate them. He commissioned them to set spiritual captives free and to comfort the oppressed, and while he foretold persecution, he told them not to fear. (140:9.1, 140:9.2, 140:9.3)

Freely you have received, freely give. (140:9.2)

That evening Jesus spoke at length about what they must be, not what they must do. They knew only a religion of doing things in order to become righteous, but in the kingdom one must be righteous in order to do the work. When Thomas asked about becoming like little children, Jesus explained that he meant not gullibility but the relationship between a child and a father. (140:10.1, 140:10.4)

When Simon Zelotes asked whether all men are the sons of God, Jesus said yes, and that this was the good news they were to proclaim. The apostles could not take in that teaching fully in words, but by living with him they gradually came to possess it. (140:10.7, 140:10.2)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 140 is the ordination of the apostles, yet it also hands today’s reader a philosophy of living. Jesus taught motive instead of rules, positive love instead of prohibition, and fatherly love beyond brotherly love. Rather than solving the world’s problems for us directly, he sought to renew the people who must solve them. (140:8.32)
