Introduction — The Story of Jesus, Told by Andrew’s Midwayer

With Paper 121 the narrative of Jesus’ life begins in earnest. The narrator is a secondary midwayer once attached to the Apostle Andrew, working under the supervision of a commission of twelve members of the United Brotherhood of Urantia Midwayers. He is authorized to record the life of Jesus as it was observed by his order and as it was partly written down by Andrew himself. (121:0.1)

Knowing how his Master so scrupulously avoided leaving written records behind him, Andrew steadfastly refused to multiply copies of his written narrative. (121:0.1)

This brief opening shows that the counsel Immanuel gave in the previous paper—to leave behind no writings and no images—was in fact honored. The other apostles took a similar attitude, and so the writing of the Gospels was long delayed. Before the story of Jesus begins, the paper first unrolls a map of the world into which he was born. (121:0.1)

I. The Occident of the First Century after Christ — The Most Favorable Time

Jesus did not come to this world during an age of spiritual decadence (121:1.1)

At the time of his birth Urantia was experiencing a revival of spiritual thinking and religious living unmatched in all its post-Adamic history. The narrator says that the world then offered the most favorable condition for a Creator Son’s bestowal that had ever existed before or has existed since. Western civilization was unified under three great influences, and one man, Paul, illustrates the whole combination well: a Hebrew of the Hebrews who proclaimed the gospel of a Jewish Messiah in the Greek tongue while himself a Roman citizen. (121:1.1, 121:1.2, 121:1.3)

Yet beneath the internal peace and surface prosperity, most of the empire’s inhabitants lived in squalor and poverty. A small upper class was rich, and a happy, prosperous middle class had only just begun to appear. These were the real conditions of the people whom the gospel would seek out. (121:1.7, 121:1.8)

II. The Jewish People — Crossroads of Three Continents

Palestine was the crossroads of three continents. The travel, trade, and armies of empires from Babylonia to Rome swept over this land in turn, and the caravan routes from the Orient passed through it to the seaports of the eastern Mediterranean. (121:2.2)

And more than half of this caravan traffic passed through or near the little town of Nazareth in Galilee. (121:2.2)

Under Roman suzerainty the Jews still enjoyed considerable self-government, and remembering the deliverance won by Judas Maccabee, they longed for a still greater deliverer, the Messiah. They took the good fortune of keeping their independence among stronger powers as proof that they were the “chosen people.” That sense of racial superiority made Roman rule all the harder to bear, and they refused to learn that their world mission was spiritual, not political. (121:2.7, 121:2.8)

Herod the Idumean built temples for strange gods, but he also built the harbor of Caesarea, which helped make Palestine the crossroads of the civilized world. His son Antipas rebuilt many Galilean cities, including the trade center of Sepphoris. When Jesus was born, Galilee was more gentile than Jewish and did not enjoy the full favor of the religious leaders in Jerusalem. (121:2.9, 121:2.11, 121:2.12)

III. Among the Gentiles — A Society of Five Strata

First-century Mediterranean society consisted of five well-defined strata. Half the population of the Roman state were slaves, and the early Christian church was largely composed of the lower classes and these slaves. (121:3.1, 121:3.6, 121:3.7)

Interestingly, people were generally content with their social rank and did not regard class distinctions as unjust. The narrator states plainly that Christianity was not a movement for correcting the economic lot of the depressed classes. The gospel turned first to the inner person, not to the structure of society. (121:3.9)

Christianity was in no sense an economic movement having for its purpose the amelioration of the miseries of the depressed classes. (121:3.9)

IV. Gentile Philosophy — A Way for the Strong and the Wise

Morally the gentiles were somewhat behind the Jews, but in the hearts of the nobler gentiles lay abundant soil of natural goodness and human affection in which the seed of the gospel could sprout. The gentile world was then dominated by four philosophies, all more or less derived from Platonism. (121:4.1)

While the Stoics professed to be the “offspring of God,” they failed to know him and therefore failed to find him. (121:4.3)

The Stoics sought to attune their minds to the harmony of the Universal Mind, but they never pictured themselves as the children of a loving Father. Here lies the difference between philosophy and religion. Lofty principles can discipline a person, but they cannot open a relationship with the Father. (121:4.3)

they were philosophies for the strong and the wise, not religions of salvation for even the poor and the weak. (121:4.6)

V. The Gentile Religions — The Mystery Cults and Beyond

In earlier ages religion had been chiefly a tribal or national affair and did little to satisfy the personal longings of the average person. In Jesus’ day the West had pagan cults, emperor worship, astrology, and the mystery religions flooding in from the Levant. The mysteries were nonnational and fraternal, with elaborate initiation ceremonies, and they promised deliverance from evil and survival after death; they quickly won over the lower classes. (121:5.1, 121:5.2, 121:5.9, 121:5.11)

The narrator reads the popularity of the mysteries as a real hunger for personal religion. They could not satisfy that longing, but they prepared the way for Jesus, who truly brought the bread of life. Paul adapted Jesus’ teachings to some extent to reach more people, yet even that compromise was superior to the best in the mysteries. (121:5.12, 121:5.13)

But do not make the mistake of confusing the teachings of Jesus with the mysteries. (121:5.12)

In the gentile world, moreover, morality was not necessarily tied to religion. The Jewish religion, the teachings of Jesus, and Paul’s Christianity were the first European religions to lay one hand on morals and the other on ethics, requiring believers to attend to both. (121:5.17)

VI. The Hebrew Religion — Hellenized Judaism and Philo

By the end of the first century before Christ, the religious thought of Jerusalem had been strongly shaped by Greek culture and philosophy. Three languages were in use in Palestine: Aramaic among the common people, Hebrew among priests and rabbis, and Greek among the educated. The Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures marked the renaissance of Judaism, and it later became the decisive influence that turned Paul’s Christianity westward rather than eastward. (121:6.1, 121:6.2)

Philo of Alexandria harmonized Greek philosophy and Hebrew theology into a fairly consistent system. The narrator counts him among the seven outstanding human teachers, with Sethard, Moses, Zoroaster, Lao-tse, Buddha, and Paul. Paul wisely removed many of Philo’s inconsistencies, but on the doctrine of the atonement he failed to keep pace with him: Philo taught deliverance from the doctrine of forgiveness only by the shedding of blood. The text adds that Paul’s theory of original sin and hereditary guilt was partly Mithraic in origin. (121:6.3, 121:6.4, 121:6.5)

VII. Jews and Gentiles — The Fetters of Tradition

The Jews had built a rigid wall of separation between themselves and the gentile world, and they indulged a self-righteousness founded on the letter of the law and the pride of descent. The Messiah they awaited was a deliverer who would arise out of their own national history. With such an outlook, it was very hard to receive Jesus’ teaching about the spiritual brotherhood of man. (121:7.1, 121:7.2)

The scribes, the Pharisees, and the priesthood held the Jews in a terrible bondage of ritualism and legalism, a bondage far more real than that of the Roman political rule. (121:7.3)

Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the Psalmist had spoken of a law written in the heart and a new spirit within, but a religion caught in traditionalistic inertia could not follow that path. So the motion of religious evolution passed westward to the European peoples, and Paul’s Christianity came to combine the philosophy of the Greeks, the law of the Romans, the morality of the Hebrews, and even the redemption doctrines of the mystery cults. (121:7.5, 121:7.6, 121:7.10)

The narrator explains that, while drawing on Andrew’s lost record and on the help of many celestial beings, he has also aimed to make use of the four Gospels. He then describes in turn how each Gospel came to be written. (121:8.1)

Each of these writers gave an honest picture of Jesus as he saw, remembered, or had learned of him, colored by Paul’s theology. The narrator does not hide their imperfection, yet he recognizes their power. (121:8.11)

And these records, imperfect as they are, have been sufficient to change the course of the history of Urantia for almost two thousand years. (121:8.11)

The acknowledgment at the end of the paper shows how to read all of Part IV. The narrator used human sources first wherever possible; only when they failed did he turn to the memories of the midwayers, and only when those failed did he resort to superplanetary sources. His memoranda gather the thought of more than two thousand human beings, because he believes that concepts born in human minds will prove more acceptable to other human minds. (121:8.12, 121:8.14)

In many ways I have served more as a collector and editor than as an original narrator. (121:8.13)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 121 does not set the life of Jesus in a vacuum. He came into a world prepared with roads, a common language, and synagogues—a world whose philosophy was lofty but could not embrace the weak, and whose religions were many but could not feed the hunger of the individual. To that generation he gave his gospel of personal religion: sonship with God. (121:5.18)
