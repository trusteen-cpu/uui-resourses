Introduction — The Night the Court Was Waiting

After his arrest, Jesus was taken first to the palace of Annas, the former high priest, on Mount Olivet. Annas wanted to keep his prestige as chief ecclesiastical authority and to gain time, since the Sanhedrin court could not lawfully be convened before the morning sacrifice. Meanwhile some thirty members of the Sanhedrin were waiting at the palace of Caiaphas, ready to sit in judgment. (184:0.1, 184:0.2)

The text states clearly how this court was made up. Since twenty-three members were enough to constitute a trial court, only those strongly and openly opposed to Jesus were summoned. The trial this paper describes should be read with that in mind: it was not the decision of a whole people but a proceeding hastily arranged by a particular few. (184:0.2)

Only those members were assembled who were strongly and openly opposed to Jesus and his teaching since it required only twenty-three to constitute a trial court. (184:0.2)

John Zebedee could move freely in the palace not only because of the Roman captain’s word, but also because Annas was a distant relative of his mother, Salome, and the older servants knew him well. (184:0.3)

I. Examination by Annas — To One Who Knowingly Rejects the Light

Enriched by the temple revenues, father-in-law of the acting high priest, and well connected with the Roman authorities, Annas was the most powerful single individual in all Jewry. He wanted the trial kept in the hands of the Sadducees. What had stirred his enmity most was not Jesus’ teaching but the threat to his revenues when Jesus drove the money-changers out of the temple. (184:1.1, 184:1.3)

At first Annas thought he might persuade Jesus to abandon his claims and leave Palestine, for he was reluctant to take part in the murder of a good man. But before the Master’s majesty and poise, he soon saw that such proposals were useless. Jesus made no reply when asked the names of his disciples, and when Annas pointed to his power over the coming trial, Jesus answered him this way. (184:1.2, 184:1.4, 184:1.5)

but you, friend, know what you are doing. How can you, therefore, reject the light of God? (184:1.5)

Asked about his teaching, Jesus said he had spoken openly to the world, so Annas should ask those who had heard him. At that the chief steward struck him in the face, and Annas spoke no word of rebuke. Jesus did not strike back; he answered with a fair question. (184:1.6)

My friend, if I have spoken evil, bear witness against the evil; but if I have spoken the truth, why, then, should you smite me? (184:1.6)

Asked whether he was the Messiah, Jesus said he claimed nothing except what his Father had appointed, and that he had been sent to all men, gentile as well as Jew. As daybreak neared, Annas sent him bound to Caiaphas. (184:1.8, 184:1.9)

When Annas asked once more whether he had claimed to be the Messiah, Jesus looked at him and replied only, “So you have said.” Even before a man who had known him since his youth, he neither made excuses nor pleaded. This bearing, neither lowering nor exalting himself before the powerful, shows the dignity an honest person can keep under threat. (184:1.8)

II. Peter in the Courtyard — The First Step of Compromise

At John’s request, the portress let Peter into the courtyard of Annas. Having thrown away his sword, Peter warmed himself by the charcoal fire. The text says he was out of place there. Jesus had told only John to stay near at hand and had specifically warned the other apostles not to endanger their lives. (184:2.1, 184:2.2, 184:2.3)

When the cock crowed, Peter remembered the Master’s warning. Just then Jesus, being led to Caiaphas, turned and looked at him. Peter never forgot that look as long as he lived. (184:2.8)

It was such a glance of commingled pity and love as mortal man had never beheld in the face of the Master. (184:2.8)

Peter sat down by the roadside and wept bitterly. The narrator adds that until the cock crowed, Peter had been congratulating himself on how cleverly he had eluded the servants’ accusations. Then the text explains why a wrong path, once entered, is so hard to leave. (184:2.9, 184:2.11)

It requires a great and noble character, having started out wrong, to turn about and go right. (184:2.12)

Peter never fully believed he could be forgiven until, after the resurrection, he saw that the Master received him just as before. The comfort this scene leaves the reader is that the look of love is not withdrawn even after failure. (184:2.13)

III. Before the Court of Sanhedrists — Silence as Refutation

At half past three on Friday morning, Caiaphas called the court of inquiry to order in the high priest’s palace. This was not a regular meeting of the Sanhedrin held in its usual place in the temple but a special court of some thirty members. The text notes that ordinary Jewish trials on capital charges proceeded with great caution and every safeguard of fairness, yet on this occasion Caiaphas was more a prosecutor than a judge. (184:3.1, 184:3.2, 184:3.4)

Ordinarily, the Jews, when trying a man on a capital charge, proceeded with great caution and provided every safeguard of fairness in the selection of witnesses and the entire conduct of the trial. (184:3.4)

Jewish law required at least two witnesses to agree, but the testimony of more than a score of false witnesses was so contradictory that the Sanhedrists themselves were ashamed. The nearest thing to agreement concerned a saying about destroying the temple and building another in three days, and even that was not exactly what Jesus had said. (184:3.6, 184:3.7)

Judas could not be a witness, because Jewish law forbade the testimony of a traitor. Jesus looked down benignly on the perjurers and answered not a word. Their testimony, full of hatred and exaggeration, fell in its own entanglements. (184:3.6, 184:3.8)

The very best refutation of their false accusations was the Master’s calm and majestic silence. (184:3.8)

The Sanhedrin had already agreed that Jesus deserved death for transgressing Jewish law. What concerned them now was framing charges that would justify Pilate in pronouncing a death sentence, since the governor’s consent was needed for a lawful execution. Annas set out to make Jesus appear a dangerous teacher to have abroad among the people. (184:3.13)

Unable to endure the Master’s silence, Caiaphas adjured him in the name of the living God to say whether he was the Son of God. To this question alone, the one concerning his bestowal mission, Jesus answered instantly and unequivocally in the affirmative. The high priest tore his garments and cried blasphemy, and the first session ended at half past four in disorder, with spitting and blows. (184:3.14, 184:3.15, 184:3.16, 184:3.18)

Man sits in judgment on God, but even then he loves them and would save them if he could. (184:3.19)

IV. The Hour of Humiliation — The Greatest Victory

Jewish law required two sessions of the court for a death sentence, with a day of fasting and mourning between them. This court waited only one hour. During that hour the guards and servants mocked Jesus, spat on him, and struck him. Fearing that John’s indignation would cost him his life, Jesus signaled him with a nod to withdraw. (184:4.1, 184:4.2)

The narrator asks here about the brutality that still lurks in man: the urge to insult and assault what one cannot spiritually attain. But Jesus, though undefending, was not defenseless, and he was not vanquished. (184:4.3, 184:4.5)

Jesus was not vanquished, merely uncontending in the material sense. (184:4.5)
Having lived to the full a life of revealing God to man, Jesus is now engaged in making a new and unprecedented revelation of man to God. (184:4.6)

The text sees this moment as the final triumph over all fears of creature personality isolation. Jesus asserts without hesitation that he and the Father are one, and he urges believers to become one with him in the same way. In this way the God-knowing believer is delivered from the isolation of the self and comes to experience the joy of spiritual socialization on a universe scale. (184:4.6)

In the middle of this record of suffering, the passage sets out a promise for the believer’s life. Loneliness and fear, with the helplessness that comes with them, form the deepest prison a person can know. In the loneliest place of all Jesus held fast to his oneness with the Father, and the text says that this experience becomes a sure way for believers to escape their isolation. (184:4.6)

V. The Second Meeting of the Court — Charges for Pilate

The court reassembled at half past five to draw up the charges to be presented to Pilate. Annas pointed out that a charge of blasphemy would carry no weight with Pilate. In half an hour a three-part indictment was complete. (184:5.1, 184:5.2)

The text judges the whole procedure irregular and wholly contrary to Jewish law. No two witnesses had agreed on any matter of substance, no witnesses spoke for the defense, and there was not even a formal ballot on blasphemy. Worth noting too is the record that three Pharisees took their leave rather than formulate charges without witnesses and in the prisoner’s absence. (184:5.6, 184:5.7, 184:5.8)

Those who had judged a sinless life did not want to look on his face again. At six o’clock in the morning Jesus was led out to appear before Pilate for confirmation of the death sentence. (184:5.9, 184:5.11)

Meanwhile some women of the palace came to look at the strange prisoner and asked whether he was the Messiah. Jesus answered that if he told them they would not believe, and if he asked them they would not answer. This short reply shows that a question seeking truth is different from mere curiosity. (184:5.10)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Moving between the midnight court and the courtyard fire, Paper 184 shows two kinds of failure: a few judges who tore down due process to protect their power and interests, and a beloved apostle who denied his Master out of fear. In the midst of them stood Jesus, enduring everything with silence and a few words of truth, and loving even them. (184:3.19, 184:2.8)
