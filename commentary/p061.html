Introduction — From Animal Paradise to the Cradle of Man

Paper 61 is the last of the papers that chronicle Urantia’s geologic history. The era of mammals runs from the origin of the placental mammals to the end of the ice age, a span of a little less than fifty million years. Its landscape was one of rolling hills, broad valleys, wide rivers, and great forests, and the trees were full of birds. (61:0.1, 61:0.2)

The trees swarmed with birds, and the whole world was an animal paradise, notwithstanding the incessant struggle of the evolving animal species for supremacy. (61:0.2)

The deposits of the five periods of these fifty million years hold the fossil records of one mammalian dynasty after another, leading right up to the actual appearance of man. So this paper is at once a history of animals and the story of how the stage was prepared for human beings. (61:0.3)

I. The Age of Early Mammals — The Placental Mammals Appear

Fifty million years ago the placental mammals suddenly appeared in North America. Nonplacental mammals had existed before, but this new type sprang directly from a reptilian ancestor whose descendants had lived on through the decline of the dinosaurs. The author calls this the most important evolutionary development up to that time. (61:1.1, 61:1.2)

The father of the placental mammals was a small, highly active, carnivorous, springing type of dinosaur. (61:1.2)

In these primitive mammals the basic mammalian instincts began to show themselves. The paper lists five reasons why mammals have an immense survival advantage over all other animals. The second of them—that they feed, train, and protect their young with affectionate regard—shows that love and care are themselves a power for survival. (61:1.3)

By 45 million years ago small horses, fleet-footed rhinoceroses, tapirs, primitive pigs, squirrels, lemurs, opossums, and monkeylike animals lived in the forests. There was also a large ostrichlike bird ten feet tall, an ancestor of the later highly intelligent passenger birds that once carried human beings through the air. The mammals of this time were all covered with hair and had large brains for their size, but no modern forms yet existed. (61:1.9, 61:1.10)

Later in this epoch most of Europe was submerged, with the highlands of the Alps and the Pyrenees standing up as islands. The foraminiferal limestone laid down in European waters at that time now stands 16,000 feet high in the Himalayas and 20,000 feet in Tibet. North America was joined by land to every continent except Australia, and the world was overrun by primitive mammals. (61:1.12, 61:1.13, 61:1.14)

II. The Age of Advanced Mammals — Brains Replace Armor

Thirty-five million years ago marks the beginning of placental-mammal dominion over the world. From carnivorous ancestors there soon branched herbivorous and then omnivorous families, and the angiosperms were their principal food. Before the period ended, over a hundred species of primitive mammals were extinct, and even the large mammals with small brains soon perished. (61:2.2, 61:2.3, 61:2.5)

Brains and agility had replaced armor and size in the progress of animal survival. (61:2.5)

In this period the ancestors of dogs, cats, coons, and weasels appeared, along with gnawing rodents such as beavers, squirrels, and rabbits. From 30 million years ago the grazing, hoofed animals of the plains evolved, in contrast to the earlier mammals of the hills. Camels and llamas originated in North America and later migrated, the camels to Europe and the llamas to South America. And in western North America the early ancestors of the ancient lemurs first appeared, establishing the line from which the true lemurs would later spring. (61:2.7, 61:2.8, 61:2.9, 61:2.10)

Meanwhile, just as the land serpents of an earlier age had gone to sea, a whole tribe of placental mammals forsook the land for the oceans, giving rise to whales, dolphins, seals, and sea lions. By the close of the Oligocene, which lasted ten million years, life on earth looked very much as it does today. (61:2.11, 61:2.13)

III. Age of the Elephant and the Horse — A Brain Equal to the Body

Twenty million years ago was truly the golden age of mammals. The Bering Strait land bridge rose, mastodons and many members of the cat family crossed from Asia to North America, and the first deer appeared. For once the world was dominated by a huge animal with a brain large enough for its body: the elephant. (61:3.4, 61:3.5, 61:3.6)

In intelligence and adaptation the elephant is approached only by the horse and is surpassed only by man himself. (61:3.6)

The author draws an interesting comparison between the elephant and the horse. It shows that intelligence is tied not only to the size of the brain but also to the ability to govern one’s emotions. (61:3.10)

Men used horses first for food, then for travel, and later in agriculture and war, and the horse played an important part in the growth of civilization. (61:3.11)

The horse has long served mankind and has played an important part in the development of human civilization. (61:3.11)

During this period the true types of the primitive monkey and the gorilla evolved in central Asia. But the author states clearly that neither of these species belongs to the line that would later become the ancestors of the human race. (61:3.12)

IV. The Last Great Mammalian Migration — The Hemispheres Divide

Preglacial land elevation raised mountain ranges and changed the courses of rivers, and Shasta, Hood, and Rainier began their mountain careers. For a short time all the land of the world except Australia was joined once more, and the last great world-wide animal migration took place. Sloths, armadillos, and bears entered North America, while North American camels went to China. (61:4.1, 61:4.2, 61:4.3)

By 5 million years ago the horse had evolved into its present form and spread from North America to all the world, yet it died out on its continent of origin long before the red man arrived. As the climate cooled, the northern land bridges sank and the link between Africa and South America was cut. From then on the Eastern and Western Hemispheres each developed life of their own. When this period closed, the ancestor of man had still not appeared. (61:4.5, 61:4.6, 61:4.7)

From this time forward distinct types of life began to develop in the Eastern and Western Hemispheres. (61:4.6)

V. The Early Ice Age — How Snow Became Ice

The ice age was not caused by cold alone. Northeastern North America and northern Europe rose high, ocean currents and seasonal winds shifted, and saturated air dropped almost constant snow over the northern highlands. The snow piled up to a depth of 20,000 feet and turned into solid but creeping ice. Half of the glacial ice lay in North America, a quarter in Eurasia, and the rest chiefly in Antarctica. (61:5.1, 61:5.2, 61:5.3)

The northern regions experienced six distinct ice invasions, each with scores of advances and retreats. Two million years ago the first glacier began moving south, taking nearly a million years to advance and withdraw. Yet away from the ice, life changed little, and between the invasions the climate was about as mild as today, perhaps a little warmer. (61:5.4, 61:5.5, 61:5.8)

VI. Primitive Man in the Ice Age — Born in a Hard Environment

The great event of the glacial period was the evolution of primitive man. On land just west of India, now under water, the dawn mammals suddenly appeared among the offspring of Asiatic migrants of the older North American lemur types. Three great mutations followed. (61:6.1)

The first human beings were born about the time the third glacier began to advance. The Eskimos, the sole survivors of these aborigines, still prefer frigid northern climes. Man did not reach the Western Hemisphere until near the end of the ice age, but during the interglacial epochs he passed west around the Mediterranean and soon spread over Europe. (61:6.3, 61:6.4)

VII. The Continuing Ice Age — Landscapes Carved by Ice and Milestones of Mankind

No other earthly activity leaves such characteristic marks as ice. Potholes, lakes, displaced stones, drumlins, and moraines are all the work of glaciers. As the sixth glacier retreated it produced North America’s Great Lakes, and it is thirty-seven thousand years since they began to empty out over the present Niagara route. (61:7.1, 61:7.10)

Woven through this chronicle of ice are the decisive events of human history. Geologic time and spiritual history are set side by side on a single timeline. (61:7.4, 61:7.8, 61:7.18)

The sixth glacier, beginning 250,000 years ago, was the largest invasion of all, with three ice sheets merging into one. The harsh glacial period wiped out many species and radically changed others. Arctic plants and animals that fled up the mountains to escape the ice were stranded there when it withdrew, and they are still found high in the Alps and the Appalachians. As long as the polar regions remain covered with ice, we are told, another glacial age is hardly possible. (61:7.7, 61:7.11, 61:7.13, 61:7.16)

Many were sorely sifted by the to-and-fro migration which was made necessary by the advancing and retreating ice. (61:7.13)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

The planetary chronicle that began in Paper 57 reaches the birth of man in this paper. Over fifty million years brains and agility replaced armor and size, and the animals that cared for their young with affection inherited the world. And the first human beings were born not in a comfortable paradise but in a hard and stimulating environment with glaciers on the advance. That difficulty can be a condition for growth is the long-standing comfort this paper offers us. (61:7.19)
