Introduction — A Victory Won by Compromise

Paper 195 surveys nearly two thousand years of history after Pentecost and then turns to the problems of the modern world. Peter was the real founder of the church, Paul carried the message to the gentiles, and Greek believers spread it through the empire. The Hebrews as a nation did not accept it, but the Western world — intellectual, weary of war, and skeptical of its existing religions — was hungry for a religion that would satisfy the soul. (195:0.1, 195:0.2)

The conflict between the new message and the old order ended in compromise. Christianity did not remain the simple spiritual appeal of Jesus; it emerged as a new social order embracing ritual, law, government, and morals. The text names five reasons why a message that had nearly failed in its birthplace captured the best minds of the empire. (195:0.3, 195:0.5)

The old order won many small victories in ritual. But Christianity gained the upper hand in four things: a higher moral standard, a greatly enlarged concept of God, the hope of immortality made a religious assurance, and Jesus of Nazareth offered to hungry souls. The text adds that had Athanasius not stood firm at Nicaea, the truth of Jesus’ bestowal might have been obscured. (195:0.13, 195:0.17, 195:0.18)

these compromised ideals of the Master are still latent in his gospel, and they will eventually assert their full power upon the world. (195:0.12)

I–II. Influence of the Greeks; The Roman Influence — Two Peoples Who Loved Truth

Hellenization began in earnest the day Paul spoke before the council of the Areopagus in Athens about “the Unknown God.” Greek philosophy and Jesus’ teaching shared a goal: the emergence of the individual. The Greeks taught intellectual freedom leading to political liberty; Jesus taught spiritual freedom leading to religious liberty. Together they became a new charter of human freedom. (195:1.1)

The Greek revered beauty, the Jew holiness, but both peoples loved truth. (195:1.6)

The Greeks earnestly debated every human problem except religion, while the Jews neglected every other field and took their religion very seriously, too seriously. Illuminated by Jesus’ message, the union of these two streams became the driving power of a new social order. By contrast, the Abnerian branch in the east, more faithful to Jesus’ teachings, refused to compromise, made little headway, and was finally lost in the Islamic movement. (195:1.6, 195:1.11)

Rome brought tolerance of foreign languages, peoples, and religions. The early persecutions arose largely because the Christians’ use of the word “kingdom” was taken as political rivalry. To the honest, devoted, self-controlled Romans, Christianity became a moral philosophy and a moral culture. Yet the text judges that it did not fully serve them as religion in the sense of an individual experience of spiritual growth. (195:2.1, 195:2.2, 195:2.4, 195:2.5)

III–IV. Under the Roman Empire; The European Dark Ages — A Spark That Did Not Go Out

The Christians had a God but no empire; the Greco-Romans had an empire but no God. So the Christians accepted the empire, and the empire adopted Christianity. To people whose language did not even have a word for unselfishness, this religion came as fresh comfort and liberation. The teaching that Christ loved little children brought an end to the practice of exposing unwanted infants, especially girls. (195:3.1, 195:3.3, 195:3.5)

That which gave greatest power to Christianity was the way its believers lived lives of service and even the way they died for their faith during the earlier times of drastic persecution. (195:3.4)

But the new religion came too late to halt the moral decline of the empire. The text counts it a misfortune that it became a cultural necessity rather than a means of spiritual salvation. Because the church had allied itself with politics, it shared the decline of the Dark Ages, and the individual almost disappeared before the authority and tradition of the church. Still, Christianity endured that long night and survived to the dawn of the Renaissance. (195:3.8, 195:4.1, 195:4.2, 195:4.3)

The text sums up this history as a single trajectory: beginning with the change from the religion of Jesus to a religion about Jesus, it passed through Hellenization and paganization, secularization and institutionalization, decline, stagnation, and hibernation, threatened extinction and rejuvenation, fragmentation, and finally a relative recovery. This tenacity shows its inherent vitality. And now Christianity faces an even more ominous struggle for survival in an age of scientific thinking and materialism. (195:4.4, 195:4.5)

V. The Modern Problem — What Religion Is and Is Not

The more civilization advances, the more necessary it becomes to seek first the realities of heaven. Truth that is fragmented and overanalyzed easily misleads; it leads aright only when grasped as a whole, as a living spiritual reality. The text clearly distinguishes religion from other high forms of thought. (195:5.1, 195:5.2, 195:5.3)

A lasting social system without a morality predicated on spiritual realities can no more be maintained than could the solar system without gravity. (195:5.9)

This section also holds gentle counsel for modern people. Do not try to satisfy every urge for adventure within one short lifetime; be patient, for a career of endless discovery lies ahead. And when you see the evil in the world, remember that it is a black spot on a white background of goodness, and do not talk too much about evil when there is so much good truth to tell. (195:5.10, 195:5.12, 195:5.13)

But of all these values, love is the true guide to real insight. (195:5.14)

VI–VII. Materialism; The Vulnerability of Materialism — A Machine Does Not Know It Is a Machine

Scientists have unintentionally thrown mankind into a materialistic panic. But the text says that the spiritual bank of humanity holds enormous reserves that can withstand the run, and that the religion of Jesus will be shown never to have been bankrupt. True religion cannot be at odds with science; what science has destroyed are only the childish illusions that misinterpreted life. (195:6.1, 195:6.2, 195:6.4)

The text’s argument against materialism is simple yet sharp. If the universe were purely a machine and man only a machine, man could not even know that he is a machine. The very concept of mechanism is a nonmaterial phenomenon of mind. A machine cannot know truth, hunger for righteousness, or cherish goodness. (195:6.11, 195:7.3, 195:7.6)

A self-conscious and avowed mechanist is the best possible answer to mechanism. (195:7.13)

The text says that materialism, mechanism, and determinism exist, but they are not the whole story. Freedom and initiative are proportional to the degree to which one does the Father’s will. The sentence that follows is a great comfort to every reader who seeks God. (195:6.14, 195:6.16)

And so, when you once start out to find God, that is the conclusive proof that God has already found you. (195:6.16)

One of the great problems of the modern age is the feeling of being too busy to find time for spiritual meditation and devotion. Another great error is that religious leaders try to call modern people with the trumpet blasts of the Middle Ages. Religion must equip itself with new slogans suited to its time. (195:6.7, 195:6.10)

VIII. Secular Totalitarianism — From the Church’s Fetters to the State’s

The text identifies the father of modern secularism as godless science and its mother as the totalitarian medieval church. Secularism began as a protest against the domination of the church and broke its fetters, but now it hands people over to the political and economic slavery of the totalitarian state. (195:8.2, 195:8.4)

Yet the text keeps its balance. It warns against hastily throwing away the blessings of the secular revolt — tolerance, social service, democratic government, and civil liberties. The great mistake of secularism was that, having rebelled against the church, it went on to rebel against God himself. There was no need to abandon faith in order to enjoy those blessings. (195:8.6, 195:8.8)

You simply cannot establish the brotherhood of men while ignoring or denying the fatherhood of God. (195:8.11)

IX–X. Christianity’s Problem; The Future — Back to Jesus

The text diagnoses Christianity soberly. Paganized and socialized Christianity needs new contact with the uncompromised teachings of Jesus. It is slowly dying of formalism, overorganization, intellectualism, and other nonspiritual trends. People take their religion secondhand, just as their teachers hand it down to them. (195:9.2, 195:9.8, 195:9.10)

The world needs more firsthand religion. (195:9.8)

Modern intellectuals shy away from the religion of Jesus because they fear it will dominate and transform them. The text says their fear is well founded, for the religion of Jesus really does ask people to devote their lives to knowing the Father’s will and to pour their energies into unselfish service of the brotherhood. (195:9.6)

Christianity has indeed done a great service for this world, but what is now most needed is Jesus. (195:10.1)

The text says it is futile to talk of reviving primitive Christianity. We must go forward from where we are and show the world Jesus living again in the experience of spirit-born mortals. Christianity willingly goes the first mile, but too few go the second mile of free service that holds a brother in love. The true church, the brotherhood of Jesus, is invisible and spiritual, characterized by unity, not uniformity. (195:10.1, 195:10.5, 195:10.11)

That is no reason to despise Christianity. It is the product of the combined moral genius of many ages and peoples, and it has been one of the greatest powers for good on earth. The teachings set in motion by commonplace disciples conquered the Roman world in three hundred years, and after a thousand-year coma it resurrected itself. (195:10.12, 195:10.18)

Christianity contains enough of Jesus’ teachings to immortalize it. (195:10.18)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 195 is a paper of criticism and of hope. Christianity won the world by compromise and dimmed Jesus’ ideals as the price. Yet those ideals still lie latent in the gospel, and an age of materialism and secularism is in fact a time ripe for great revelation. In the end, hope lies in learning anew the living gospel of the fatherhood of God and the brotherhood of man. (195:0.12, 195:9.3, 195:10.21)

Ever bear in mind—God and men need each other. (195:10.3)
