Introduction — Not the Touch, but Faith

After the news of Amos’s cure spread, a great crowd waited on the Capernaum shore, among them new observers sent by the Sanhedrin. Jairus, a ruler of the synagogue, fell at Jesus’ feet on behalf of his dying only daughter, and Jesus answered that he would go with him. On the way a woman secretly touched the hem of his garment and was healed of a long-standing hemorrhage. (152:0.1, 152:0.2)

It was her faith and not her touch that made her whole. (152:0.3)

Jesus had a reason for calling this woman, Veronica of Caesarea-Philippi, forward: to correct two errors that might linger in her mind or in the minds of the witnesses. Touching his garment was merely the superstitious part of her belief; with the faith she had, it was enough to approach the Master’s person. (152:0.3)

Source 152:0.3

This paper covers the peak and turning point of the Galilean ministry. Jesus’ fame as a healer reaches its height, and the feeding of the five thousand raises popular enthusiasm to its highest pitch, but when Jesus refuses a crown, his following shrinks rapidly. Following this course, readers can compare what Jesus wanted with what the people wanted. (152:1.3, 152:6.2)

I. At Jairus’s House — A Girl Awakened, and a Life That Cannot Be Repeated

Before they reached the house a servant came to say the girl was dead, but Jesus said, “Fear not; only believe.” He put the mourners out, went in with the parents and three apostles, took the girl by the hand, and told her to awake and arise. Then he directed that she be given something to eat, for she had long been without food. (152:1.1)

Jesus explained to the family and the apostles that the girl had been in a coma following a long fever and that he had merely aroused her. But being miracle-minded, they all believed he had raised her from the dead. Here the gap shows between Jesus, who honestly stated the facts, and followers eager to ascribe yet another wonder to him. (152:1.2)

As he left Jairus’s house, two blind men led by a dumb boy followed him, crying out for healing. Everywhere the sick were waiting for him, and the Master looked much worn; his friends feared he would teach and heal to the point of collapse. This weariness forms the background for his departure across the lake in the next section. (152:1.3, 152:2.1)

The narrator says that neither science nor religion can ever check up on these events, because such a situation can never occur again anywhere in Nebadon: a being in mortal flesh embodying creative energy together with spiritual endowments. Yet this does not close the door on believers today. (152:1.4, 152:1.5)

Though the Master is absent as a material being, he is present as a spiritual influence in the hearts of men. (152:1.5)

II. Feeding the Five Thousand — The First and Only Planned Nature Miracle

Jesus crossed to a park south of Bethsaida-Julias to rest, but the people hired boats and walked around the lake after him. From a thousand on Sunday the crowd grew to about five thousand by Wednesday noon, and the food they had brought ran out. Meanwhile it was quietly whispered that this secluded spot was the place to crown Jesus king, and a young evangelist named Joab led the plot. (152:2.1, 152:2.4, 152:2.5)

Philip had provided only a three days’ supply of food for Jesus and the twelve, kept by the Mark lad, their chore boy. His reckoning that two hundred denarii worth of bread would not be enough was realistic. But Jesus’ question was, “can you not feed them?” He wanted the apostles to take the crowd’s need as their own responsibility rather than send it away. (152:2.5, 152:2.6)

Philip and Andrew proposed dismissing the multitude; Andrew in particular feared the king plot might materialize. Jesus said the people were like sheep without a shepherd and that he wished to feed them, and the Mark lad brought the remaining five barley loaves and two dried fishes. The people sat down in companies of a hundred; Jesus gave thanks and the food was distributed; all ate and were filled, and the fragments filled twelve baskets. (152:2.6, 152:2.7, 152:2.8, 152:2.9)

And this is the first and only nature miracle which Jesus performed as a result of his conscious preplanning. (152:2.10)

The narrator briefly describes how it happened: Michael multiplied the food elements as he always does, except that the time factor and the visible life channel were eliminated. Later Jesus told the apostles that he yielded to his sympathy for the multitude only after he had ascertained that it was according to the Father’s will. (152:2.10, 152:6.6)

III. The King-Making Episode — A Crowd with Full Stomachs

To the well-fed multitude, this seemed the fulfillment of the long-held expectation that the Messiah would bestow the bread of life. The idea of making Jesus king spread through the crowd like a contagion that needed no further direction, and the people rose as one man, shouting to make him king. (152:3.1)

In the eyes of these simple-minded people the power to feed carried with it the right to rule. (152:3.1)

Jesus stepped onto a huge stone and told them they meant well but were shortsighted and material-minded. They wanted him as king not because their souls had been lighted with a great truth but because their stomachs had been filled with bread. The kingdom of heaven is a spiritual brotherhood with no material throne, and its Ruler is the Father. (152:3.2)

If you must have a king, let the Father of lights be enthroned in the heart of each of you as the spirit Ruler of all things. (152:3.2)

The multitude went away stunned and disheartened, and many followed him no more from that day. The apostles stood speechless by the baskets of fragments; only the Mark lad spoke, saying that he had refused to be their king. Before going alone into the hills, Jesus told Andrew to pray with his brethren, especially for Simon Peter. (152:3.3)

IV–V. Simon Peter’s Night Vision; Back in Bethsaida

Rowing without the Master against a contrary wind that night, Peter, the most downcast of all, fell into an exhausted sleep. He dreamed that he saw Jesus walking on the sea, that he himself walked on the water, began to sink, and cried out to be saved. Acting out the dream, he actually stepped overboard and awoke as his companions pulled him from the water. (152:4.1, 152:4.2, 152:4.3)

To Peter the experience was always real. He only partially convinced Mark, while Luke, who searched carefully into these matters, concluded it was a vision and left it out of his narrative. The text in effect invites readers to distinguish the sincerity of an experience from the factuality of an event. (152:4.4)

Jesus, meanwhile, had walked back to Bethsaida during the night with the Mark lad. Of the five thousand, only about five hundred kept following him. He gathered the apostles and their associates, reminded them of the psalmists’ words of trust, and rebuked their material motives. (152:5.1, 152:5.2, 152:5.3)

Of the five thousand who were miraculously fed, and who, when their stomachs were full and their hearts empty, would have made him king, only about five hundred persisted in following after him. (152:5.2)
And now do you all see that the working of miracles and the performance of material wonders will not win souls for the spiritual kingdom? (152:5.4)

The narrator judges that the miracle did nothing for halfhearted believers but brought to a head the miracle-seeking and king-craving tendencies of Jesus’ closest followers. It marked the turning point into the last year of his ministry, given to proclaiming divine sonship, spiritual liberty, and eternal salvation. (152:5.6)

VI. At Gennesaret — Appeal to the Spirit

While resting at Gennesaret, Jesus held conferences with the apostles every afternoon. Meanwhile his open followers, more than fifty thousand in Galilee alone, shrank in less than a month to fewer than five hundred. Jesus wanted the apostles to experience for themselves how fickle popular acclaim is, so that later they would not rely on transient religious hysteria. (152:6.1, 152:6.2)

It requires time for men and women to effect radical and extensive changes in their basic and fundamental concepts of social conduct, philosophic attitudes, and religious convictions. (152:6.1)

Retelling the parable of the sower, Jesus distinguished three ways of appealing to people. Emotion is only a technique for arresting attention; the quickened mind is the gateway to the soul; and true transformation of character comes when the spiritual nature within responds to truth. (152:6.3, 152:6.4)

Preparing them for the crisis only days away, Jesus explained that the Jerusalem leaders would conspire with Herod Antipas to destroy them. The apostles began to realize that he would not sit on David’s throne and that spiritual truth is not advanced by material wonders. Recognizing that the feeding of the five thousand was the height of popular acclaim readied them for the sifting and adversity to come. (152:6.5)

VII. At Jerusalem — The Approaching Crisis

Jesus went to Jerusalem quietly with only the twelve and forbade any teaching or preaching during this Passover. There the apostles learned how bitter feeling toward their Master had become, and they returned convinced that a crisis was impending. (152:7.1, 152:7.2)

Jesus sent Andrew to ask permission to speak in the Capernaum synagogue, knowing well that it would be the last time he would be allowed to speak there. So the paper ends with the Capernaum crisis of the next paper standing at the door. (152:7.3)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 152 shows both the height of Jesus’ popularity and its collapse. He refused the crown the crowd wanted and asked that the throne of each heart be given to the Father. The lesson that miracles can fill stomachs but cannot win souls is sharp for readers today who measure faith by results or by the intensity of experience. (152:3.2, 152:5.4)
