Introduction — The Two Most Crucial Years

The narrator calls the fourteenth and fifteenth years the most crucial and most trying of Jesus’ earth life. He had become self-conscious of divinity and destiny, but he had not yet achieved a large measure of communication with his indwelling Thought Adjuster. No youth passing through the confusions of adolescence, says the text, ever faced a more crucial testing. (126:0.1)

It is this period of two years which should be called the great test, the real temptation. (126:0.1)

When her son came back from Jerusalem, Mary hoped he would now be more responsive to her plans, but she was soon more completely disillusioned. The boy spent more and more time with his father, and his parents could not understand how he moved back and forth between the affairs of this world and his Father’s business. (126:0.2)

Frankly, they did not understand him, but they did truly love him. (126:0.2)

Jesus respected sincere Pharisees and honest scribes but held hypocritical religious leaders in contempt, and his righteous resentment of the politically appointed priests grew. At times he was tempted to look with favor on becoming the Messiah of Jewish expectation, but he never yielded. Meanwhile, the people of Nazareth, hearing of his exploits in the temple, predicted that he would become a great teacher. (126:0.3, 126:0.4)

I. His Fourteenth Year (A.D. 8) — A Future That Looked Bright

At fourteen Jesus was a good yoke maker, skilled with canvas and leather, and fast becoming an expert carpenter and cabinetmaker. He often climbed the hill of Simeon northwest of Nazareth to pray and meditate, gazing on Megiddo and Taanach, Dothan, Ebal and Gerizim, and recalling the history of his father’s people. There he was gradually becoming more self-conscious of the nature of his bestowal. (126:1.1, 126:1.2)

Joseph set aside property income for Jesus’ studies in Jerusalem the following year. Yet his parents frequently doubted their son’s destiny. The devout people of that day believed that men of promise proved their calling by miracles and wonders, but nothing extraordinary or miraculous ever happened to Jesus. Scores of times his mother waited breathlessly, only to have her hopes dashed. (126:1.4, 126:1.5)

Still, the family’s finances improved, and Jesus resumed his harp lessons. The narrator sums up the year this way. (126:1.6)

Throughout this year it can truly be said that Jesus “grew in favor with man and with God.” (126:1.7)

II. The Death of Joseph — Head of the Family at Fourteen

On September 25, A.D. 8, word came that Joseph had been severely injured by a falling derrick while working on the governor’s residence at Sepphoris. Mary hurried there with James, but Joseph died before she arrived. The family never heard his farewell or his last blessing. (126:2.1, 126:2.8)

Coming just when the future looked brightest, this blow demolished every plan for study in Jerusalem. A boy just past fourteen now had to carry, along with his heavenly Father’s commission, the care of his widowed mother, seven brothers and sisters, and another yet to be born. The narrator judges these responsibilities heavy but highly educational and disciplinary. (126:2.2)

This lad of Nazareth now became the sole support and comfort of this so suddenly bereaved family. (126:2.2)

Jesus cheerfully accepted these responsibilities and carried them faithfully to the end. Ironically, the problem of going to the rabbinical academy was now settled. He was willing to learn from anyone, but he never took his authority to teach from men. (126:2.3)

He was ever willing to learn from even the humblest of little children, but he never derived authority to teach truth from human sources. (126:2.3)

The narrator adds that Jesus still knew nothing of Gabriel’s visit to his mother before his birth; he learned of it only from John on the day of his baptism. He was finding his way through his own experience and reflection, not through heavenly announcements. That winter the neighbors would drop in during the evenings to hear him play the harp, tell stories, and read from the Greek scriptures. A grieving home became a small gathering place for the village. (126:2.4, 126:2.6)

As the years passed, he measured every institution of society and every religious usage by one unvarying test. He gave his time and energy more and more to two purposes: the care of his family and preparation to do his Father’s will. Joseph had left a sum of money, so at first the household ran smoothly, and Jesus proved a liberal but frugal administrator of his father’s estate. (126:2.5, 126:2.7)

III. The Fifteenth Year (A.D. 9) — The Lord’s Prayer and “the Son of Man”

In April of A.D. 9 Ruth, the baby of the family, was born, and for almost twenty years Jesus loved and nurtured her and the other children as faithfully as any father. Their savings were nearly gone, and they had to dispose of one of the houses that Joseph owned in partnership with his neighbor Jacob. (126:3.1, 126:3.2)

He tried to lead his brothers and sisters to pray spontaneously, each in his own words, but the children simply took the suggestive phrases he offered as their prayer. At last, one evening in October, by the little lamp on the low stone table, he wrote out a standard family prayer in charcoal on a cedar board. The prayer later known as “The Lord’s Prayer” was first formulated in this way, at the family altar. (126:3.3, 126:3.4)

His family responsibilities removed all thought of immediately beginning his Father’s business. He rightly reasoned that the care of his earthly father’s family must be his first obligation. Meanwhile he pondered deeply who he was and what he should be called. (126:3.5, 126:3.7)

He knew that the Book of Enoch was not written by Enoch of old and that it mixed in Eastern mysticism. Even so, he recognized and embraced the truth it contained. He looked to the truth of the content rather than to the source. (126:3.8)

Jesus had an unerring ability for the recognition of truth, and truth he never hesitated to embrace, no matter from what source it appeared to emanate. (126:3.8)

By now he had settled many things about his coming work for the world, but he said nothing of them to his mother, who still held that he was the Jewish Messiah. The essence of the mission he had settled on was “to be about his Father’s business”—to show forth his Father’s loving nature to all mankind. (126:3.9, 126:3.10)

Yet great confusion followed. To what did the Messianic prophecies refer? Could his mother be right after all? What should be his relation to the Jewish commonwealth, to Rome, and to the gentiles? He pondered each question while making a living at the carpenter’s bench for his mother and eight younger brothers and sisters. Mary was uneasy about his strange ideas, and he learned not to disclose all his thoughts. Outwardly he became ordinary, but he longed for a friend in whom he could confide. (126:3.10, 126:3.11, 126:3.14)

The uniqueness of the unusual situation compelled him to bear his burdens alone. (126:3.14)

IV. First Sermon in the Synagogue — The Chosen Words Were the Message

On the first Sabbath after his fifteenth birthday Jesus conducted the morning service of the Nazareth synagogue for the first time. Instead of commenting, he read a selection of scriptures, and the choice itself became his message: the words about being anointed to bring good news to the meek, the call to cease doing evil and learn to do good, and the charge to defend the fatherless and plead for the widow. (126:4.1, 126:4.2, 126:4.4)

And what does the Lord require of you but to deal justly, love mercy, and walk humbly with your God? (126:4.5)

This passage, which asks for justice, mercy, and humility in place of sacrifices, matches the conviction of the boy who had been sickened by the slaughter in the temple. Last he read of the Creator who gives power to the weak and of the promise “fear not.” The townspeople had never seen him so solemn and so authoritative. That afternoon he climbed the hill with James and, on returning, wrote out the Ten Commandments in Greek on two boards. (126:4.6, 126:4.8, 126:4.9)

V. The Financial Struggle — An Idealism That Did Not Break

Gradually the family returned to a simple life. A carpenter’s wages fell, so that working early and late Jesus could earn only about twenty-five cents a day, and the tax collector even threatened to take his harp. The great shock of his fifteenth year came when Herod ruled that nothing was due Joseph at his death. (126:5.1, 126:5.5, 126:5.7)

In these years he came to understand almost completely how people of his day lived. That intimate grasp of life in home, field, and workshop shows clearly in his later teaching. The Nazareth chazan still believed he would become a great teacher, perhaps the successor of Gamaliel. (126:5.2, 126:5.3)

To all appearances, every plan for his career had been thwarted. But he did not falter and was not discouraged; day by day he did the present duty well. (126:5.4)

Jesus’ life is the everlasting comfort of all disappointed idealists. (126:5.4)

The narrator sees the hard experience of supporting the family as a protection, leaving no room for idle meditation or mysticism. As a thoroughly practical idealist, Jesus met his situation as he found it and did all he could to satisfy his family’s longings as fully as possible. (126:5.9, 126:5.10)

the rigorous experience of supporting his family was a sure safeguard against his having overmuch time for idle meditation or the indulgence of mystic tendencies. (126:5.9)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 126 shows a real life beginning in the place where plans have collapsed. When the fifteenth year closed, the growth period of mind and body had ended, and the real career of this young man began. He passed through those years with faithfulness instead of miracles, responsibility instead of escape, and daily duty instead of discouragement. (126:5.12)
