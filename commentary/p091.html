Introduction — From Monologue to Communion

Paper 91 completes the series on primitive religion, which ran from the ghost cult to shamanism, with the subject of prayer. Its author, the Chief of the Urantia Midwayers, explains that prayer as an agency of religion evolved from earlier nonreligious monologue and dialogue. When primitive man became self-conscious, other-consciousness inevitably followed, carrying a twofold potential: social response and the recognition of God. (91:0.1)

The earliest prayers were not addressed to Deity at all; they were much like saying to a friend before some important undertaking, “Wish me luck.” This muttered wish for luck widened into ceremonies of friends, family, and clan; when ideas of ghosts and spirits arose, it was addressed to superhuman beings; and with the consciousness of gods it rose at last to genuine prayer. The author illustrates prereligious praying with the Toda tribe and the mana practices of the Melanesians. (91:0.2, 91:0.3, 91:0.5)

I–II. Primitive Prayer; Evolving Prayer — An Agency for Conserving Values

The function of early evolutionary religion is to conserve and augment social, moral, and spiritual values, and this is chiefly done through prayer. Because prayer was allied only with values that had group approval, a person seeking antisocial ends had to turn to magic and sorcerers rather than prayer. Prayer therefore became, very early, a mighty promoter of social evolution and moral progress. (91:1.1, 91:1.2)

But for the safeguarding of prayer, all holy days would speedily revert to the status of mere holidays. (91:1.1)

Primitive man prayed for food, rain, and game. The author calls this a perversion of prayer, yet credits it with raising the mores, since it encouraged people to pursue material goals through social and ethical action. Among the dangers of prayer he lists a morbid sense of sin, along with ignorance, superstition, crystallization, devitalization, materialism, and fanaticism. (91:1.3, 91:1.6)

Prayer moved from verbalized wishes to a technique of gaining spirit co-operation, and then to the higher function of conserving all worth-while values. When man learned that prayer could not coerce the gods, it became petition and favor seeking. The idea of sacrifice weakens true prayer, because people offer material possessions in place of their own consecrated wills; and when religion loses a personal God, its prayers turn into theology and philosophy. (91:2.1, 91:2.3, 91:2.4, 91:2.5)

But the truest prayer is in reality a communion between man and his Maker. (91:2.3)

For the average person prayer is largely an exchange with his own subconscious, but the spiritually progressing individual attains some contact with the superconscious levels of the mind, the domain of the indwelling Thought Adjuster. Beyond that there is also a definite spiritual phase in which prayer is received and recognized by the spiritual forces of the universe. (91:2.6)

It is a mighty influence working to prevent isolation of personality. (91:2.7)

III. Prayer and the Alter Ego — From Fiction to Reality

Children learning to speak think out loud, and when imagination dawns they converse with imaginary companions. This ability to turn monologue into a pseudo dialogue is the psychological root of prayer. Much of an adult’s thinking, too, is carried on in conversational form. (91:3.1)

The author says that from the first conceiving of an alter ego to the concept of a heavenly Father, prayer has always been a socializing, moralizing, and spiritualizing practice. Even apart from its spiritual side, ethical prayer is a splendid way to elevate and reinforce the self; it sends the ego looking both ways for help—downward to the subconscious reservoir of experience, upward to the superconscious borders where the Mystery Monitor makes contact. (91:3.3, 91:3.5)

Prayer ever has been and ever will be a twofold human experience (91:3.6)

The most practical counsel of this section is that enlightened prayer must recognize not only an external, personal God but also the internal Divinity, the indwelling Adjuster. As primitive man spoke to a near-by alter ego, so we may now speak directly with that alter ego, knowing that it is no fiction but the presence of God dwelling within us. (91:3.7)

IV–V. Ethical Praying; Social Repercussions of Prayer

No prayer can be ethical when it seeks selfish advantage over others; such praying slips back into primitive pseudo magic. The author advises us not to expect God to love us more than our friends, neighbors, or even enemies. When a prayer seeks nothing for the one who prays or for anyone else, the soul tends toward true worship. (91:4.1, 91:4.3)

Prayer must never be so prostituted as to become a substitute for action. (91:4.2)

Materialistic praying breeds disillusionment as science reveals a universe of law, yet even such petitions are somewhat effective because they lead to efforts that help bring about their answers. The spiritually advanced should therefore be very cautious about discouraging the prayers of the immature. (91:4.4)

Remember, even if prayer does not change God, it very often effects great and lasting changes in the one who prays in faith and confident expectation. (91:4.5)

Prayer echoes beyond the individual into society. A group praying together for moral uplift makes every member better, and confession, repentance, and prayer have led whole cities and nations to reform. Prayer also holds the remedy for a critical habit. (91:5.2, 91:5.3)

If you truly desire to overcome the habit of criticizing some friend, the quickest and surest way of achieving such a change of attitude is to establish the habit of praying for that person every day of your life. (91:5.3)

The social effect grows when the person prayed for knows of it and the one praying comes into close contact with him. Prayer also gives rise in time to secondary agencies such as priests, holy books, and rituals; those who are God-conscious without symbols must be patient and tolerant toward those who need them. The strong must not look with disdain upon the weak. (91:5.4, 91:5.5, 91:5.6, 91:5.7)

VI. The Province of Prayer — What It Can and Cannot Do

Prayer has no direct effect on the physical environment unless it is linked with the will of the spiritual forces and material supervisors of a realm. Nor is it a technique for curing organic disease. Yet it has contributed enormously to abundant health and to the cure of many mental, emotional, and nervous ailments, has added to the efficacy of other remedies, and has turned many a complaining invalid into a paragon of patience. (91:6.1, 91:6.2)

Apart from its religious meaning, prayer is a sound psychological practice for realizing the reserve powers stored in the unconscious mind. The author urges us, however hard it may be to reconcile scientific doubts with the urge to seek divine help, never to forget that the sincere prayer of faith is a mighty force for happiness, self-control, social harmony, and spiritual attainment. (91:6.3, 91:6.4)

Do not be so slothful as to ask God to solve your difficulties (91:6.5)

The rest of the sentence restores the balance: never hesitate to ask God for wisdom and spiritual strength while you yourself courageously attack the problems at hand. With the counsel to pray as Jesus taught—honestly, unselfishly, with fairness, and without doubting—the author stresses that the efficacy of prayer does not depend on knowledge, social level, or culture; every person can reach through prayer the threshold of contact with the Adjuster. (91:6.5, 91:6.6, 91:6.7)

VII. Mysticism, Ecstasy, and Inspiration — Tested by the Fruits

Mysticism, as a technique of cultivating the consciousness of God’s presence, is praiseworthy, but when it leads to social isolation and fanaticism it is all but reprehensible. What the overwrought mystic takes for divine inspiration is too often the uprising of his own deep mind. Contact with the Adjuster is favored by meditation but more frequently facilitated by loving, unselfish service to others. (91:7.1)

The soul of man requires spiritual exercise as well as spiritual nourishment. (91:7.2)

VIII–IX. Praying as a Personal Experience; Conditions of Effective Prayer

Prayer is spontaneous. Long before he had a clear concept of God, primitive man reached out for help in dire need and burst into expressions of joy when jubilant. The author makes plain that prayer did not evolve from magic; each arose independently. (91:8.1, 91:8.2)

Magic was an attempt to adjust Deity to conditions; prayer is the effort to adjust the personality to the will of Deity. (91:8.2)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 91 shows that prayer is not a descendant of magic but, from its own separate root, the effort to adjust personality to the will of God. Prayer that began as a muttered wish for luck can grow into communion with the indwelling Adjuster. Prayer does not change God but changes the one who prays; it does not replace action but inspires it. So the paper asks less about the theory of prayer than about the attitude of a praying life.
