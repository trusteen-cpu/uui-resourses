Introduction — A Child of Promise Who Grew Up Ordinary

John the Baptist was born on March 25, 7 B.C., in fulfillment of the promise Gabriel had made to Elizabeth. The narrator makes clear that apart from Gabriel’s visit and Zacharias’s dream, nothing unusual or supernatural attended his birth. He grew up as an ordinary child in a small village west of Jerusalem. (135:0.1, 135:0.2)

Excepting the visit of Gabriel to Elizabeth and the dream of Zacharias, there was nothing unusual or supernatural connected with the birth of John the Baptist. (135:0.1)

The most eventful occurrence of his early childhood was a visit to Jesus and the Nazareth family when he was a little over six. There was no synagogue school in the village, but Zacharias, a priest, and Elizabeth, herself of priestly descent, gave much time to the mental and spiritual training of their only son. (135:0.3, 135:0.4)

I–II. John Becomes a Nazarite; The Death of Zacharias

At fourteen John was inducted for life into the Nazarite brotherhood at Engedi, by the Dead Sea, vowing to abstain from intoxicating drink, to let his hair grow, and to refrain from touching the dead. A life Nazarite was held in great respect, since such Nazarites were the only persons besides high priests ever permitted to enter the holy of holies. At sixteen, impressed by reading about Elijah, he decided to adopt the prophet’s style of dress. (135:1.1, 135:1.2, 135:1.4)

When his father died, eighteen-year-old John was greatly embarrassed by his vow against contact with the dead, and after the burial he offered the sacrifices required for his cleansing. Visiting Nazareth that year, he had all but resolved to launch out on his lifework, but Jesus’ words and example counseled him to go home and care for his mother. (135:2.1, 135:2.2)

return home, take care of his mother, and await the “coming of the Father’s hour.” (135:2.2)

Because John refused the priest’s allowance, the family’s circumstances grew hard, and he and his mother moved to Hebron, where he kept sheep in the wilderness of Judea. He was so different from most Nazarites that he could not fully fraternize with the brotherhood, though he was very fond of Abner, the leader at Engedi. His waiting—sensing a calling while still caring first for the family before him—shows how to govern an impatient zeal. (135:2.3, 135:2.4)

III. The Life of a Shepherd — A Man of the Wilderness Reading Daniel

Along a brook John built a dozen stone shelters and night corrals, and with Ezda, an orphan lad he had in a way adopted, he lived simply on mutton, goat’s milk, wild honey, and locusts. A shepherd’s life gave him a great deal of time for thought. (135:3.1)

He read Daniel’s description of the great image a thousand times and believed that after the kingdoms from Babylon to Rome, the God of heaven would set up a kingdom never to be destroyed. Yet Daniel’s “one like the Son of Man” did not harmonize with what his parents had told him about Jesus, and he never completely rose above this confusion. Reports of the wickedness of Rome and Herod convinced him that the end of the age was impending. (135:3.2, 135:3.3, 135:3.4)

The feeling grew in John’s heart that he was to be the last of the old prophets and the first of the new. (135:3.4)

IV. The Death of Elizabeth — Waiting for Elijah

When his mother died in A.D. 22, John gave his flocks to the brotherhood and fasted and prayed. He knew only the old methods of approach to divinity, and Elijah was his ideal of a prophet. During two and a half years at Engedi he persuaded most of the brotherhood that the end of the age was at hand, though his early teaching rested on the current Jewish idea of the Messiah as a deliverer from gentile rule. (135:4.2, 135:4.3)

What held him back was Malachi’s promise that Elijah would come first. He knew he was not Elijah, and his doubts kept him from ever calling himself by that name. But Elijah’s influence led him to adopt a direct and blunt assault on the sins and vices of his day. (135:4.4, 135:4.5)

He was hardly an example to his age, but he was an eloquent rebuke. (135:4.5)

V. The Kingdom of God — The Expectations John Met

To understand John’s message we must know the situation of the Jews. For almost a hundred years Israel had been unable to explain why God’s chosen people remained subject to gentile overlords. The apocalyptists who arose then taught that this suffering was punishment for the nation’s sins and was nearly over, and they equated the end of Roman rule with the end of the age. (135:5.1, 135:5.2)

In that day “Messiah” meant not one who taught God’s will but one who would actually establish the kingdom of God; a holy man who taught righteous living was called a prophet. So when John cried that the kingdom of heaven was at hand, his hearers took his words in no fewer than half a dozen different senses. That the same words can sound wholly different according to the listener’s expectations is true of the language of faith today as well. (135:5.6, 135:5.8)

VI–VII. John Begins to Preach; John Journeys North — The Cry at the Jordan

In March of A.D. 25 John took his stand by the ancient ford where Joshua had crossed and began to preach to those passing by. A herdsman like Amos and dressed like Elijah, he was taken for a prophet. What was new was that he baptized Jews themselves “for the remission of sins.” Baptism had been used for gentile proselytes, but never had Jews been asked to submit to the baptism of repentance. (135:6.1, 135:6.3, 135:6.4)

He was a heroic but tactless preacher, warning the Pharisees and Sadducees who came for baptism to bring forth fruit worthy of repentance. Yet to his disciples he taught the concrete details of a new life. (135:6.7, 135:6.8)

Traveling north along the Jordan, he spoke for the first time of “another one who is to come after me.” The longer he preached, the more confused he grew about the nature of the kingdom, but his conviction that it was coming at once never wavered. In fifteen months he baptized considerably over one hundred thousand penitents. (135:6.4, 135:7.1, 135:7.2)

In mind John might be confused, but in spirit never. (135:7.1)

As his disciples asked questions, John kept adding more helpful and comforting teaching to his early message, “Repent and be baptized.” Throngs came from Galilee and the Decapolis, and scores of earnest believers stayed with him day after day. By the time he reached Pella, Jesus had spoken favorably of his message, and James and John, the sons of Zebedee, went down to be baptized and brought Jesus firsthand reports. (135:7.3, 135:8.1)

VIII–IX. Meeting of Jesus and John; Forty Days of Preaching

On January 13, A.D. 26, Jesus laid down his tools in the shop, said, “My hour has come,” and set out for Pella with his brothers James and Jude. He took his place in the line of penitents until he stood before John. When John protested that he needed to be baptized by Jesus, Jesus whispered that it was fitting to set this example for his brothers and to let the people know his hour had come. (135:8.3, 135:8.4, 135:8.5)

This is my beloved Son in whom I am well pleased. (135:8.6)

After the baptism the four men still in the water heard this voice, and Jesus left in silence for the hills to the east, unseen for forty days. Meanwhile John’s camp argued over whether the Messiah would be a military leader or the founder of a spiritual kingdom, and selfish ambitions began to appear. John forbade the disciples who wanted to go in search of Jesus. (135:8.6, 135:9.5, 135:9.6)

Our times are in the hands of the God of heaven (135:9.6)

When Jesus returned on February 23, John stood on a rock and proclaimed him the Son of God, the deliverer of the world. The next day Jesus left for Galilee, saying only that the Father would guide John in the future as in the past, and the two never again met in the flesh. (135:9.7, 135:9.9)

X–XII. John Journeys South; John in Prison; Death of John the Baptist

After Jesus’ baptism John felt that the responsibility for the coming kingdom was no longer on his shoulders, and he was disconsolate and lonely. Still he kept preaching, increasingly proclaiming mercy to the common people while denouncing corrupt rulers with renewed vehemence. His attack on Herod Antipas’s unlawful marriage, and Herod’s fear of rebellion, led to his arrest on June 12, A.D. 26. (135:10.1, 135:10.2, 135:10.3)

In the prison at Machaerus he was often tempted to doubt why the Messiah did nothing to deliver him. Yet to disciples who resented Jesus’ prosperity he gave a remarkable answer. (135:11.1, 135:11.2)

He must increase but I must decrease. (135:11.2)
This man can do nothing unless it has been given him by his Father in heaven. (135:11.2)

Jesus loved John greatly, but knowing that his work on earth was finished and what great things awaited him, he did not interfere in the natural outworking of his career. A few days before John’s death, Jesus answered his message by saying that he would be abundantly blessed in the age to come if he found no occasion to doubt and stumble over him; this word steadied John’s faith. (135:11.3, 135:11.4, 135:12.6, 135:12.7)

Herod feared that releasing John would bring rebellion and that killing him would set off a riot among the Pereans who held him a prophet, so he simply kept him in prison. John would never agree to give up public activity as a condition of release. Herod talked with him several times about the kingdom of heaven and was at times impressed, but fear kept him from acting, and at last, at his birthday feast, drawn by Herodias’s scheme and his own drunken oath, he had John beheaded. John’s disciples laid the body in a tomb and went and told Jesus. (135:12.2, 135:12.3, 135:12.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

The John of Paper 135 was faithful without complete understanding. He never clearly grasped the nature of the kingdom or Jesus’ part in it, and in prison he went through bitter doubt. Yet he finished the work of preparing the way that had been given him, and he knew how to rejoice as one greater than himself increased. (135:7.1, 135:11.2)
