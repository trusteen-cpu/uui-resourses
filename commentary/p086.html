Introduction — Religion That Grows Without Revelation

Following the nature worship of the previous paper, Paper 86 looks at the experiences through which primitive religion took shape. Its author, an Evening Star of Nebadon, explains that man’s prereligious fear of natural forces became religious as nature was personalized, spiritized, and finally deified in human consciousness. (86:0.2, 86:7.7)

THE evolution of religion from the preceding and primitive worship urge is not dependent on revelation. (86:0.1)

The normal functioning of the human mind under the guidance of the sixth and seventh mind-adjutants is quite enough to ensure this development. The paper follows four experiences that drove the process—chance and bad luck, the mystery of death, dreams and breath, and an imagined ghost environment—and shows what all of them finally prepared the way for. (86:0.1)

I–II. Chance and Its Personification — The Religion of Good and Bad Luck

The results of the hunt always varied, and for primitive people living on the edge of survival, bad luck was a great factor. They struggled not for a standard of living but for existence itself, and dread of unseen calamity overshadowed every joy. Even a run of good luck was feared as a sign of coming disaster. Caught between the desire to get something for nothing and the fear of getting nothing for something, existence itself was a gamble. (86:1.1, 86:1.2, 86:1.4)

Unthinking men forget good luck—take it for granted—but they painfully remember bad luck. (86:1.3)

Herders inherited the same outlook, and farmers were even more keenly aware that their crops depended on things beyond their control, such as drought and flood, hail and insects. These ideas permeated the philosophy of all ancient peoples and survived in the confession that the race is not always to the swift, and that time and chance happen to all. The gambling tendency that remains among partially civilized peoples today is a trace of this ancient experience. (86:1.4, 86:1.5, 86:1.6)

Unable to find a material cause, primitive man asked, “Who is doing this to me?” and found his answer in spirits. So religion was born of fear of the mysterious and awe of the unseen. His mind was logical but short of ideas; when one event followed another, he took it as cause and effect. What civilized man calls superstition was for the savage simply the result of ignorance. (86:2.2, 86:2.3)

The difference between the minds of savage and civilized men is more one of content than of nature, of degree rather than of quality. (86:2.4)

The author points out that to keep ascribing hard-to-understand things to supernatural causes is a lazy way of avoiding intellectual hard work. Words like luck and chance describe a state of being too ignorant or too indolent to determine causes. But as exploration continues, the belief in chance breaks down, and in its place comes a universe of law and order in which every effect is preceded by a definite cause. (86:2.5)

Thus is the fear of existence replaced by the joy of living. (86:2.5)

The author also says that when anxiety becomes pain, activity is inhibited and evolutionary change follows; pain and suffering are essential to progressive evolution. He adds that not all religion grew out of animism. Other concepts of the supernatural existed at the same time, and they too led to worship. (86:2.1, 86:2.7)

III–IV. Death, and Survival Beyond Death

Not the sanctity of life but the shock of death inspired fear and thus effectively fostered religion. (86:3.1)

Among savages, death usually came by violence, so nonviolent death was all the more mysterious. It took ages for the idea of death as the natural end of life to take hold. At first all sickness and natural death were believed to be caused by spirit influence, and even later, complex theologies attributed death to the action of the spirit world, giving rise to doctrines such as original sin and the fall. (86:3.1, 86:3.3)

The idea of survival after death was born of the chance association of everyday events with ghost dreams. When several tribesmen dreamed of a dead chief on the same night, it seemed proof that he had returned. Breath, visible as a cloud on a cold day, was the sign that divided the living from the dead, and the breath without a body became the spirit, the ghost. This dream-ghost-future-life idea began to serve as an antidote to the fear of death. (86:4.1, 86:4.2, 86:4.3, 86:4.4)

The primitive doctrine of survival did not mean immortality. People who could not count beyond twenty could not grasp eternity, so they imagined repeated reincarnations. At first there was no idea of hell or future punishment; the next life was thought of as something like this one without bad luck. The paper notes that the belief that a person dies three to seven times preserves a remnant of Adam’s teaching about the mansion worlds, showing that traces of revelation are mixed even into evolutionary religion. (86:4.5, 86:4.6, 86:4.7)

V. The Ghost-Soul Concept — The Other Self of Dreams

The soul was first thought of as the dream self, exactly like oneself except that it could not be touched. This belief spread to the idea that all things, living and nonliving, had souls, and it long sustained belief in nature spirits. The soul was thought to be related to the body as perfume to a flower, and it was believed able to leave the body in various ways. (86:5.1, 86:5.4)

There were many beliefs about where the soul resided: in the breath, the shadow, the head, the hair, the heart, the liver, the blood, or the fat. From these came customs such as turning mirrors to the wall, the taboo against eating fat, and the expression calling the eyes the windows of the soul. The descendants of Badonan believed in two souls, the breath and the shadow, and the Greeks in three: vegetative, animal, and intellectual. (86:5.14, 86:5.15, 86:5.17)

Because the soul was thought to pass with the breath, a brave chief would breathe into a newborn child to impart courage, and an eldest son would try to catch his dying father’s last breath. The idea that a soul could enter an animal spread into the belief in werewolves. Those who believed in several souls held that losing one meant discomfort, two meant illness, and three meant death, and the shaman was thought to perform a “new birth” by exchanging a sick soul for a new one. (86:5.12, 86:5.13, 86:5.16)

The Hebrews believed that God spoke in dreams, and Moses forbade the belief; the author says Moses was right, because ordinary dreams are not the method by which personalities of the spiritual world communicate with material beings. As civilization advances, man comes to depend on revelation and personal religious experience for a new concept of the soul: the soul as the joint creation of the God-knowing mortal mind and the indwelling Thought Adjuster. (86:5.2, 86:5.11)

VI. The Ghost-Spirit Environment — The Birth of Right and Wrong

Man inherited a natural environment, acquired a social environment, and imagined a ghost environment. When this imagined spirit world was accepted as real, the mind and moral life of mankind were changed forever. Until the age of revelation this was the only religion man had, and many races today still have only this crude evolutionary religion. (86:6.1, 86:6.2, 86:6.3)

Each passing generation smiles at the foolish superstitions of its ancestors while it goes on entertaining those fallacies of thought and worship which will give cause for further smiling on the part of enlightened posterity. (86:6.5)

This sentence teaches humility. Among the ideas we are sure of today, some will be errors at which later generations smile. Yet this age of superstition bore an unexpected fruit. Once people believed that angry spirits brought bad luck and pleased spirits good luck, they had to adjust their conduct accordingly, and with that the idea of right and wrong was born. The long and wasteful effort spent on tombs, temples, sacrifices, and priesthoods was a terrible price, but it was worth what it cost. (86:6.6, 86:6.7)

human ethics was born! (86:6.7)

VII. The Function of Primitive Religion — From Insurance to Philosophy

The savage insured himself against bad luck by paying costly premiums of fear, superstition, and gifts to priests. Primitive religion was the payment of premiums against the perils of the forest. Modern society has moved this function from religion to economics, and actuaries have replaced the capricious gods. As a result, religion is moving away from being a system of insurance against bad luck and rising to higher philosophic levels. (86:6.4, 86:7.1, 86:7.2)

Even so, ancient religion kept people from falling into fatalism or hopeless pessimism, because they believed they could do something to change their fate. The author also leaves a warning for modern people: while giving up the error of blaming bad luck on spirits, they are accepting an almost equally false idea that attributes all inequality to political, social, and industrial causes alone. Legislation and philanthropy are good, but they will not remedy the facts of birth and the accidents of living. (86:7.3, 86:7.4)

Scientific knowledge, leading to scientific action, is the only antidote for so-called accidental ills. (86:7.4)

The last paragraph of the paper gives meaning to the whole long story. By the force of false fear, primitive religion plowed the soil of the human mind and prepared it for the bestowal of a true spiritual force of supernatural origin, the Thought Adjuster. Ever since, the Adjusters have labored to transmute God-fear into God-love. (86:7.6)

Evolution may be slow, but it is unerringly effective. (86:7.6)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 86 does not hide that religion’s beginnings were not beautiful. Religion grew out of fear of bad luck, the shock of death, and the illusions of dreams. Yet from that rough soil a sense of right and wrong sprouted, and a place was prepared for the Adjuster to dwell. The task left to us is to complete a religion that began in fear as a religion of love, and that is the very work the Adjuster is already doing within us. (86:6.7, 86:7.6)
