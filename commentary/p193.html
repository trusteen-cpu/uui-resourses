Introduction — The Last Great Teaching in Nicodemus’s Courtyard

On Friday night, May 5, the believers in Jerusalem gathered together for the first time since the resurrection. The eleven apostles, the women’s corps, about fifty leading disciples, and a few Greeks were in the courtyard of Nicodemus. Jesus appeared there in his sixteenth appearance, called it the most representative group yet, gathering apostles and disciples, men and women, and began at once to teach. (193:0.1, 193:0.2)

He asked first why they were so surprised. He had spoken plainly of his death and resurrection, but they had heard his words without grasping their meaning. So now he urged them not to repeat the mistake of hearing with the mind and failing to understand with the heart. And he declared again that his one purpose had been to reveal the Father to his children. (193:0.2, 193:0.3)

Here Jesus drew a clear line against the change the apostles had just begun — the move to put the proclamation of the resurrection at the center of the message. Their mission was still to proclaim the fatherhood of God and the sonship of man, and to preach the whole truth of the gospel, not a part of it. (193:0.4)

Your message is not changed by my resurrection experience. (193:0.4)

He said there was nothing strange in his having risen, for the Father gives his Paradise Sons the power to lay down life and take it up again. Something else, rather, should stir their hearts. (193:0.5)

You should the rather be stirred in your hearts by the knowledge that the dead of an age entered upon the eternal ascent soon after I left Joseph’s new tomb. (193:0.5)

Jesus told them that as he had revealed God by loving and serving them, so they could reveal God to their fellows through loving service. And he told them to remain in Jerusalem until the Spirit of Truth came. The believers stayed until dawn discussing his counsel. (193:0.5, 193:0.6)

I–II. The Appearance at Sychar; The Phoenician Appearance — Faith and the Fruits of the Spirit

On the Sabbath afternoon of May 13, Jesus appeared near Jacob’s well at Sychar to Nalda and about seventy-five Samaritan believers. It was his seventeenth appearance. He said that rejoicing to know him as the resurrection and the life would avail nothing unless they were first born of the spirit and possessed eternal life by faith. And he said the time had come to worship God neither on Gerizim nor at Jerusalem, but where they were, as they were, in spirit and in truth. (193:1.1, 193:1.2)

It is your faith that saves your souls. (193:1.2)

He added that salvation is a free gift, yet it is followed by the experience of bearing the fruits of the spirit life lived in the flesh. To accept the fatherhood of God is also to accept the brotherhood of man. A brother is even more than a neighbor: one to be loved with family affection and served as one would serve oneself. (193:1.2)

On Tuesday evening, May 16, came the eighteenth appearance, at Tyre. Here Jesus spoke words that are the key to this paper. Resurrection and survival are great facts related to the gospel, but they are only part of the result of believing the good news. (193:2.1, 193:2.2)

The fact that the dead rise is not the gospel of the kingdom. (193:2.2)

He then named one by one the fruits borne in the lives of spirit-born, God-knowing mortals. The list is a mirror in which believers can examine their own lives. Professed believers who do not bear these fruits, he said sternly, are useless branches on the living vine. (193:2.2)

The believers at Tyre had known Jesus in the flesh, and they recognized him as soon as he began to speak. The text says that though his friends did not easily recognize the morontia form, they were never slow to recognize his personality. The next day the news spread to Sidon, Antioch, and Damascus. (193:2.3)

III. Last Appearance in Jerusalem — Do Not Be Alone

On Thursday morning, May 18, Jesus made his last morontia appearance on earth to the eleven apostles as they were about to breakfast in the upper room. Simon Zelotes asked whether he would now restore the kingdom, and Jesus replied that he still clung to his old ideas of a material kingdom. (193:3.1, 193:3.2)

Just before leaving them, Jesus urged them again to love and trust one another. Judas was no longer with them, he said, because his love had grown cold and he had refused to trust his brethren. He explained that he had sent them out two and two, and had not allowed himself to be alone for long periods while in the flesh, precisely to guard against the mischief of isolation. (193:3.2)

Trust, therefore, and confide in one another. (193:3.2)

Jesus pointed to his own example. From the beginning of their association he had always kept two or three of them by his side, even when he communed with the Father. That the one most deeply united with God did not make light of human companionship tells us that the spiritual life is not a path of solitary isolation. Because he was leaving them alone in the world that very day, this charge was all the more urgent. (193:3.2)

When he had finished, he led the apostles out to Olivet. From the time they left the upper room until they stopped on the mountain, no one spoke a word. (193:3.3)

IV. Causes of Judas’s Downfall — How Isolation Destroys

At the beginning of his farewell message Jesus held up Judas as a solemn warning against the dangers of social and fraternal isolation. In the light of those words and of the centuries of enlightenment since, the narrator reviews the causes of Judas’s downfall. The chief reason was that he was a markedly isolated personality. Yet isolation alone would not have done such harm; he also failed to grow in love and spiritual grace, and he nursed grudges and a craving for revenge. (193:4.1, 193:4.2)

The text states plainly that Judas need not have gone wrong. Thomas and Nathaniel had the same kind of suspicion and overdeveloped individualism, and Andrew and Matthew had similar leanings. The difference was that over time they grew in love and trust. Judas, by contrast, whenever his conflicts piled up, sought consolation not from his brethren but from those who were indifferent or hostile to the kingdom. (193:4.3)

but all these men grew to love Jesus and their fellow apostles more, and not less, as time passed. (193:4.3)

The habit of blaming someone for our disappointments, and the stubborn refusal to confide in those close to us, can be found in anyone. The remedy is simple: trust our brethren more, and slowly develop the ability to confide in them. (193:4.1, 193:4.3)

V. The Master’s Ascension — Farewell on Olivet

About half past seven that morning Jesus and the eleven reached the western slope of Olivet, overlooking Jerusalem and Gethsemane. Without being told, the apostles knelt in a circle about him. Jesus urged them once more to remain in Jerusalem until they were endowed with power from on high, and he left them his last words. (193:5.1, 193:5.2)

By the spirit fruits of your lives impel souls to believe the truth that man is a son of God, and that all men are brethren. (193:5.2)

About a quarter to eight he vanished from their sight. The text does not dramatize the scene. It says plainly that the so-called ascension was no different from the other times he had disappeared from view during the forty days. (193:5.3, 193:5.5)

This so-called ascension of Jesus was in no way different from his other disappearances from mortal vision during the forty days of his morontia career on Urantia. (193:5.3)

The journey that followed is recorded on a universe scale. The Master went by way of Jerusem to Edentia, where the Most Highs released him from morontia status and restored him to the status of a Paradise Son and the supreme sovereignty of Salvington. He went on to the right hand of the Father to receive formal confirmation of his completed sovereignty of Nebadon. (193:5.4, 193:5.5)

VI. Peter Calls a Meeting — The Twelve Disperse

At half past ten, one hundred and twenty of the leading disciples in Jerusalem gathered at the home of Mary Mark. Jesus’ mother Mary and his brother James were there. Peter gave a moving report of the last meeting and the farewell; this was the first council the disciples held after the end of the Master’s planetary career. (193:6.1, 193:6.2)

It was a meeting the like of which had never before occurred on this world. (193:6.2)

The apostles chose Matthias by lot to succeed Judas, though he took little part in their later work. After Pentecost the twins returned to Galilee, Simon Zelotes withdrew for a time, and Nathaniel, parting from Peter over preaching about Jesus, went to Philadelphia and on beyond Mesopotamia. Only six — Peter, Andrew, James, John, Philip, and Matthew — remained on the scene of the first proclamation in Jerusalem. (193:6.3, 193:6.4, 193:6.5)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 193 closes Jesus’ life on earth. In his last appearances he stressed the same thing again and again: the resurrection is a result of the gospel, not the gospel itself, and the gospel is the fatherhood of God and the brotherhood of man. And that gospel is carried less by words than by a life that bears the fruits of the spirit. (193:0.4, 193:2.2)
