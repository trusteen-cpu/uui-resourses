Introduction — John and Jesus Compared

Jesus began his public work at the height of popular interest in John’s preaching. The narrator draws the difference between the two men clearly: John was an eager and earnest worker, while Jesus was a calm and happy laborer. John preached the kingdom of heaven but hardly entered into its happiness. (136:0.1)

I. Concepts of the Expected Messiah

The Jews saw their history as running from Abraham to the new age of the Messiah. Their titles for the deliverer evolved from “the servant of the Lord” to “the son of David,” “the Son of Man,” and “the Son of God.” The rabbis had gathered almost five hundred passages said to foretell the Messiah, yet amid all those details they almost completely lost sight of his personality. They looked for Israel’s temporal exaltation rather than the salvation of the world. (136:1.1, 136:1.3)

They believed the Messiah would restore the Shekinah, the symbol of the Divine Presence no longer seen in the temple. On the evil nature of man they disagreed: some held that Adam’s sin had cursed the race, others that God had put both good and evil natures into man. Most believed the Messiah delayed because of the nation’s sins, which is why John’s call to repentance had such a powerful appeal. (136:1.4, 136:1.5)

Above all, they lacked the idea of the human and the divine united in one personality. They imagined the Messiah as a perfected human, as superhuman, even as divine, but never as the union of the two. This became the great stumbling block of Jesus’ early disciples as well. (136:1.6)

The incarnation of the Creator in the form of the creature had not been revealed beforehand. (136:1.6)

II. The Baptism of Jesus — The Personalization of the Adjuster

Many devout Jews of that time were baptized not for their own sins but for the good of Israel. Jesus’ baptism, too, was in no sense a rite of repentance or for the remission of sins. He was simply following the example of many pious Israelites. (136:2.1)

When Jesus went down into the Jordan, he was a mortal who had reached the pinnacle of human evolutionary ascension in the conquest of mind and self-identification with the spirit. Superhuman observers expected to witness the fusion of his Adjuster with his mind, but something new and even greater took place. As John laid his hands on him, the Adjuster took leave of the perfected human soul of Joshua ben Joseph, and in a few moments returned as a Personalized Adjuster. (136:2.2, 136:2.3)

This day ended the purely human life of Jesus. From then on the Personalized Adjuster was with him throughout his earth life. The narrator also corrects the chronology: counting Tiberius’s years as coemperor, the fifteenth year of his reign was A.D. 26, the year of Jesus’ baptism. Our own baptism or confession of dedication can likewise be more than a rite of cleansing—a decision to do the Father’s will. (136:2.5, 136:2.7, 136:2.8)

III. The Forty Days — Told That His Sovereignty Was Complete

Jesus had already overcome the great temptation on Mount Hermon before his baptism. The forty days of retirement afterward were not for fasting or afflicting the soul; he was no ascetic, and he came to destroy such notions. He withdrew to the Perean hills to adjust to his changed relationship with the universe brought about by the personalization of his Adjuster, and to plan his public work. (136:3.1, 136:3.2, 136:3.3)

In the hills he met Gabriel, and then the Constellation Father of Edentia appeared to declare that his sovereignty was complete and to bring him the bestowal release of Immanuel. He was now free to end the bestowal whenever he chose. (136:3.4, 136:3.5)

Technically, your work on Urantia and in the flesh of the mortal creature is finished. Your course from now on is a matter of your own choosing. (136:3.5)

According to Gabriel, two things had already been accomplished. The completion of his bestowal in mortal flesh had been demonstrated by the personalization of his Adjuster on the day of his baptism, and the end of the Lucifer rebellion had become a fact of history on the day he came down from Mount Hermon to join the lad Tiglath. The rest of his earth life, then, was not for winning sovereignty but was work he freely chose: the revelation of the Father. During this time James and John, the sons of Zebedee, often came near his shelter but never found him. (136:3.4, 136:3.7)

IV. Plans for Public Work — A Season of Decision, Not Temptation

Jesus first decided not to teach at the same time as John, until John’s work was finished or cut short. Recalling Immanuel’s instructions to leave no permanent writing, he never again wrote on anything except sand. He did not fast during the forty days; on the third day he went in search of food. Nor was he tempted by any evil spirits or rebel beings. (136:4.1, 136:4.2, 136:4.3)

Gabriel reminded him that there were two ways he might manifest himself. On the third day he promised himself to return to the world and finish his earth career, always choosing the Father’s will. From then on the mind of man became the mind of God, though the human selfhood remained, and that mind ever said, “Not my will but yours be done.” (136:4.4, 136:4.6, 136:4.9)

He lived in an ancient rock cavern in the hills near a village called Beit Adis and drank from a small spring. These forty days were neither the visions of a starved mind nor the symbolism later recorded as the “temptations of Jesus in the wilderness.” He reviewed the whole history of mankind on Urantia, from the days of Andon and Fonta through Adam’s default to the ministry of Melchizedek, and laid plans to serve this world and the other rebellion-isolated spheres. (136:4.5, 136:4.14)

V–X. The Six Decisions

The following sections present, one by one, the decisions Jesus made in the hills. The common principle is single: not to use superhuman power for himself, to work within natural law and the existing social order, and to surrender everything to the Father’s will. (136:6.2)

The first decision had one exception. Since a Personalized Adjuster is a nontime being, it could fully limit the heavenly hosts as to space but not as to time. So, unless the Father ruled otherwise, any supernatural element in Jesus’ ministry pertained wholly to the elimination of time, and to prevent time miracles he had to remain constantly time conscious. (136:5.4, 136:5.5)

Man shall not live by bread alone but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God. (136:6.4)

He might use his superhuman power for others, but for himself, never, and he held to this even when mocked on the cross. In light of this decision, the narrator states plainly that he never walked on the water. Prostituting divine gifts for personal aggrandizement was precisely the sin of Lucifer and Caligastia. (136:6.5, 136:6.9, 136:7.3)

Overawing people with miracles would call forth only outward allegiance; it would neither reveal God nor save men. Jesus chose natural, ordinary, difficult, and trying methods, the very procedures his children would later have to follow in extending the kingdom. For those of us who look for shortcuts, his choice is a reminder of the worth of the slow and sure way. (136:8.2, 136:8.5, 136:8.6)

The narrator frankly admits that this inner dialogue held a human element of questioning and near-doubting. If he would do just one unnatural thing, the human mind would know for certain that it was subject to a divine mind. But Jesus judged that it was not the Father’s will for divinity to make that concession to doubt. And because he was incarnate in the mind of a first-century man, his humanity was naturally shaped by the heredity, environment, and education of his time. (136:8.3, 136:8.7)

You shall worship the Lord your God and him only shall you serve. (136:9.3)

He saw that God’s way would not be easy and that the cup of the rest of his human experience might be bitter, but he decided to drink it. Throughout his public ministry he faced the clamor to be fed, the insistence on miracles, and the request to make him king, yet he never departed from the decisions made in the hills. On the last day he gave his pledge to the Adjuster and came down the mountain with his face shining with the glory of spiritual victory. (136:9.4, 136:9.13, 136:10.1)

And in all other matters, as in these now of decision-record, I pledge you I will be subject to the will of my Father. (136:10.1)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 136 shows the principles on which Jesus’ public life was built. He held all power, yet he did not use it for himself, for display, or for rule. In doing so he chose a path that would disappoint the Jews’ expectations and lead to his rejection. But that path was the way to reveal the Father. (136:9.6, 136:9.12)
