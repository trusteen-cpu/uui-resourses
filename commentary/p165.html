Introduction — The Master’s Final Ministry

On January 3, A.D. 30, Abner, chief of the seventy, gave his associates their final instructions before sending them out to the cities and villages of Perea. This Perean mission lasted almost three months and was the Master’s last ministry; when it was over, Jesus went straight to Jerusalem to pass through his final experiences in the flesh. The seventy worked in thirty-five cities and towns and in about fifty villages. (165:0.1)

The text spells out the character of this period. There were no miracles, and most of the ministry to the sick was carried out by the women’s corps, now sixty-two strong. Perea, half gentile and half Jew, was the most beautiful district in Palestine; it was more thoroughly worked than any other, and its better classes of citizens widely received the Master’s teaching. (165:0.2, 165:0.3)

I. At the Pella Camp — Choosing to Finish at the Height

By the middle of January more than twelve hundred people had gathered at Pella. Jesus usually taught the multitude at nine in the morning, the apostles taught in the afternoon, and in the evening he held question-and-answer sessions with about fifty advanced disciples. By the middle of March his morning audiences numbered more than four thousand. (165:1.1, 165:1.2)

The text says that Jesus chose to finish his work on earth when interest in his message was at its height — the highest point reached in the second, miracle-free phase of his ministry. Three quarters of the crowd were seekers after truth, but Pharisees and fault-finders were mixed in among them. Once Abner and the seventy set out on their mission, they never again returned to Pella. (165:1.2, 165:1.3)

II. Sermon on the Good Shepherd — Both Door and Shepherd

After the feast of dedication more than three hundred Pharisees and others followed Jesus from Jerusalem to Pella. With friends and enemies both present, he said he would speak in parables, and that each should take what found a welcome in his own heart. He pointed out that the sin of teachers who claim to see remains upon them. (165:2.1, 165:2.2, 165:2.3)

Jesus said that whoever would be an undershepherd must lead the flock to green pastures and still waters. Then he declared that he was both the door of the sheepfold and the true shepherd, that he would not flee in the face of danger, and that he would finish the service of completing his Father’s will. (165:2.6, 165:2.7, 165:2.8)

I have come that you all may have life and have it more abundantly. (165:2.8)

He added that he had other sheep not of this fold, and that these words did not refer to this world alone. All would come to know the voice of the one shepherd and be led into one brotherhood of the sons of God. He also said that no one could take his life from him; he had the power both to lay it down and to take it up again. (165:2.9, 165:2.10)

No man nor any other creature can take away my life. (165:2.10)

The Pharisees declared him mad and went out into the night, but some of the Jerusalem teachers said he spoke like one having authority. The next day about half of those teachers professed belief in Jesus. (165:2.11, 165:2.12)

III. Sabbath Sermon at Pella — Trust and Spiritual Preparedness

On January 28, before nearly three thousand people, Jesus preached on “Trust and Spiritual Preparedness.” He warned them to beware of the leaven of the Pharisees, which is hypocrisy, born of prejudice and nurtured in tradition, though he added that many of the Pharisees were honest of heart. And he told them not to fear those who can kill only the body. (165:3.1, 165:3.2, 165:3.3)

And if all of this is true, why should you live in fear of the many trifles which come up in your daily lives? (165:3.4)

Not one sparrow exists without the Father’s knowledge, and to the seraphic guardians even the hairs of our heads are numbered. He warned too that the deliberate rebel who knowingly ascribes the works of God to the forces of evil will seldom seek forgiveness. And he promised that when they were brought before high authorities, the indwelling spirit would teach them what to say. (165:3.4, 165:3.6, 165:3.7)

the spirit that dwells within you shall certainly teach you in that very hour what you should say in honor of the gospel of the kingdom. (165:3.7)
I came into this world to reveal the Father to you and to lead you to the Father. The first I have done, but the last I may not do without your consent (165:3.8)

These words show concisely how God’s invitation and man’s response fit together. The Father never compels anyone to enter the kingdom. For the one who lingers in the valley of decision, the only thing left is his or her own consent. (165:3.8)

When the sermon ended, many went down to the Jordan to be baptized by the apostles. The teaching to lay aside fear led at once to decision. Meanwhile Jesus continued teaching, listening to the questions of those who stayed behind. (165:3.9)

IV. Dividing the Inheritance — Rich Toward God

While the apostles were baptizing, a young man asked Jesus to make his brother divide an inheritance with him. Jesus asked who had made him a divider over them and then told everyone to beware of covetousness. He did not meddle even in the temporal affairs of his own apostles. Yet he used the question as an occasion for deeper teaching. (165:4.1, 165:4.4)

a man’s life consists not in the abundance of the things which he may possess. (165:4.1)

He told the story of a rich man who tore down his barns to build bigger ones, stored up his goods, and planned to take his ease. That very night robbers killed him, plundered and burned his barns, and his heirs quarreled over what was left. He had laid up treasure on earth but was not rich toward God. (165:4.2, 165:4.3)

Asked whether it was a sin to possess honest wealth, Jesus answered that honorable wealth is not a sin. But when wealth becomes a treasure that absorbs one’s interests and turns one’s affections away from spiritual pursuits, it is sin. He pointed to David Zebedee’s tent city, which was kept going by the gifts of generous people. (165:4.5)

Fail not to recognize the danger of wealth’s becoming, not your servant, but your master. (165:4.6)

Earlier, the young man had felt that Jesus treated him harshly, and while the others watched the baptisms he came back privately. Jesus heard him out again and told him that the synagogue court would administer the laws of inheritance fairly. His own work, he said, was to make known the heavenly inheritance, and he urged the young man not to give way to covetousness on a day when he might eat the bread of life. His parting question was brief and weighty. (165:4.8)

My son, what shall it profit you if you gain the whole world and lose your own soul? (165:4.9)

The text makes clear that Jesus neither taught nor tolerated improvidence, idleness, indifference to the needs of one’s family, or dependence on alms. He taught that material things must be subordinate to the welfare of the soul and the progress of the spiritual nature. To someone who asked about the judgment of rich and poor, he answered that the lives people live will judge them all. (165:4.7, 165:4.10)

V–VI. Talks to the Apostles on Wealth; Answer to Peter’s Question — The Watchful Steward

That evening, at Andrew’s request, Jesus spoke to the apostles on the same subject, but he said his words to them must differ from his words to the multitude, since they had left everything to be ordained as ambassadors of the kingdom. Pointing to the ravens and the lilies, he told them not to be anxious about what they would eat or wear. (165:5.1, 165:5.2, 165:5.3)

Seek the greater thing, and the lesser will be found therein; ask for the heavenly, and the earthly shall be included. The shadow is certain to follow the substance. (165:5.3)

Looking ahead to the severe testing to come, he told them to gird up the loins of their minds and keep their lamps burning, for the Son of Man would depart at an hour they did not expect and in a way they did not foresee. The twelve sat in silence for several minutes. They had heard such warnings before, but never in circumstances like these. (165:5.5, 165:5.6, 165:5.7)

When Peter asked whether this parable was meant for the apostles alone, Jesus answered that he would entrust the welfare of his children to the faithful steward who had endured trial. By contrast, the servant who thinks his master delays his coming and begins to mistreat his fellows will be cast out in disgrace. (165:6.1, 165:6.2, 165:6.3)

In the time of testing, a man’s soul is revealed; trial discloses what really is in the heart. (165:6.2)
Remember, much has been given to you; therefore will much be required of you. (165:6.3)

Jesus said honestly that his mission would not bring peace to the material affairs of men, at least not for a time. The gospel would cause division even within families. Yet each believer would have great and lasting peace in his own heart, and they were still to proclaim this gospel to all the world. (165:6.3)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 165 opens the period in which, with miracles gone, the teaching grows deepest. As a shepherd Jesus guards the sheep, and he teaches trust instead of fear, heavenly treasure instead of possessions, and watchfulness instead of complacency. In the end these teachings come down to one question: what is master of my life? (165:0.2, 165:4.6)
