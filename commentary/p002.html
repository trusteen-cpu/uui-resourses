Introduction — Where Do We See God’s Nature?

If Paper 1 introduced God as a Father who can be known, Paper 2 looks at that Father’s nature—the character he possesses. It takes up in turn his infinity, his perfection, his justice, his mercy, his love, his goodness, and his truth and beauty. At the outset the author makes clear that the best window on the divine nature is not an abstract definition but the life of Jesus of Nazareth. (2:0.1, 2:0.2)

At the same time the author frankly admits the limits of the task. The mortal mind has limited capacity, and human language is poor in the illustrations needed to portray divine values. The attempt is not futile, however, because the mortal mind is indwelt by the Adjuster and pervaded by the Spirit of Truth. Understanding God becomes possible, in the end, when the God within us helps. (2:0.3)

I. The Infinity of God — The Immeasurable One Draws Near

The first section gathers the confessions of ancient scripture to show God’s infinity. God is fully self-conscious of his infinity and perfection; he is neither a cosmic accident nor a universe experimenter. The Universe Sovereigns may engage in adventure and the Constellation Fathers may experiment, but the Father sees the end from the beginning. For him there is no past or future; all time is present. (2:1.3, 2:1.4, 2:1.5)

No thing is new to God, and no cosmic event ever comes as a surprise (2:1.5)

Yet this very infinity shuts him off from direct personal communication with finite creatures. So the Father, in love, downsteps and dilutes his infinity to make a way of drawing near to his children. The paper names three such ways. (2:1.6, 2:1.8)

Source 2:1.7

The close of the section shows that infinity is not a concept that pushes us away. Man cannot encompass infinity, but the infinite Father fully comprehends and lovingly embraces all the finity of his children. That is why the words “In Him we live and move and have our being” are literal truth. The Adjuster within us is itself a fragment of that infinity. (2:1.10, 2:1.11)

II. The Father’s Eternal Perfection — A Purpose That Never Changes

God’s plans and purposes are like himself: eternal, perfect, and forever changeless. He does not repent of the wise purposes he first set. When the attitudes of his creatures change, his reactions may seem to change as well—but only on the surface. Beneath every outward manifestation flows the same unchanging purpose. (2:2.1, 2:2.2, 2:2.3)

What deserves special notice in this section is the character of that perfection. God’s perfection is not an assumed righteousness but the inherent perfection of the goodness of his nature. And the perfect One does not keep to himself. The whole scheme of life on the worlds of space is centered on lifting will creatures to share the Father’s perfection. (2:2.5)

God is neither self-centered nor self-contained; he never ceases to bestow himself upon all self-conscious creatures (2:2.5)

Remarkably, the perfect God, who cannot personally know imperfection as his own experience, nonetheless shares the consciousness of all the imperfectness experienced by the struggling creatures of the evolutionary universes. Our immaturity and our mistakes are not far removed from God; the Father actually participates in them. This is a comfort on the days we fail: we are not alone. (2:2.6)

III. Justice and Righteousness — We Reap What We Sow

God is righteous, and therefore he is just. His justice is not swayed by the deeds of his creatures; it shows no respect of persons and takes no gifts. So, the paper says, it is futile to make childish appeals to God in the hope of escaping the just consequences of his natural laws and spiritual mandates. (2:3.1, 2:3.2)

whatsoever a man sows that shall he also reap (2:3.2)

The same paragraph at once restores the balance. Even in the reaping of the harvest of wrongdoing, divine justice is always tempered with mercy. The final result of wholehearted sin is annihilation, yet this is less a penalty imposed by God than the outcome of a creature becoming wholly unreal through the embrace of iniquity. And even that end is delayed until the ordained order of justice has been fully complied with. (2:3.2)

Beneath this section lies a simple principle. Undiluted evil and willful sin are inherently suicidal; what is unreal survives for a time only because of the tolerance of mercy. In the local universes the Creator Sons devote themselves to the rehabilitation of rebels, and the decree of dissolution is executed only when all such loving efforts are finally and forever rejected. (2:3.5, 2:3.6)

IV. The Divine Mercy — Justice Tempered by Wisdom

The fourth section defines mercy not as the opposite of justice but as a form of it. God knows perfectly the natural weaknesses and environmental handicaps of his creatures, and the wisdom that grows from that knowledge tempers his justice. That is mercy. (2:4.1)

Mercy is simply justice tempered by that wisdom which grows out of perfection of knowledge and the full recognition of the natural weaknesses and environmental handicaps of finite creatures. (2:4.1)

Nor is God’s mercy something anyone has to coax out of him. The creature’s need alone is enough to ensure the full flow of the Father’s mercy. Because God knows all about his children, forgiveness is easy for him. The paper adds that the same principle holds between people. (2:4.2)

The better man understands his neighbor, the easier it will be to forgive him, even to love him. (2:4.2)

God is never torn between justice and mercy, because infinite wisdom chooses the conduct that satisfies the demands of all his attributes perfectly, simultaneously, and equally. Unlike us, who are forever pulled between the two, God knows no conflict of attitudes. Together, eternal justice and divine mercy constitute what in human experience would be called fairness. (2:4.3, 2:4.4)

V. The Love of God — The Adjuster as Proof

“God is love.” His only personal attitude toward the affairs of the universe is therefore always a reaction of divine affection. The paper plainly calls it wrong to think that God is coaxed into loving his children by the sacrifices of his Sons or the intercession of others. The Father himself loves us, and it is in response to that affection that he sends the Adjusters. (2:5.1, 2:5.2)

The author says that the supreme reason for loving God is the Adjuster patiently waiting within us. Though we cannot find God by searching, if we submit to the leading of the indwelling spirit, we will be guided step by step and life by life until at last we stand in the Father’s presence. The distance in space and the gulf of spiritual difference are great, but God has already bridged that gulf in his own way. (2:5.5, 2:5.6)

The experience of loving is very much a direct response to the experience of being loved. (2:5.8)

The author’s own confession is striking. He says he would love God just as much if he were not so great and powerful, so long as he is good and merciful. We love the Father more for his nature than for his amazing attributes. Yet God’s love is not blind. It is an intelligent and farseeing parental affection that works in unity with divine wisdom. (2:5.7, 2:5.10)

The paper also sums up where God’s love is shown. Its greatest manifestation to mortals is the bestowal of the Thought Adjusters; its greatest revelation is the bestowal life of Michael, who lived on earth the ideal spiritual life. And it is the indwelling Adjuster who individualizes that love to each human soul. (2:5.10)

God is love, but love is not God. (2:5.10)

VI. The Goodness of God — He Loves as a Father

Beauty may be seen in the physical universe and truth discerned in the intellectual world, but the goodness of God is found only in personal religious experience. The God of philosophy may be great and absolute, but the God of religion must be good. Man might fear a great God, but he trusts and loves only a good God. (2:6.1)

God loves not like a father, but as a father. (2:6.4)

From this standpoint the paper criticizes the atonement doctrine. To suppose that God’s righteousness cannot be reconciled with his love is to make him a divided personality, one of justice and one of mercy. But the Father needs no mediator in order to forgive; God as a father transcends God as a judge. God is never wrathful, vengeful, or angry. (2:6.5, 2:6.6, 2:6.7)

The saying “God loves the sinner and hates the sin” is also made more precise here. Persons can love and hate only other persons, and sin is not a person. So the love of God saves the sinner, and the law of God destroys the sin. The goodness of God is the universal tendency to love, show mercy, manifest patience, and minister forgiveness. (2:6.8, 2:6.9)

VII. Divine Truth and Beauty — The Unity of Three Values

The last section begins by teaching humility. All finite knowledge is relative; even information from high sources is only locally accurate and personally true. The philosopher’s gravest error is the “fallacy of abstraction”—seizing one aspect of reality and declaring it the whole truth. Truth is beautiful because it is both replete and symmetrical. (2:7.1, 2:7.4, 2:7.5)

The section says that all things in the universe move toward unification. The physical universe coheres in the Isle of Paradise, the intellectual universe in the Conjoint Actor, and the spiritual universe in the personality of the Eternal Son. The isolated mortal of time and space coheres in God the Father through the indwelling Thought Adjuster. The Adjuster within us is a fragment of God that forever seeks divine unification. (2:7.7)

The section also points out what goes wrong when religion stresses goodness in isolation. The Hebrew religion failed to associate the goodness of God with the truths of science and the beauty of art, and the isolated morality of modern religion fails to hold the loyalty of many. The remedy is a new philosophy of living that gives equal consideration to truth, beauty, and goodness. (2:7.9, 2:7.10)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

The God of Paper 2 is infinite yet draws near, perfect yet shares in our imperfection, righteous yet merciful. What binds all these qualities together is love, and that love has been given to us concretely in the Adjuster and in the life of Jesus. The paper shows that knowing God’s nature is closely tied to changing our own attitudes.

Paper 3 goes on to the attributes of God—his omnipresence, omnipotence, omniscience, and more. If we read it remembering the “Father who is love” met in Paper 2, we will find that even those vast attributes are other faces of his love for his children.
