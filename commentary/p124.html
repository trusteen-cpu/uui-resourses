Introduction — Why Nazareth and Not Alexandria

Paper 124 covers Jesus’ life from his ninth year to his first Passover at thirteen. The narrator begins by noting what it meant for him to grow up in Nazareth. Alexandria might have offered better schooling, but Nazareth gave him something Alexandria could not. (124:0.1)

Source 124:0.1
AspectHad he stayed in AlexandriaWhat he actually gained in Nazareth
EducationDirected by Jews, along exclusively Jewish linesA setting for working out his own life problems with a minimum of guidance
Contacts—Constant contact with men and women of all classes from every part of the civilized world
Result—Preparation to understand the gentiles, and a balanced view of the Eastern (Babylonian) and Western (Hellenic) views of Hebrew theology

The insight is that a wider life, more than a better school, forms a person. Most of the events in this paper take place outside school—at home, on the road, and at the caravan shop. (124:0.1)

I. Jesus’ Ninth Year (A.D. 3) — The Drawing and His First Workbench

Jesus loved drawing landscapes and modeling objects in clay. In late winter he challenged the chazan over the teaching that all images and pictures were idolatrous, and when he was caught drawing a charcoal picture of the teacher on the schoolroom floor, a committee of elders came to Joseph. Resenting their blame of his father, Jesus fearlessly faced his accusers, courageously defended his viewpoint, and with consummate self-control announced that he would abide by his father’s decision. (124:1.3, 124:1.4)

Joseph ruled that the rabbinical interpretation of the second commandment should prevail, and Jesus never again drew or modeled a likeness while he lived in his father’s house. Giving up a favorite pastime while still unconvinced that he had done wrong was one of the great trials of his young life. When conviction and obedience collided, he chose to state his view plainly while respecting the right to decide. (124:1.5)

After Martha was born in September, Joseph built an addition to the workshop, and for the first time Jesus had his own tools and a small workbench. The first ice he ever saw, and the steam escaping from boiling pots, set him thinking a great deal about the constitution of the physical world. (124:1.7, 124:1.8)

and yet the personality embodied in this growing youth was all this while the actual creator and organizer of all these things throughout a far-flung universe. (124:1.8)

That May he helped with the grain harvest on his uncle’s farm for the first time. Before he was thirteen he had learned something about nearly every kind of work done around Nazareth except metal working, and he often went with his father to Cana, Nain, and Sepphoris, the capital of Galilee. He came to know the lives of working people from the inside. (124:1.11, 124:1.12)

Before he was ten he had become the leader of seven boys who formed a society for promoting the acquirements of manhood. His trips away from home gave him a more generous understanding of his own family, and by now even his parents were beginning to learn from him. (124:1.13)

II. The Tenth Year (A.D. 4) — The Boy Who Would Not Fight

On the Sabbath of July 5, A.D. 4, while walking in the country with his father, Jesus first voiced thoughts showing that he was becoming self-conscious of the unusual nature of his mission. The next day he had a longer talk with his mother. Both listened attentively but volunteered no information, and almost two years passed before Jesus raised the subject again. (124:2.1)

His playmates saw nothing supernatural in him. He asked more questions than the others, but in most ways he was just like them. In one respect, however, he was different. (124:2.3)

Perhaps his most unusual and outstanding trait was his unwillingness to fight for his rights. (124:2.4)

He did not suffer much for this trait because Jacob, a neighbor boy a year older, appointed himself Jesus’ defender. Jesus was fair, and he possessed a rare, understanding sympathy that bordered on discreet compassion, so he became the well-loved leader of the boys his age. He loved talking politics and religion with older people, and he turned even play toward more serious channels. (124:2.4, 124:2.5, 124:2.6)

He was a born teacher and simply could not refrain from so functioning, even when supposedly engaged in play. (124:2.8)

After watching the fishing industry on the lake of Galilee, he nearly decided to become a fisherman; his father’s trade later drew him toward carpentry; and at last a combination of influences led him to choose to become a religious teacher of a new order. His calling was not settled all at once but narrowed through experience. (124:2.10)

III. The Eleventh Year (A.D. 5) — Scythopolis, and the End of Boyhood

Joseph and Mary were often tempted to show Jesus favoritism or to betray their knowledge that he was a child of promise, but they wisely refrained; on the rare occasions they did show a preference, the boy was quick to refuse it. At the caravan supply shop he gathered an amazing store of information about international affairs. (124:3.2, 124:3.3)

In May, on a trip with his father to Scythopolis, Jesus was delighted by the orderliness of the Greek city and thrilled by the games in the amphitheater. That night, when he suggested building an amphitheater at Nazareth, Joseph for the first and only time grew angry with his son and told him never to say such a thing again. (124:3.6, 124:3.7, 124:3.8)

He only replied, “Very well, my father, it shall be so.” (124:3.8)

Jesus kept that promise as long as his father lived. Yet he did not give up the idea of wholesome recreation, and later he tried to bring it, as far as Jewish practice allowed, into the program of his twelve apostles. Obedience need not mean abandoning an idea; it can mean waiting for its time. (124:3.9)

On June 24 Jude, the seventh child, was born, and Mary was very ill for several weeks. Just before he turned eleven, Jesus took on the responsibilities of the first-born son a year or two early. From the end of this year he gave himself more and more to deep meditation on how to keep both his obligations to his family and his mission to the world. (124:3.4, 124:3.10)

Never again did this youth find it possible to return to the childlike attitude of his earlier years. (124:3.4)

IV. The Twelfth Year (A.D. 6) — A Dual Nature, and Two Great Commands

Jesus began working regularly in the carpenter shop and was permitted to manage his own earnings, and he learned the discretion of concealing whatever might make him seem different from others. This year he went through many seasons of uncertainty, if not actual doubt, about the nature of his mission. It was hard to recognize a double origin within a single personality. (124:4.1, 124:4.2)

His naturally developing human mind did not yet fully grasp the reality of his dual nature. (124:4.2)

His parents increasingly realized that something superhuman resided in their son, but they never knew that he was the creator of the local universe. Jesus inclined more and more toward his father’s spiritual view, and Mary was hurt as she sensed her guidance being set aside. This breach of understanding widened with the years. (124:4.4, 124:4.5)

Joseph and Mary lived and died without ever learning that their son Jesus really was the Universe Creator incarnate in mortal flesh. (124:4.4)

From this time on he grew more tactful and considerate with his brothers and sisters. He always got along well with James, Miriam, and Martha; his difficulties at home came mainly from friction with Joseph and Jude, particularly Jude. He taught the home school for his siblings, and because the Nazareth synagogue was under a liberal school of rabbis, he was able to modify religious forms such as the family prayers. (124:4.3, 124:4.5, 124:4.8)

He did not fully understand the ban on images, but with his high concept of consistency he pointed out that kissing the doorpost parchment was idolatrous by the same logic, and Joseph removed the parchment. Above all, he was torn between two great commands. (124:4.7, 124:4.9)

V. His Thirteenth Year (A.D. 7) — Graduation and Assurance

In January of A.D. 7 his baby brother Amos was born. About the middle of February Jesus became humanly assured that he was destined to perform a mission on earth for the enlightenment of man and the revelation of God. Outwardly he was an average Jewish boy, but far-reaching plans were forming in his mind. (124:5.2, 124:5.3)

The intelligent life of all Nebadon looked on with fascination and amazement (124:5.3)

On March 20 he graduated from the synagogue school and was pronounced a “son of the commandment.” The elders made plans to send him to the academies in Jerusalem, but Jesus grew more and more sure he would never go. His father’s approaching death and the support of a large family would soon change all such plans. (124:5.4, 124:5.5, 124:5.6)

VI. The Journey to Jerusalem — “The Hour Has Come”

Now graduated, Jesus set out with his parents in a company of 103 to attend his first Passover. They avoided Samaria by going down the Jordan valley, and along the way he heard the old stories of Israel. (124:6.1, 124:6.3)

At no time in his life did Jesus ever experience such a purely human thrill as that which at this time so completely enthralled him (124:6.10)

In Jerusalem the family stayed at the home of a well-to-do relative of Mary’s. Amid the busy preparations Joseph took his son to visit the academy where he was to continue his studies at fifteen, but he was puzzled at how little interest Jesus showed in these careful plans. The boy’s heart was already turned elsewhere. (124:6.12, 124:6.13)

Deeply impressed by the temple services, he asked fewer questions than at any time since he was four, absorbed in his own meditations. He did ask why so many innocent animals had to be slaughtered, and his father’s answers did not satisfy him. On the day before the Passover Sabbath, affectionate pity for the spiritually blind multitudes filled his heart, and that night, for the first time, a messenger commissioned by Immanuel appeared to him. (124:6.14, 124:6.15)

“The hour has come. It is time that you began to be about your Father’s business.” (124:6.15)

This visit, which came even before the heavy burdens of the family fell on him, announced that the hour had come to resume the responsibilities of a universe. It was the first act in a long series of events that culminated in the completion of the bestowal. The narrator confesses that even for the midwayers the mystery grew deeper over time. (124:6.16, 124:6.17)

As time passed, the mystery of the incarnation became, to all of us, more and more unfathomable. (124:6.17)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 124 shows divine self-consciousness slowly emerging within the ordinary growth of a boy. He gave up drawing, set aside his dream of an amphitheater, and suffered between two commands, yet he never avoided his duty. So ends the career of the Nazareth lad, and so begins the story of a youth striving to integrate his expanding life purpose with his obligations to his family. (124:6.18)
