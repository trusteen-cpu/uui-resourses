Introduction — A Master Who Seemed to Come Too Late

It was a little after noon on Thursday when Jesus came over the hill near Bethany. Lazarus had been dead four days; he had been laid in the family tomb at the end of the garden on Sunday afternoon, and that morning the stone had been rolled across the entrance. The sisters believed that if the Master had only spoken a word of healing, Lazarus would have been made whole, so they thought he had received the news too late. (168:0.1, 168:0.2)

What puzzled them still more was the word brought back by the messenger on Tuesday: this sickness was not unto death. Mary, overwhelmed with grief, gave up hope, but Martha clung to hope until the moment the stone was rolled into place and posted a neighbor boy to watch the Jericho road. (168:0.3, 168:0.5)

When Martha met Jesus she fell at his feet and said that had he been there her brother would not have died, yet she voiced no doubt or criticism. She said she believed in the resurrection at the last day, and Jesus looked straight into her eyes and answered her. (168:0.6, 168:0.7)

I am the resurrection and the life; he who believes in me, though he dies, yet shall he live. (168:0.7)

Martha confessed that she had long believed him to be the Son of the living God. She called Mary secretly, hoping to avoid the many enemies from Jerusalem who were present, but that plan failed when all who had come to console Mary followed her out. (168:0.7, 168:0.8, 168:0.9)

When Mary too fell at his feet with the same words, Jesus’ soul was moved with compassion. But his human mind was deeply troubled, torn between love for the bereaved sisters and distaste for the outward mourning of those who harbored murderous intent. Among the mourners, of course, were also true friends of the family who grieved sincerely. (168:0.10, 168:0.12)

I. At the Tomb of Lazarus — Why Jesus Wept

Jesus asked where they had laid him, and as he walked to the tomb, he wept. Friendly onlookers said, “See how he loved him,” while his enemies sneered that if he loved him, why had he come so late? The narrator honestly admits that he is not wholly certain of the real cause of Jesus’ weeping, and offers several thoughts. (168:1.1, 168:1.2, 168:1.8)

Source 168:1.3
Possible reason for his tearsThe text’s explanation
Sympathy for the sistersHe felt a genuine and deep human affection for Martha and Mary in the loss of their brother
Mixed feelings about the mournersSincere grief was mingled with pretended mourning, and he always disliked outward displays of mourning
Reluctance to call him backKnowing the bitter persecution Lazarus would suffer because of his resurrection, he hesitated to bring his friend back to mortal life

The text adds a striking detail. Jesus appeared to act within the limited knowledge of the human mind, yet his Personalized Adjuster had issued an order, just fifteen minutes before Lazarus died, that Lazarus’s Thought Adjuster be held on the planet indefinitely. Whether the divine mind of Jesus knew in advance, the narrator says only that he cannot tell. (168:1.6, 168:1.7)

We do not know. We know only what we are herewith placing on record. (168:1.7)

When Jesus ordered the stone taken away, Mary hoped for a resurrection, but Martha hesitated, saying that after four days the body would have begun to decay. Jesus asked whether he had not said at the outset that this sickness was not unto death, and how long it would take them to believe and obey. The apostles and neighbors rolled the stone away. (168:1.12, 168:1.13)

The Jews of the time firmly believed that the soul might linger near the tomb until the end of the third day but departed before the fourth day dawned. A resurrection on the fourth day was therefore, for everyone present, an unquestionably genuine case. Here we see why Jesus had stayed two more days at Philadelphia. (168:1.14, 168:1.15)

II. The Resurrection of Lazarus — The First and Last on Urantia

At half past two on Thursday afternoon, about forty-five people stood before the tomb. They did not know that a vast host of celestial beings of every order, under the direction of Gabriel, waited close by for the signal. The text calls this event the greatest manifestation of divine power during Michael’s life in the flesh. (168:1.9, 168:1.10, 168:2.1)

A resurrection in the likeness of the mortal flesh involves far greater practical difficulties than the usual resurrection in morontia form, and requires far more celestial personalities and universe facilities. Jesus lifted his eyes, gave thanks to the Father, and then called Lazarus with a loud voice. (168:1.11, 168:2.2)

because of those who stand here with me, I thus speak with you, that they may believe that you have sent me into the world, and that they may know that you are working with me in that which we are about to do. (168:2.2)

SequenceWhat happened

2. The celestial host actsThey moved in unison in obedience to the Creator’s word
3. Twelve secondsThe lifeless form stirred and sat up on the edge of the stone shelf

5. The recordGabriel recorded that this resurrection in the form of the flesh was the first and last on Urantia

Apart from the apostles and the sisters, most fled home in fear. Lazarus asked why he was dressed in grave clothes and had awakened in the garden, and Martha had to explain his death and resurrection. He could tell nothing at all about the four days in the tomb. (168:2.4, 168:2.5, 168:2.9)

Time is nonexistent to those who sleep the sleep of death. (168:2.9)

Lifting up the kneeling Lazarus, Jesus told him that what had happened to him would be experienced in a more glorious form by all who believe this gospel. Then he said they should all go into the house and take nourishment for their bodies. These plain words, spoken just after the greatest display of power, show that this power is one with the love that cares for ordinary human needs. (168:2.7)

You shall be a living witness of the truth which I spoke—I am the resurrection and the life. (168:2.7)

III. Meeting of the Sanhedrin — A Death Sentence Before Any Trial

Many believed because of this, but others only hardened their hearts. Lazarus’s testimony strengthened the faith of believers, but it did little to change the attitude of the Jerusalem leaders; instead it hastened their decision to destroy Jesus. The same event produced opposite results according to the hearts that received it. (168:2.10, 168:3.1)

Source 168:3.2
The Sanhedrin’s movesThe text’s record
Friday’s meetingA Pharisee proposed an unprecedented resolution to put Jesus to death at once, without trial
Fourteen resignationsThe resolution never came to a vote; fourteen members withdrew and never returned
Five expulsionsMembers thought friendly to Jesus were ousted, leaving the council nineteen short
Testimony of Lazarus and his sistersThe council virtually admitted the resurrection but ascribed the miracle to the prince of devils
The reason for their fearThat all the people would believe in him, and that messianic hopes would bring conflict with Rome
It is better that one man die, than that the community perish. (168:3.6)

It was at this meeting that Caiaphas the high priest first voiced that old adage. Though warned, Jesus was not disturbed, and he rested over the Sabbath with friends at Bethpage. On Sunday morning he said farewell to the family at Bethany and set out on the road back to Pella. (168:3.6, 168:3.7)

IV. The Answer to Prayer — Ten Points

On the way to Pella the apostles were deeply troubled that Lazarus had actually died despite the promise that his sickness was not unto death. They kept coming back to the question of prayer and its answer. Jesus declined to discuss the details of the resurrection as beyond their understanding, but he answered their questions about prayer freely. The text summarizes his answer in ten points. (168:4.1, 168:4.2, 168:4.3)

Source 168:4.4
PointWhat it says
1. The finite and the infinitePrayer is the finite mind’s effort to approach the Infinite, so its answer is conditioned by the purposes of the Infinite
2. DelayAn apparently unanswered prayer often betokens a better answer delayed — Lazarus had been dead eleven hours when the word was spoken
3. Answers in eternitySome prayers are so vast they can be received only on Paradise
4. Recognized after survivalThe answer may be recognized only after the one who prayed has advanced to the spirit level
5. Translated answersPrayers distorted by ignorance and superstition are answered in altered form by spirit beings
6. Spiritual answersTrue prayers are addressed to spiritual beings and answered in spiritual realities
7. Faith’s delegationFaith grants in advance to those who hear prayer the right to answer it in their wisdom and love
8. Child and parentA parent may answer a child’s request by delaying, modifying, or going beyond it
9. Answers held in trustAnswers not yet timely are held in trust until we reach the level to receive them
10. The time-space factorEvery genuine spirit-born petition is certain of an answer, but the factor of time and space must be reckoned with
No sincere prayer is denied an answer except when the superior viewpoint of the spiritual world has devised a better answer (168:4.5)
Material beings can pray effectively only when they “pray in the spirit.” (168:4.9)

This teaching helps us understand prayer not as a device for getting wishes granted but as a relationship. It is always a child’s right to ask of a parent, and it is always within a parent’s duty to modify the answer by higher wisdom. The assurance that waiting is not refusal brings deep peace to those who pray without yet seeing an answer. (168:4.11, 168:4.12)

All genuine spirit-born petitions are certain of an answer. (168:4.13)

V. What Became of Lazarus

Lazarus stayed at Bethany until the day of Jesus’ crucifixion, the object of attention from believers and the curious alike. But the Sanhedrin, judging that killing Jesus would accomplish little while Lazarus lived to testify, decreed his death as well. Already suffering bitter persecution, he fled across the Jordan to Abner at Philadelphia. (168:5.1, 168:5.2)

Martha and Mary also disposed of their property at Bethany and joined him. Lazarus became treasurer of the church at Philadelphia and supported Abner in his disputes with Paul and the Jerusalem church; he died at sixty-seven of the same sickness that had taken him in his younger years. The persecution Jesus foresaw at the tomb did indeed follow him through life. (168:5.3, 168:1.5)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 168 records the greatest manifestation of divine power in Jesus’ life on earth, yet at its heart are a weeping Jesus, two waiting sisters, and a prayer whose answer seemed late. That even the greatest miracle could not change hardened hearts reminds us that faith depends more on an open heart than on evidence. And the teaching on prayer shows a way to keep trusting even while we wait. (168:1.9, 168:3.1, 168:4.5)
