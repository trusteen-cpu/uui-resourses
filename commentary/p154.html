Introduction — The Council at Tiberias and Herod’s Hesitation

On that Saturday night of the Capernaum crisis, while Jesus was comforting his disciples, special commissioners of the Sanhedrin were at Tiberias urging Herod Antipas to arrest him. This paper covers the three weeks or so from that night to the morning of May 22, when Jesus fled by boat. At its center is the way Jesus treated his followers and his family as the pressure closed in. (154:0.1, 154:7.1)

At first Herod refused to deal with Jesus as a political offender, and the text gives several reasons why. When the Jews threatened to report to Caesar that he was shielding a traitor, he ordered them out of his council chamber, and matters rested there for a week. (154:0.1, 154:0.3)

I. A Week of Counsel — Healthy Growth from the Lowest Ebb

From May 1 to May 7 Jesus held counsel at the Zebedee house, morning, afternoon, and evening, with the tried and trusted disciples only. About one hundred had the moral courage to brave the opposition of the Pharisees and openly follow him. On Friday of that week the Capernaum synagogue was closed to Jesus and his followers, and Jairus resigned as chief ruler and openly took his stand with Jesus. (154:1.1, 154:1.2)

Fewer than one hundred and fifty gathered at the last seaside meeting on May 7. The text calls this night the lowest ebb of popular regard, yet it adds that from then on a slower but healthier growth of favorable sentiment began. The compromising stage, in which material and spiritual ideas of the kingdom had been mixed together, now came to an end. (154:1.3)

a new following was built up which was better grounded in spiritual faith and true religious experience. (154:1.3)

II. A Week of Rest — Caring for the Sick Without a Miracle

On May 8 the Sanhedrin passed a decree closing every synagogue in Palestine to Jesus and his followers. Since each synagogue had always been an independent congregation, this was an unprecedented usurpation of authority, and five members of the Sanhedrin resigned. Only the synagogue at Hebron refused, not out of sympathy with Jesus but to defend congregational autonomy; shortly afterward it was destroyed by fire. (154:2.1)

That same day Jesus declared a week’s holiday for his disciples. He urged them to go home or to their friends, rest their troubled souls, and encourage their loved ones. There was no public teaching that week; Jesus visited families about the seaside and went fishing with David Zebedee. (154:2.2, 154:2.3)

Go to your several places to play or fish while you pray for the extension of the kingdom. (154:2.2)

During that week Nathaniel and James Zebedee were ill for three days with a painful digestive disturbance. On the third night Jesus sent Salome to rest and nursed them himself. He could have healed them instantly, but he did not. The text explains that never once in his life in the flesh did Jesus perform any supernatural ministry for his earth family or his immediate followers, and it gives the reason. (154:2.4)

The animal nature and the lower forms of will creatures do not progress favorably in environmental ease. (154:2.5)

Difficulties are not accidents to be avoided but part of the experience training provided for the growth of the soul. Problem situations, joined with the stimulus of effort, call forth the activities of mind, soul, and spirit. For the reader who asks in the midst of suffering why God does not rescue at once, this passage shows that the love that stays and cares may differ from the power that removes by miracle. (154:2.5)

III–IV. The Second Tiberias Conference; Saturday Night in Capernaum

At the second Tiberias conference on May 16, the Jewish leaders reported that practically every synagogue had been closed. Herod at first refused again, but then gave way: if the Roman ruler agreed, the Sanhedrin could take Jesus to Jerusalem for trial. When word of that agreement came on the night of May 21, he signed the decree authorizing the arrest just before midnight, yielding to pressure though he knew well that Jesus could not expect a fair trial in Jerusalem. (154:3.1, 154:3.2)

That same Saturday night fifty leading citizens of Capernaum debated past midnight over the question “What shall we do with Jesus?” but could reach no agreement. Apart from a few who thought he might be the Messiah or a prophet, the meeting split into four nearly equal groups. (154:4.1)

Source 154:4.1

His enemies argued that his teachings were impractical and that everything would go to pieces if everybody tried to live by them. The narrator grants that many well-meaning people say much the same today, but points out that they forget an important fact: a much better civilization could have been built on Jesus’ teachings, and sometime will be. (154:4.6)

This world has never seriously tried to carry out the teachings of Jesus on a large scale (154:4.6)

V. The Eventful Sunday Morning — Parting Instructions

Before dawn on May 22 one of David’s messengers brought word that Herod had authorized the arrest. David Zebedee called an emergency council for seven o’clock, and Jesus gave farewell instructions to the disciples who were soon to be scattered. He told them to seek God’s guidance and carry on the work of the kingdom regardless of consequences. (154:5.1, 154:5.2)

David assured him that the messengers would never lose contact, and that whatever happened to him, he had already appointed leaders to succeed him. At about half past seven Jesus began his parting address to nearly one hundred believers; though the occasion was solemn, he was unusually cheerful, once more like his normal self. (154:5.3, 154:5.4)

I am neither a teacher nor a preacher, but it is in my heart to do this, and none can stop me. (154:5.3)

VI. Jesus’ Family Arrives — A Meeting That Missed

About eight o’clock Mary, James, Joseph, Jude, and Ruth arrived at the Zebedee house. The night before, the Pharisees had worked to persuade Mary that Jesus was beside himself and that she and her sons should take him home. While Jesus was popular, the family had let matters drift; now that the people had turned against him, they felt keenly the supposed disgrace, and they feared for their own safety. (154:6.1, 154:6.2)

Source 154:6.1
Family memberAttitude toward Jesus
RuthBelieved wholeheartedly and continuously in the divinity of his mission; opposed the plan to dissuade him
Jude, James, and JosephKept much of their faith, but let pride cloud their better judgment
MaryTorn between love and fear, mother love and family pride; could never quite forget Gabriel’s visit

When Peter whispered that his mother and brothers were outside, Jesus did not break off his address but sent word that they should have no fear for him. Then, stretching out his hands toward the disciples in the room, he said that those who do the Father’s will are his family. When Mary heard this, she collapsed. (154:6.4, 154:6.5, 154:6.6)

For whosoever does the will of my Father who is in heaven, the same is my mother, my brother, and my sister. (154:6.5)

The text explains the circumstances carefully so that we do not read the scene as coldness. Jesus did not want their first meeting after so long a separation to become a public affair in the middle of his discourse, and he meant to see them once he had finished. But the news that the arresting officers were coming, and a commotion at the rear entrance, sent the company hurrying out the front to the boat, and he never saw his family waiting on the back porch. (154:6.10, 154:6.11)

While Mary was recovering in the garden, a woman in the room cried out that the womb that bore him was blessed. Jesus honored the courage to hear and obey the word above the honor of kinship. His family thought he had lost interest in them, little realizing that it was they who had failed to understand him. (154:6.7, 154:6.8)

No, rather is the one blessed who hears the word of God and dares to obey it. (154:6.7)

Jesus knew that people may be easily moved by a sermon and readily respond to logic, yet find it far harder to break with their past. So he did not reproach his family’s misunderstanding but accepted it as something that would take time. Years later, after the resurrection, when James joined the early movement, he suffered immeasurably for having missed that earlier association. (154:6.8, 154:6.9)

Jesus did not forsake his earth family to do his Father’s work—they forsook him. (154:6.9)
It is forever true that all who may think they are misunderstood or not appreciated have in Jesus a sympathizing friend and an understanding counselor. (154:6.9)

As he stepped into the boat, Jesus asked David to tell his mother and brothers that he appreciated their coming and had intended to see them. He left them the counsel to seek a knowledge of God’s will and the grace and courage to do it. For anyone who feels the pain of a family whose path of faith has parted from his or her own, this section offers deep comfort. (154:6.12)

VII. The Hasty Flight — Days with Nowhere to Lay His Head

At about half past eight Jesus, with the twelve apostles and twelve evangelists, rowed for the eastern shore. A smaller boat carrying six of David’s messengers followed to keep up communication. Jesus was never again to make his home at the Zebedee house, so long the headquarters of the kingdom. (154:7.1, 154:7.2)

For the rest of his earth life the Master had no settled abode. The party left their boat with friends near Kheresa and stayed for a time in the domains of Philip, beginning the eventful wanderings that would take them by way of Caesarea-Philippi to the coast of Phoenicia. (154:7.2, 154:7.3)

The Jerusalem officers arrived only after the boats had gone and spent nearly a week searching vainly around Capernaum. Jesus’ family passed that week in confusion, and found peace only on Thursday afternoon, when Ruth learned from David that her brother was safe and making his way toward the Phoenician coast. (154:7.4, 154:7.5)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 154 portrays a time when power was closing in from outside and even his own family misunderstood him. Through it all Jesus ordered rest, nursed his sick apostles with his own hands, and was cheerful at the moment of parting. The text’s observation that a better-grounded fellowship of faith began to grow from the lowest ebb of numbers is a hope for any community passing through a season of decline. (154:1.3, 154:5.4)
