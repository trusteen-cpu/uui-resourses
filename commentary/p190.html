Introduction — Jesus Living a Morontia Career

The risen Jesus did not leave at once; he stayed for a while on Urantia. The text explains that this period corresponds to the morontia career that the mortals of Satania pass through on the mansion worlds. On the very world where he had lived as a mortal, he himself walked the next stage of the ascending mortal’s path. (190:0.1)

The text says that the bestowal of life which enabled him to rise from death is the same gift of eternal life that he gives to the believers of the kingdom. Jesus’ resurrection, then, does not end with him alone. Believers too will rise on the morning of the resurrection with the same type of transition body. (190:0.2, 190:0.3)

The believers who saw Jesus after his resurrection were not deceived by visions; they truly saw him. The text says that this abiding faith in his resurrection was the central element that bound together all branches of the early gospel teaching. Teachers at Jerusalem and Alexandria, at Antioch and Philadelphia, differed on other points, yet were one in this faith. (190:0.3, 190:0.4)

The text also notes the role of Mary Magdalene. As Peter was chief spokesman for the apostles, she was chief spokesman and leading teacher of the women’s corps. That this cautious woman boldly spoke to a stranger she took for the gardener shows how great her astonishment was before the empty tomb, and how deep her love. (190:0.5)

I. Heralds of the Resurrection — David Zebedee’s Last Mission

Because the apostles did not want Jesus to leave them, they had taken lightly his words about his death and resurrection. So they did not believe the women’s report; of the ten in the upper room, only John was disposed, however faintly, to believe. Most of them supposed the Jews had removed the body. (190:1.1, 190:1.3)

The men received the women’s report in different ways. Most of those at the home of Nicodemus doubted, but Joseph of Arimathea and David, inclined to believe, hurried to examine the tomb and found everything as the women had said. They were the last to see the tomb in that condition, for at half past seven the captain of the temple guards, sent by the high priest, removed the grave cloths and threw them over the cliff. (190:1.2)

Having seen the tomb himself, David did not argue with the apostles. He simply stated his own belief, and as he left he dropped the bag of Judas into Matthew’s lap. Then he released the twenty-six messengers gathered at the home of Nicodemus from their oath and gave them one last assignment. (190:1.3, 190:1.5)

I heard the Master say that, after he should die, he would rise on the third day, and I believe him. (190:1.3)
Jesus has risen from the dead; the tomb is empty. (190:1.5)

The messengers did not wait to talk with those who had seen Jesus; they took David’s word and went. Even those who doubted somewhat carried the news with the same assurance and speed. They scattered to every center of believers, from Beersheba in the south to Sidon in the north, and from Philadelphia in the east to Alexandria in the west. (190:1.6, 190:1.8)

It is worth noticing that the first proclamation of the resurrection came not from the apostles but from laymen. Office does not guarantee faith, and this scene shows that trust and courage may move first. (190:1.7)

After sending out his messengers, David took his mother and joined Jesus’ family at Bethany. He accompanied Martha and Mary as they disposed of their property and went to join Lazarus at Philadelphia, and he left Bethany the day after he married Ruth, Jesus’ youngest sister, in early June. Jesus’ mother Mary was taken by John Zebedee to his home at Bethsaida. (190:1.9, 190:1.10)

II. Jesus’ Appearance at Bethany — His Brother James and His Earthly Family

From his resurrection to his ascension Jesus made nineteen separate appearances to believers. He did not appear to his enemies or to any who could not make spiritual use of the manifestation. The first and second appearances were to the women at the tomb and to Mary Magdalene; the third was to his eldest brother, James, at Bethany. (190:2.1, 190:2.2)

James had wanted to believe in his brother’s mission, but he had long been estranged from the work and deeply doubted the claim to be the Messiah. As he stood by the empty tomb of Lazarus, he felt as if someone had touched him on the shoulder, and a strange form spoke his name. (190:2.2, 190:2.3)

James, I come to call you to the service of the kingdom. Join earnest hands with your brethren and follow after me. (190:2.3)

When James moved to kneel, Jesus bade him stand, and the two walked in the garden for almost three minutes, talking of the past and the future. That afternoon Jesus appeared to twenty family members and friends and asked why they had lingered so long. One by one, the doubting family turned to faith. (190:2.4, 190:2.6)

Why have you lingered so long before choosing to follow the light of truth with a whole heart? (190:2.6)

When James rushed into the house crying that he had seen his brother, the family began at last to believe in the resurrection. He announced that he would not return to Galilee, and David, remarking that not only excited women but strong men were beginning to see him, hoped to see him too. Because of something said to him in the garden, James asked that this visit not be widely reported, and he allowed only Mary Magdalene to return to Joseph’s home. (190:2.5, 190:2.7)

There is something moving in the fact that the brother who had watched Jesus closely all his life and yet failed to believe received the first personal visit of the risen Jesus. Jesus did not rebuke his doubt; he called him by name and invited him to serve. Those closest to us may be slowest to believe, but the door stays open to the end. (190:2.3)

III–IV. At the Home of Joseph; Appearance to the Greeks — A Kingdom Without Distinction

The fifth appearance came at about a quarter past four that afternoon, before some twenty-five women believers gathered at the home of Joseph of Arimathea. As Mary Magdalene was telling what had happened at Bethany, a solemn hush fell, and Jesus stood among them. He gave the women, too, the commission to proclaim the gospel to all the world. (190:3.1)

In the fellowship of the kingdom there shall be neither Jew nor gentile, rich nor poor, free nor bond, man nor woman. (190:3.1)

At about the same time, at the home of Flavius, some forty Greek believers were discussing the news of the resurrection behind fastened doors. In his sixth appearance Jesus came among them and said that the Son of Man had appeared among the Jews but had come to minister to all men. Many of them had been at Gethsemane when Jesus was arrested. (190:4.1)

While the Son of Man appeared on earth among the Jews, he came to minister to all men. (190:4.1)

Mary Magdalene had witnessed four of the five appearances made so far. The Greeks were so overcome with awe and fear that none of them slept that night; they stayed awake talking of these things and hoping the Master would come again. That women and gentiles were among the earliest witnesses of the risen Lord matches perfectly the message of a kingdom without distinctions that he proclaimed. (190:3.2, 190:4.1)

Meanwhile the Sanhedrin, disturbed by the rumors of resurrection, met that evening and decided to expel from the synagogue anyone who spoke of it. They thought they were finished with Jesus, but the text says their real trouble was only beginning. Jesus left the apostles a few more hours for reflection before visiting them. (190:3.3, 190:4.2)

V. The Walk with Two Brothers — Hearts That Burned Within

Cleopas and Jacob, shepherd brothers from Emmaus, were walking home arguing about the reports of the empty tomb. The elder was half inclined to believe; the younger insisted it was a fraud. In his seventh appearance the morontia Jesus walked beside them, but even Cleopas, who had often eaten with him, did not recognize him. (190:5.1, 190:5.2)

Asked what they were discussing, Cleopas said he must be the only one in Jerusalem who did not know, and he told the story: a prophet mighty in word and deed had been crucified; many had hoped he would be Israel’s deliverer; and that day women had reported an empty tomb and a conversation with him. Jacob added that the two apostles who went to the tomb had not seen Jesus. (190:5.3)

Jesus said he knew that man’s teachings better than anyone and set about enlightening them. He reminded them that the kingdom was not of this world, that Jesus had foretold he would rise on the third day, and he went one by one through the promises of a deliverer in the Scriptures. (190:5.4)

At their door the brothers urged him to stay, and as he began to break the bread and hand it to them, their eyes were opened. The moment Cleopas said it was the Master, he vanished. Without eating, the two hurried back to Jerusalem to tell the apostles in the upper room. (190:5.5, 190:5.7, 190:5.8)

No wonder our hearts burned within us as he spoke to us while we walked along the road! (190:5.6)

The brothers had already been stirred by the teaching on the road, but their eyes were opened only when they sat together and shared bread. Understanding the truth and recognizing him as a person do not always come at the same moment. A small act of hospitality, urging a stranger to stay, made room for that recognition. (190:5.5, 190:5.6)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 190 follows the single day of the resurrection Sunday. On that day Jesus appeared first to those who had doubted most and to those most often overlooked: his brother James, the women, the Greeks, and two shepherds on the road. And the first to carry the news through the land were lay messengers. (190:2.1, 190:1.6)
