Introduction — The Family He Could Not Meet

In August of A.D. 29, before taking the twelve on a short sojourn to Caesarea-Philippi, Jesus arranged to meet his family at the Zebedee boatshop in Capernaum. David Zebedee and Jesus’ brother Jude had arranged for Mary and the whole family to come, but a group of Pharisees from Jerusalem got wind of it and installed themselves in Mary’s home. The family could not leave the house, and when Jesus heard of it, he gave up the visit. (157:0.1, 157:0.2)

The text records quietly that this happened through no fault of either side. It is common in life for two parties with good intentions to miss each other because of circumstances beyond them. The paper begins with such a missed meeting and goes on to record the decisive days in which it finally became clear to the apostles who Jesus was.

I. The Temple-Tax Collector — The Wisdom of Giving No Offense

As Jesus and his companions waited by the lake, a temple-tax collector came up and asked Peter whether the Master did not pay the temple tax. Peter was inclined to be indignant at the idea of supporting the religious work of their enemies, but sensing a plan to trap them into refusing the half shekel, he promised at once to pay. The money, however, was with Judas on the other side of the lake. (157:1.1, 157:1.2)

Let us give these men no occasion for offense at our attitude. (157:1.2)

Jesus told Peter to catch some fish, sell them, and pay the tax for all three of them. David’s secret messenger, who had overheard, and a fisherman friend handed Peter several baskets of fish and added money to the sale, and so the tax was met. The text explains that Jesus’ half-humorous remark—“maybe you will catch the fish with the shekel in its mouth”—later grew into a miracle story. (157:1.3, 157:1.4)

Source 157:1.4
SceneWhat actually happened

The story shows Jesus’ practical wisdom in avoiding needless conflict where no principle is at stake. It also shows the honesty of keeping one’s word, and how ordinary cooperation can turn into a miracle as it is retold.

II. At Bethsaida-Julias — Discern the Times Rather Than Demand Signs

On August 8 more than one hundred believers, evangelists, and members of the women’s corps gathered for a conference at Magadan Park, and Pharisees came too—now joined by some of the Sadducees. The hecklers demanded a sign of his authority, and Jesus asked how they could discern the face of the heavens yet fail to discern the signs of the times. (157:2.1)

To those who would know the truth, already has a sign been given (157:2.1)

At the evening conference they decided on a united mission throughout the Decapolis as soon as Jesus and the twelve returned from Caesarea-Philippi. Dismissing the company, Jesus warned them to beware the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees and to place their faith not in dead forms but in living experience. (157:2.2)

It is not the purpose of true religion merely to bring peace but rather to insure progress. (157:2.2)

This charge still holds for believers today. Respect for learning and tradition is valuable, but when it blinds the eyes and stops the ears, it becomes leaven. His counsel not to stifle feelings of love for a neighbor but to express them in intelligent ministry to that person’s real needs is a concrete guide for joining feeling to action. (157:2.2)

III. Peter’s Confession — “Who Say You That I Am?”

Early Tuesday morning the company set out for Caesarea-Philippi, with Mount Hermon in full view. Jesus wished to return to that mount of trial and triumph from his early ministry, hoping the apostles would gain new strength there for the trying times ahead. When they paused for lunch, he asked them for the first time a question about himself. (157:3.1, 157:3.2, 157:3.3)

Peter sprang to his feet and spoke, and the other eleven rose with one accord. Jesus said this had been revealed to them by his Father, yet charged them to tell no one for the time being. That night the apostles slept little, sensing that a great event in their lives had taken place. (157:3.6, 157:3.7)

IV. The Talk about the Kingdom — The Rock of Spiritual Reality

Since the baptism and Cana, the apostles had several times come close to accepting him as the Messiah, but each time some word or deed of Jesus had dashed their hopes, and they had long been in turmoil. The next day in Celsus’ garden the two Simons labored to bring their brethren to accept Jesus not only as the Messiah but as the divine Son of God. When the twelve rose to greet him, Jesus smiled and waved them to their seats, and they never again showed him such an outward mark of respect. (157:4.1, 157:4.2, 157:4.3)

Jesus asked whether, a full day later, they still held to their decision, and Peter confessed again. Jesus said that this belief was not human knowledge but a revelation of the Father’s spirit, and he declared that upon it he would build the brotherhood of the kingdom of heaven. (157:4.4, 157:4.5)

Upon this rock of spiritual reality will I build the living temple of spiritual fellowship in the eternal realities of my Father’s kingdom. (157:4.5)

He left spiritual guidance to the Father’s spirit, and to the apostles and their successors he delivered the keys of the outward kingdom—authority over the social and economic affairs of the fellowship of believers. The distinction matters: the authority to manage a community and the spirit’s guidance of each soul belong to different levels. The text adds that those who become self-conscious sons of God are the human stones of this living temple. (157:4.5, 157:4.7)

On this day Jesus began to believe in the faith of his apostles—all but one. He foresaw that even after the apparent wreckage of their hopes, their faith would go on to enlighten the world in a new light. (157:4.6)

V. The New Concept — Son of Man and Son of God

What was new in Peter’s confession was the clear recognition of Jesus’ divinity. The Messiah the Jews awaited was to be the “anointed one,” not one who sprang from divinity. The second confession emphasized his combined nature as Son of Man and Son of God, and it was on this truth that Jesus said he would build the kingdom. (157:5.1)

Source 157:5.2
Jesus’ approachContent and outcome
First — to live as the Son of ManHe sought to complete his bestowal as the Son of Man, while his followers regarded him as the Messiah
Second — to modify the Messiah conceptHe tried to reshape it so as to meet their hopes in part, but saw that this could hardly succeed
Third — to announce his divinity openlyHe acknowledged Peter’s confession as true and proclaimed himself a Son of God

Jesus decided to stop trying to convince them that he was not the Messiah. Instead he boldly revealed what he is and simply ignored their insistence on regarding him as the Messiah. It is an attitude of showing the truth plainly and leaving understanding to time, rather than endlessly correcting another’s misunderstanding. (157:5.3)

VI. The Next Afternoon — The Fourth Stage of His Life

After his popularity collapsed, the treasury was empty, but David Zebedee had foreseen this and had his messengers collect funds, which arrived in time to carry them until the Decapolis tour. Matthew, too, arranged for the money from the sale of his last property to go anonymously to Judas. At this point the apostles had confessed Jesus’ divinity, but they had no adequate idea of what it meant. (157:6.1, 157:6.2)

Yet from this time a new note appeared in the Master’s message. His one ideal of living became the revelation of the Father, and his teaching centered on presenting to his universe that supreme wisdom which can only be comprehended by living it. The passage reminds us that truth shown in a life goes deeper than truth explained in words. (157:6.2)

He came that we all might have life and have it more abundantly. (157:6.2)

The text says that Jesus exemplified in his own life what he taught: not an incessant struggle between soul and body, but the growth of the spiritual nature through living progress. His teaching that the spirit is easy victor over both and reconciles much of the warfare between intellect and instinct offers another way to those worn out from fighting themselves. (157:6.4)

I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners. (157:6.9)

That day in the garden Jesus told them to assume the obligations of sonship and follow him, that he had come to minister, that before Abraham was, he is, and that he and the Father are one. When he asked whether they could believe this even though his kingdom was not of this world and he had nowhere to lay his head, the apostles listened in stunned silence. (157:6.8, 157:6.11, 157:6.12, 157:6.15)

the Father and I are one. He who has seen me has seen the Father. (157:6.13)

VII. Andrew’s Conference — The Shadow of Judas

That evening Andrew held searching talks with each of his brethren, finding all of them heartening except Judas Iscariot. Worried by Judas’s attitude, he went to Jesus late that night, but Jesus only said there was nothing more they could do; Andrew should go on placing the utmost confidence in Judas and say nothing to the others. (157:7.1)

The other apostles went through the same trials, but they loved Jesus. Judas’s great mistake was that whenever Jesus sent them off alone to pray, instead of communing with the spiritual forces of the universe, he indulged in thoughts of fear and nursed doubts and feelings of revenge. The passage shows that, faced with the same trials, what separates people is what they fill their hours alone with. (157:7.3, 157:7.4)

On the morning of Friday, August 12, Jesus told the apostles to prepare for a journey to the mountain. He was beginning the fourth phase of his ministry as the Son of God, and as at his baptism, he wanted some of them present as witnesses to hear his authority for it. (157:7.5)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 157 marks the turning point at which understanding of Jesus moves from “a prophet” to “the Son of God.” Yet that confession begins not as knowledge in the head but as a revelation from the Father’s spirit, and what is built upon it is not an institution but a living temple of spiritual fellowship. The paper’s deepest invitation is that each reader may become a stone in that temple. (157:4.5, 157:4.7)
