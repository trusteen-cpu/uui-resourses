Introduction — Three Ministries That Raised Up Evolutionary Religion

Paper 92 follows the earlier papers on primitive religion and the origin of prayer and sums up the course religion took afterward. Its author, a Melchizedek of Nebadon, begins by noting that man possessed a religion of natural origin long before any systematic revelation. That religion grew out of man’s superanimal endowments and developed slowly through influences working within, and upon, savage, barbarian, and civilized man alike. (92:0.1, 92:7.16)

Source 92:0.5

1. The adjutant of worshipSuperanimal potentials for reality perception in animal consciousness — the primordial human instinct for Deity
2. The adjutant of wisdomThe tendency to direct adoration into higher channels and toward ever-expanding concepts of Deity
3. The Holy SpiritThe initial supermind bestowal — the capacity to realize the postulate of survival in concept and experience
Later added helpThought Adjusters, seraphim, and the Spirit of Truth — accelerating religious development

The co-ordinated working of these three ministries is quite sufficient to begin and carry forward evolutionary religion. Much of the potential of these divine agencies has never yet found expression, and much will be revealed as mortal religion ascends. Here the paper lays down its basic outlook: religion is neither merely man-made nor simply dropped from above. (92:0.5)

I. The Evolutionary Nature of Religion — From Fear to Love

Religion began with fear and ghosts and passed through stages of first coercing and then cajoling the spirits. Fetishes grew into totems and tribal gods; magic formulas became prayers. Having moved from nature worship through ghost worship to fetishism, mankind, the author judges, is now approaching maturity and ripening for real religion and the revelation of truth itself. (92:1.1, 92:1.2)

Religion is the last thing to perish or change in a race, and as a social institution it embraces rites, symbols, scriptures, altars, and temples. Its basic stimulus has always been fear. As civilization advances, fear is softened by reverence, admiration, respect, and sympathy, and then further conditioned by remorse and repentance. (92:1.3, 92:1.4)

One Asiatic people taught that “God is a great fear”; that is the outgrowth of purely evolutionary religion. Jesus, the revelation of the highest type of religious living, proclaimed that “God is love.” (92:1.5)

II. Religion and the Mores — An Institution That Clings to the Old

Religion is the most rigid and unyielding of human institutions, yet it eventually reflects the changing mores. Because it regards the ancient as sacred, it clung to old forms such as altars of unhewn stone, primitive fire drills, and unleavened bread. The author adds a word of advice: when modern readers are puzzled by things in old scriptures, they should remember that later generations feared to remove what their ancestors held holy. (92:2.1, 92:2.2, 92:2.3)

Nor can religious growth be hurried. A people absorbs from an advanced religion only what fits its own evolutionary status, and it outwardly accepts a new faith while actually adjusting it to its old mores. The author then draws an important line concerning conscience. (92:2.4, 92:2.5)

Conscience is not a divine voice speaking to the human soul. (92:2.6)

Conscience is merely the sum of the moral content of the mores of its time, and untaught by experience and unaided by reason, it can never be an unerring guide. This passage can be read as counsel not to equate our convictions with the will of God, but to test them continually by experience and reason. (92:2.6)

III. The Nature of Evolutionary Religion — Costly but Effective

The study of religion is like examining the fossil-bearing social strata of the past. Evolutionary religion has been preoccupied with rites and dogmas, often tainted by the chosen-people delusion, and its cardinal ideas—atonement, sacrifice, salvation, and the rest—go back to the age of primordial ghost fear. Unlike science, it makes no provision for its own correction, so revision must always be forced on it from outside. (92:3.1, 92:3.2, 92:3.4)

Always remember, the cults are formed, not to discover truth, but rather to promulgate their creeds. (92:3.1)

Only two influences can uplift natural religion: the pressure of slowly advancing mores and the periodic illumination of epochal revelation. The author records the merits and faults of evolutionary religion with balance. (92:3.5)

So we must be careful when criticizing evolutionary religion. It is what actually happened, a historical fact, and it is the sacred heritage of animal ascent, which must ever be refined by the censorship of revealed religion and the fiery furnace of genuine science. (92:3.3, 92:3.10)

IV. The Gift of Revelation — Five Epochal Revelations

Revelation too is progressive. It sorts and censors the religions of evolution, but because teaching too far removed from the thought of the age would not be received, it is always limited by man’s capacity of receptivity. Revealed religions are marked by belief in a Deity of final value and in the survival of personality after death. (92:4.1, 92:4.2)

Evolutionary religion pictures the circuitous gropings of humanity in quest of truth; revelatory religion is that very truth. (92:4.3)

It is striking that the fifth revelation, these papers themselves, admits its own incompleteness. No revelation short of the attainment of the Universal Father can ever be complete, and all other celestial ministrations are partial and transient. This candor, offered even at the risk of weakening its authority, asks the reader not for blind belief but for a reflective faith. (92:4.9)

V. The Great Religious Leaders — Seven Religious Epochs since Adam

Most great religious epochs have been launched by the life and teachings of some outstanding personality. People tend to venerate the leader even more than his teachings, because of an instinctive longing for help from above. That longing was designed to anticipate the Planetary Prince and the Material Sons; deprived of them, Urantia has tried to make good the loss by wrapping its human leaders in legends of supernatural origin. (92:5.5, 92:5.6)

The author finds the greatness of Moses in his wisdom and sagacity: others had greater concepts of God, but no one ever succeeded in leading so many people to adopt such advanced beliefs. The section closes with the hope that future teachers will work less to strengthen interreligious barriers and more to build a brotherhood of spiritual worship among the followers of differing theologies. (92:5.11, 92:5.16)

VI. The Composite Religions — The Religions of Twentieth-Century Urantia

The religions of the twentieth century offer an interesting study of the social evolution of the worship impulse. Some groups remain close to the ghost cult; others have long held to monotheism. Evolutionary and revealed religion are progressing side by side, blending into diverse theologic systems, which the author lists as eleven. (92:6.1, 92:6.2)

Source 92:6.2

The text sketches each briefly. The Hebrew religion is the link between the religions of evolution and those of revelation; Buddhism has shown remarkable adaptability to the mores of many peoples; Christianity, after Paul, became so thoroughly Occidentalized that many non-European peoples see it as a strange revelation for strangers. From the difficulty Mohammed’s followers had with the doctrine of the Trinity comes a general principle: it is always hard for evolutionary minds to accept advanced revealed truth suddenly. (92:6.16, 92:6.17, 92:6.18, 92:6.19)

Man is an evolutionary creature and in the main must get his religion by evolutionary techniques. (92:6.19)

VII. The Further Evolution of Religion — Unity in Worship

Religion can never become a scientific fact, nor can new religions be invented; they are either evolved or revealed, and the old is never lost but merged with the new. On this principle the author sets out a standard for the relations among religions. (92:7.1, 92:7.2)

The many religions of Urantia are all good to the extent that they bring man to God and bring the realization of the Father to man. (92:7.3)

For any group to regard its creed as The Truth bespeaks theological arrogance. Since every faith contains truth, it is far better to borrow the best in a neighbor’s living faith than to denounce the worst in his lingering superstitions. Religions can never achieve uniformity of creeds and rituals, but they can, and someday will, realize unity in true worship of the Father of all. (92:7.3, 92:7.4)

A child’s religious experience depends greatly on whether fear or love dominated the relationship with its parents. The author regrets that those who venerate the risen Christ have overlooked the man—the valiant and courageous hero Joshua ben Joseph. Modern man must make more readjustments of values in one generation than were made in two thousand years, and so religion must be redefined. (92:7.11, 92:7.12, 92:7.14)

True religion must ever be, at one and the same time, the eternal foundation and the guiding star of all enduring civilizations. (92:7.15)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 92 looks on the long history of religion with a fair eye. It hides neither the superstitions nor the harm of evolutionary religion, yet acknowledges it as the most effective institution that sustained civilization, and it says that revelation too is given progressively, within the limits of human receptivity. Its conclusion is a call to humility and respect toward other faiths, and to grow into a religion of love rather than fear.
