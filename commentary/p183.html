Introduction — The Son of Man Waiting Alone

After the prayers of Paper 182, Jesus woke Peter, James, and John and urged them to go to their tents and rest. But the arrival of excited messengers looking for David Zebedee stirred the camp, and only the eight apostles remained sound asleep. The Greeks, who had posted a sentinel, streamed out of their tents fully armed when the alarm was given. (183:0.1, 183:0.2)

Unable to disperse his followers, Jesus left them and went down to the olive press near the entrance to Gethsemane Park. He feared that if the apostles were present at his arrest, they would resist and be taken and perhaps killed with him. He knew, too, that while the plot began in the councils of the rulers, it had the full approval of the rebels. (183:0.3, 183:0.4)

Jesus sat down, alone, on the olive press, where he awaited the coming of the betrayer, and he was seen at this time only by John Mark and an innumerable host of celestial observers. (183:0.5)

I. The Father’s Will — Drinking the Cup Is Not the Same as Cruelty

Before entering the story of the arrest, trials, and death, the narrator guards against a misunderstanding. The fact that Jesus did the Father’s will in patiently enduring suffering and humiliation must not lead us to think that the actions of those who abused him were also the Father’s will. The Father’s will was that his Son should drink to the full the cup of mortal experience, from birth to death. (183:1.1)

These inhuman and shocking experiences which Jesus was called upon to endure in the final hours of his mortal life were not in any sense a part of the divine will of the Father (183:1.1)

The Father wanted the bestowal Son to finish his earth career naturally, as all mortals must. Just as ordinary men and women cannot expect a special dispensation to ease their last hours, Jesus chose to lay down his life in keeping with the natural outworking of events. The cruelty was willed neither by God in heaven nor dictated by Jesus’ archenemies; it was the work of thoughtless and evil mortals. (183:1.2)

every bit of all this astounding manifestation of hatred and this unprecedented demonstration of cruelty was the work of evil men and wicked mortals. (183:1.2)

This distinction matters for today’s reader as well. A faith that accepts suffering does not thereby justify the evil that caused it. Doing God’s will does not mean believing that God brought about everything that happens; it means walking on, in any circumstance, while committing oneself to the Father. (183:1.1)

II. Judas in the City — A Plan Grown Out of Fear

Leaving the Last Supper, Judas went with his cousin to the captain of the temple guards and offered to lead them to Jesus. But when they reached the Mark home, Jesus and the eleven had left fully fifteen minutes before and were on their way to the Olivet camp. Judas’s plan to take Jesus while the city was quiet had gone wrong. (183:2.1, 183:2.2)

Judas knew that more than threescore disciples were at the camp and that Simon Zelotes had a store of arms. Afraid that the eleven would hate him and seek to destroy him, he asked for forty armed soldiers. The Jewish rulers reminded him that Jesus had always preached nonresistance, but Judas was not sure all his followers would obey that teaching. The narrator puts his inner state plainly. (183:2.2, 183:2.3)

He was not only disloyal, but he was a real coward at heart. (183:2.2)

The betrayal is portrayed not as a decision of a moment but as an act that grew out of long-accumulated fear and suspicion. The more his anxiety to protect himself grew, the more Judas relied on more soldiers and tighter calculation. The passage shows how far fear can drive a person. (183:2.2)

Pilate was reluctant to be involved, but he granted the petition, thinking he could later right any wrong they might commit. The text notes calmly that more than an hour was consumed going from one authority to another. It is worth remembering that this hesitation to take responsibility for a decision recurs in the trials that follow. (183:2.3, 183:2.4)

III. The Master’s Arrest — “I Am He”

As the band with torches approached the garden, Judas stepped out in front. He meant to point out Jesus before the apostles could rally to his defense, and to avoid appearing connected with the armed guards. But Jesus, though he spoke to him kindly, greeted him as a traitor, and Judas was so confused that he made no attempt to flee. (183:3.1, 183:3.3)

Jesus made one last effort to save Judas from actually betraying him. Before Judas could reach him, he himself asked the Roman captain whom they sought and declared his identity. At his calm majesty, those in the front ranks fell backward. There was no longer any need for Judas to betray him, yet Judas went through with his plan in order to claim the reward and honors he expected. (183:3.4)

Friend, is it not enough to do this! Would you even betray the Son of Man with a kiss? (183:3.5)

The apostles and disciples were stunned. Disengaging himself from the traitorous embrace, Jesus stepped up to the guards and asked the same question again, this time adding words for the sake of his companions. (183:3.6)

I have told you that I am he. If, therefore, you seek me, let these others go their way. I am ready to go with you. (183:3.6)

The Roman captain was willing to let the apostles go in peace. But when Malchus, the high priest’s Syrian bodyguard, moved to bind Jesus’ hands without any order to do so, Peter drew his sword. Jesus raised a forbidding hand and stopped him. (183:3.6, 183:3.7)

Peter, put up your sword. They who take the sword shall perish by the sword. (183:3.7)

He could have commanded more than twelve legions of angels, but he did not. As he was bound, he asked only why they came out with swords and staves as if to seize a robber, when he had taught publicly in the temple every day. His followers fled into the ravine. John Mark, hiding in a shed, was seized by the coat by a soldier; he slipped out of the garment, escaped, and ran to tell David Zebedee. (183:3.7, 183:3.8, 183:3.9)

IV. Discussion at the Olive Press — The Scattered Sheep

After the arrest, the remaining apostles and disciples gathered at the olive press to decide what to do. Andrew, released from responsibility for managing the group, was silent. Simon Zelotes stood on the stone wall and urged them to go after the mob and rescue Jesus, but others reminded them of the teaching of nonresistance and the charge to preserve their lives. (183:4.1, 183:4.2)

By half past two in the morning the camp was deserted. Five apostles hid at Bethpage and Bethany, four hid in the city, and Peter and John followed to the home of Annas. As Jesus had directed, John stayed near him to the end and sent word through David’s messengers to the hiding apostles and to Jesus’ family. (183:4.2, 183:4.3, 183:4.5)

Surely, the shepherd is smitten and the sheep are scattered! (183:4.6)

They vaguely realized that Jesus had forewarned them of this very situation, but the shock of his sudden disappearance kept them from thinking clearly. At daybreak Peter wandered back to the camp in deep despair, and David sent him with a messenger to Andrew at the home of Nicodemus. The scene honestly shows that a warning heard in advance is not easily held onto once the crisis actually comes. (183:4.4, 183:4.6)

It is worth noting that even in their scattering, David Zebedee’s messenger service kept all the groups in touch. In a time of confusion, one person’s steady service can become the bond of a community. Jude, Jesus’ brother, also arrived breathless and carried the news back to the family. (183:4.7, 183:4.8)

V. On the Way to the High Priest’s Palace — A Place for One Friend

Before they left the garden, the captain of the temple guards wanted to take Jesus to Caiaphas, while the Roman captain directed that he be taken to Annas. Since the Romans were in the habit of dealing with Annas on matters of Jewish ecclesiastical law, the Roman captain’s order was obeyed. Judas marched near the two captains, but neither would so much as speak to him. (183:5.1, 183:5.2)

When John Zebedee came up beside Jesus, the captain of the temple guards ordered him bound, but the Roman captain protected him. He recognized John’s courage: he had not drawn a sword in the garden, yet he had come forward to be with his Master. (183:5.3)

The Roman law allows that any prisoner may have at least one friend to stand with him before the judgment bar (183:5.3)

Hearing this, Judas was so ashamed that he dropped behind the marchers. The Roman captain told his assistant to see that the Jews did not kill the prisoner without Pilate’s consent and that John be permitted to observe. So John could stay near Jesus up to his death on the cross, while the other ten apostles remained in hiding. One officer’s fair judgment gave one disciple a place to watch to the very end. (183:5.3, 183:5.4)

From the time of his arrest to the time of his appearance before Annas, the Son of Man spoke no word. (183:5.5)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 183 records the night of the arrest calmly and sets two faces side by side: Judas, driven by fear into calculation after calculation, and Jesus, stepping forward himself to protect his companions. The paper teaches us to distinguish between accepting suffering and justifying evil. (183:1.1, 183:3.4)

Jesus could have called legions of angels, yet he used neither sword nor miracle, and to the end he put his companions’ safety before his own. Around him were Peter with his drawn sword, the scattered apostles, David keeping the lines of communication open, and John staying by his side. The reader may ask where he or she stands among them. (183:3.7, 183:4.5)
