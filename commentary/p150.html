Introduction — Seven Weeks, the Last Round of Galilee

In January of A.D. 29, Abner and the apostles of John, whose headquarters were at Hebron, came to Bethsaida for a joint conference with Jesus’ apostles. John’s apostles proposed making the anointing of the sick with oil a uniform practice for both groups, but Jesus’ apostles refused to bind themselves by such a regulation, and Jesus declined to take part in the discussion. A difference of practice was not settled by a rule. (150:0.1, 150:0.2)

On January 18 the twenty-four, joined by about seventy-five tested evangelists, set out on the third preaching tour. The evangelists went in groups of five, and the apostles went out two and two to baptize as occasion required. For seven weeks they covered Magdala, Tiberias, Nazareth, and the cities of central and southern Galilee; except for the northern parts, this was their last message to Galilee. (150:0.3, 150:0.4)

I. The Women’s Evangelistic Corps — A Proclamation of Emancipation

The narrator says that of all the daring things Jesus did, the most amazing was his sudden announcement on the evening of January 16 that the next day ten women would be set apart for the work of the kingdom. These women had served in the former encampment and the tented infirmary, and neither they nor their teachers had imagined that women would be commissioned to teach the gospel. (150:1.1)

On the morrow we will set apart ten women for the ministering work of the kingdom. (150:1.1)

The women organized themselves and, after the first funds for equipment, provided their own support. In an age when women were confined to the synagogue gallery, their commissioning stunned even the twelve. The narrator calls Jesus’ charge to them an emancipation proclamation for all women and for all time, while recording that later generations fell back into the old customs. (150:1.2, 150:1.3)

no more was man to look upon woman as his spiritual inferior (150:1.3)

The whole country was stirred and Jesus’ enemies made great capital of it, but women believers everywhere stood stanchly behind their chosen sisters. In the early church, the narrator says, women teachers and ministers were called deaconesses and given general recognition. Paul, however, though he conceded all this in theory, never really made it part of his own attitude. The passage shows the gap between knowing a principle and living it. (150:1.3)

II. The Stop at Magdala — The Fruit of the Women’s Ministry

With the women’s corps in place, women believers could far more easily confer with Jesus or the apostles. At Magdala the wisdom of the choice was first demonstrated. The apostles were bound by strict rules about personal work with women of questionable character, but the women evangelists could freely enter the evil resorts, preach the glad tidings directly, and draw close to their afflicted sisters. (150:2.1, 150:2.2)

As a result, Mary Magdalene was won for the kingdom. The narrator explains that a succession of misfortunes and the attitude of reputable society had brought her to one of Magdala’s resorts. Martha and Rachel made plain to her that the doors of the kingdom were open even to her, and she was baptized the next day. She became the most effective gospel teacher among the twelve women. (150:2.2, 150:2.3)

notwithstanding the apostles all fled but one, these women were all present, and not one either denied or betrayed him (150:2.3)

III. Sabbath at Tiberias — Magic and Superstition

At Jesus’ direction the Sabbath service was placed in the women’s hands, and Joanna led it in the banquet room of Herod’s new palace. That evening, when Andrew asked whether a bright new star signified the birth of a great man, Jesus entered on a thoroughgoing discussion of human superstition, which the narrator summarizes in modern terms. (150:3.1, 150:3.2)

The heart of this talk is to give up every attempt to move the spiritual world by material means. The urge to lean on omens and luck in anxious times is common even today. Jesus teaches us to fill that place with a direct relationship with the indwelling spirit. (150:3.6, 150:3.12)

IV. Sending the Apostles out Two and Two — Fear Not

Saying that the harvest was plenteous but the laborers few, Jesus kept the younger teachers with him and sent the apostles out in pairs to pass quickly through Galilee. This time they were not to go to gentile cities or Samaria, but to the lost sheep of the house of Israel, proclaiming the saving truth that man is a son of God. (150:4.1, 150:4.2)

Jesus warned them in advance that peace would not always attend their preaching. When only part of a family receives the gospel, division and sorrow can follow, so they were to labor to save the whole family. It was a sending-forth that joined comfort with realistic warning. (150:4.3)

V–VI. What Must I Do to Be Saved? — Salvation as a Gift; The Evening Lessons

At Shunem Rachel asked how to answer women who ask what they must do to be saved. Jesus’ answer was simple: believe the gospel, accept divine forgiveness, and by faith recognize the indwelling spirit of God. Entrance into the kingdom is wholly free, but growth in grace is essential to continuing in it. (150:5.1, 150:5.2)

You cannot buy salvation; you cannot earn righteousness. (150:5.5)

The women rejoiced all the more to know they were included in the plans for the kingdom. In the evening lessons that followed, Jesus spoke on many subjects—the love of God, dreams and visions, humility and meekness, music and worship, doubts and unbelief—and with the older apostles away, the younger men and women joined the discussions more freely. (150:5.4, 150:6.1)

Jesus moved from group to group, spending two or three days with each, and knew where all the workers were through David’s messengers. News from the other groups was always a source of encouragement to the scattered workers. That news of one another strengthens those who labor far apart holds just as true for communities today. (150:6.2)

VII–VIII. The Sojourn at Nazareth; The Sabbath Service

On March 4 all the groups gathered at Nazareth, Jesus’ first visit since beginning his public ministry. Unrecognized, he walked past his childhood home and the carpenter shop, and not since his baptism had such a flood of human emotion stirred within him. But the town was hostile. There was resentment and jealousy that he had done no great works there, and his enemies had hired rough men to make trouble. (150:7.1, 150:7.2, 150:7.3)

In the synagogue on the Sabbath Jesus was handed the very scroll he had once presented to it, and he read from Deuteronomy and Isaiah: the word of life is not in heaven or beyond the sea but near, in one’s heart. Saying, “Today are these Scriptures fulfilled,” he spoke on the sons and daughters of God, and many marveled at his graciousness. (150:8.1, 150:8.8, 150:8.10)

The narrator gives the order of service in detail: a prayer blessing the One who forms the light, a prayer to the One who in love chose his people, the recitation of the Shema, and the first and last of the nineteen benedictions. The service ran just as it had when Jesus was a boy; he honored the old forms of worship while bringing new tidings within them. (150:8.2, 150:8.4, 150:8.6)

IX. The Nazareth Rejection — Disillusioned Preachers

In the question time after the discourse, rude taunts poured in. The people demanded that he work wonders in his hometown, but Jesus answered that the transformations of grace come in response to the faith of those who receive them. (150:9.1, 150:9.2)

The transformations of grace are wrought in response to the living faith of those who are the beneficiaries. (150:9.2)

The narrator records that Jesus could have managed the crowd good-naturedly, but the tactical blunder of Simon Zelotes, who gathered friends and took a belligerent stand, aroused the mob spirit. The crowd rushed Jesus to the brow of a cliff; but when he turned and quietly folded his arms, facing them, the mob parted and he walked through unmolested. (150:9.3)

They came together as a sober and serious group of disillusioned preachers of the gospel of truth and not as an enthusiastic and all-conquering band of triumphant crusaders. (150:9.5)

The disciples began to realize that the kingdom would come only through much sorrow and bitter disappointment. The scene holds two lessons: even anger meant to protect someone we love can make matters worse, and a community can grow more mature through failure. (150:9.3, 150:9.4)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 150 holds a bold beginning and a bitter ending. Women were commissioned as gospel teachers for the first time; a relationship with the indwelling spirit was set in place of superstition; salvation was proclaimed as a gift, neither bought nor earned. And rejection in his hometown turned the workers into sober preachers without illusions. (150:1.3, 150:5.5, 150:9.5)
