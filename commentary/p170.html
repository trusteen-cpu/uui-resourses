Introduction — One Word, Many Expectations

On Saturday afternoon, March 11, Jesus preached his last sermon at Pella. The narrator counts it among the notable addresses of the public ministry, a full and complete discussion of the kingdom of heaven. Jesus used “kingdom of heaven” and “kingdom of God” interchangeably, but his hearers were confused about what the terms meant. (170:0.1)

The idea of a temporal king was too deep-rooted in the Jewish mind thus to be dislodged in a single generation. (170:0.1)

The problem was not the word itself but the expectations attached to it. The hope of an earthly king ran too deep in the Jewish mind to be uprooted in one generation. The narrator tells us that he will amplify this address with sayings Jesus gave on other occasions and with remarks made to the apostles that same evening, and that he will also comment on how the kingdom idea later worked itself out in the Christian church. (170:0.1, 170:0.2)

I. Concepts of the Kingdom of Heaven — A Tangled Inheritance

The Hebrew scriptures already held a dual concept of the kingdom. The prophets presented it both as a present reality and as a future hope to be fulfilled with the Messiah. To these were added the later Jewish idea of a transcendental kingdom and the Persian idea of a final contest between good and evil, and just before Jesus came the Jews had blended them all into an apocalyptic expectation of an age of Jewish triumph. (170:1.1, 170:1.5, 170:1.7)

In choosing the term kingdom of heaven, Jesus took up the most vital heritage of both the Jewish and Persian religions. Yet to his apostles he always taught the kingdom as a personal experience—man’s experience in relation to his fellows on earth and to the Father in heaven. (170:1.7, 170:1.13)

Concerning the kingdom, his last word always was, “The kingdom is within you.” (170:1.13)

The narrator traces centuries of confusion to three causes: the successive phases through which the kingdom idea passed as Jesus and the apostles recast it, the transplanting of early Christianity from Jewish to gentile soil, and the drift by which the gospel of the kingdom became more and more a religion about Jesus. (170:1.14, 170:1.15, 170:1.16, 170:1.17)

II. Jesus’ Concept of the Kingdom — Doing the Will of God

Jesus made it plain that the kingdom must rest on the truth of the fatherhood of God and the brotherhood of man. Accepting this teaching, he declared, would free man from the age-long bondage of animal fear and give him new courage and spiritual power, new comfort, a new moral standard, and spiritual attainment as the true goal of living. (170:2.1, 170:2.2, 170:2.6)

The central effort of the sermon was to translate the kingdom into the ideal of doing God’s will. In place of kingdom, king, and subjects, Jesus wanted to give the picture of a heavenly family, a heavenly Father, and liberated sons of God serving their fellows with joy. Yet the narrator records, without ornament, that he did not succeed. (170:2.11, 170:2.12)

The great effort embodied in this sermon was the attempt to translate the concept of the kingdom of heaven into the ideal of the idea of doing the will of God. (170:2.11)
Source 170:2.18
Two phases of the kingdomContent
The kingdom of God in this worldThe supreme desire to do the will of God; the unselfish love of man which yields the good fruits of improved ethical and moral conduct
The kingdom of God in heavenThe goal of mortal believers, where the love for God is perfected and the will of God is done more divinely

By this time the apostles saw the kingdom in two ways: as a personal experience already present in the believer’s heart, and as a world event still to come, suddenly and spectacularly. Jesus never tired of telling them that the kingdom was their personal experience of realizing the higher qualities of spiritual living, an experience that grows to ever higher levels of certainty and grandeur. This gradual growth is what the parables of the leaven and the mustard seed point to. (170:2.13, 170:2.16)

By faith the believer enters the kingdom now. Jesus named two things essential to that entrance: sincere faith, receiving sonship as a gift with the open mind of a little child, and truth hunger, the thirst for righteousness. (170:2.20, 170:2.21, 170:2.22)

Jesus taught that sin is not the child of a defective nature but rather the offspring of a knowing mind dominated by an unsubmissive will. (170:2.23)

He taught that God has already forgiven, and that when we forgive our fellows we create in our own souls the capacity to receive that forgiveness. Jesus tried many substitutes for the word kingdom—the kingdom of life, the family of God, the Father’s will, the brotherhood of man, and others—but in the end he could not escape the kingdom idea. (170:2.23, 170:2.24, 170:2.25)

III. In Relation to Righteousness — Religion the Cause, Ethics the Result

Jesus taught that by faith we must acquire a righteousness exceeding the righteousness of slavish works that some scribes and Pharisees paraded before the world. Childlike faith is the key that opens the door of the kingdom, but once inside there are progressive steps of righteousness to climb in order to grow to the full stature of the sons of God. (170:3.1, 170:3.2)

Source 170:3.3

2A man will not truly forgive his fellows unless he loves them as himself
3To love your neighbor as yourself is the highest ethics
4Moral conduct, true righteousness, becomes the natural result of such love
But Jesus did not put ethics in the place of religion. He taught religion as a cause and ethics as a result. (170:3.8)
The righteousness of any act must be measured by the motive; the highest forms of good are therefore unconscious. (170:3.9)

Because every act is to be judged by its motive, the highest good is done unconsciously. The religion of the kingdom is personal, but its fruits are familial and social. The deeper our inward fellowship with God, the more naturally it overflows into loving service, so this section offers a practical counsel: never separate faith from its practice. (170:3.9, 170:3.10)

IV. Jesus’ Teaching about the Kingdom — Five Phases and Five Features

Jesus never gave a precise definition of the kingdom. In this sermon he pointed instead to five phases, or epochs, of it. For that reason, the narrator says, whenever Jesus uses the word kingdom we must ask which phase he has in mind. (170:4.1, 170:4.7)

Source 170:4.2
Five phases of the kingdomFive cardinal features of the gospel
The inward experience of the individual believer’s fellowship with the FatherThe pre-eminence of the individual
The enlarging brotherhood of believers — the social aspects of enhanced morals and quickened ethicsThe will as the determining factor in man’s experience
The supermortal brotherhood of invisible spiritual beingsSpiritual fellowship with God the Father
The dawn of a new social order — the next age of manThe supreme satisfactions of the loving service of man
The kingdom in its fullness — the age of light and lifeThe transcendency of the spiritual over the material in human personality
Neither make the fatal mistake, in looking for the age manifestation of the kingdom, of failing to effect its establishment within your own souls. (170:4.14)

The narrator urges us not to be discouraged if the kingdom seems slow in coming. Progressive evolution, material and spiritual alike, is subject to sudden periodical changes, and Jesus’ bestowal was itself just such an unexpected event. Meanwhile Michael and his associates, by gradually changing the wills of men, are slowly but surely changing the whole course of human evolution. (170:4.7, 170:4.14)

Jesus promised a future revelation of the kingdom, and he promised to return in person, but he never said that the two were the same event. The apostles and disciples hastily linked them and lived in hope of an immediate second coming. The narrator keeps a careful reserve: these promises may, or may not, refer to the same event. (170:4.15, 170:4.16)

V. Later Ideas of the Kingdom — Church and Kingdom

The narrator then describes how the kingdom idea changed in later times. Mingled with Greek idealism, the ideas of Philo, and the Persian doctrines of good and evil, the Messiah of the kingdom became the Redeemer of the church. Jewish believers waited for the Messiah to return soon and set up a world-wide kingdom, while gentile believers came to see Jesus as the Redeemer of the children of the church. (170:5.2, 170:5.3, 170:5.5, 170:5.6)

The narrator does not simply disparage this change. Building on the Master’s life and teachings, and adding the Greek and Persian concepts of eternal life and the doctrine of Philo, Paul founded one of the most progressive human societies ever to exist on Urantia. But in the process Christ became the head of the church rather than the elder brother of each believer, and the concept of the kingdom in the individual heart was gravely weakened. (170:5.16, 170:5.17)

The church itself is not blamed. As a social outgrowth of the kingdom, its appearance was natural and even desirable. The trouble was that this social response almost completely displaced the spiritual kingdom that Jesus taught and lived. Membership in a church does not necessarily mean fellowship in the kingdom, for one is spiritual and the other mainly social. (170:5.7, 170:5.10, 170:5.18)

but you should comprehend that this church is only the larval stage of the thwarted spiritual kingdom (170:5.21)

The paper ends in hope. A greater John the Baptist will one day arise to call people back to Jesus’ lofty spiritual concept, and the church, the cocoon in which the kingdom now slumbers, will at last release it like a butterfly. The narrator adds that believers may differ in intellectual interpretation, but a lack of spiritual brotherhood among them is inexcusable. (170:5.19, 170:5.20, 170:5.21)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 170 untangles a long confusion over a single word. For Jesus the kingdom was neither a territory nor an institution; it was the personal experience of knowing God as Father and seeking to do his will. That experience begins within, shows itself outwardly in loving service, and in time transforms societies and ages. (170:4.7, 170:5.11)
