Introduction — Six Months in the Capital of the World

Jesus reached Rome as companion to the Indian merchant Gonod and his son Ganid. On the third day the three of them appeared before the emperor Tiberius. The usually morose emperor was unusually cheerful that day, talked with them at length, and afterward remarked how he envied Jesus’ bearing. While Gonod was busy with business and Ganid kept his regular hours of study, Jesus came to know this city of two million inhabitants, from the forum to the Capitolium and the Palatine hill. (132:0.1, 132:0.2)

If I had that fellow’s kingly bearing and gracious manner, I would be a real emperor, eh? (132:0.1)

The narrator states plainly the chief reason Jesus consented to this journey: his desire to study and mingle with the people of the Roman Empire, gathered from every country of the Eastern Hemisphere. This paper shows what he did in those six months, in this order: conversations with philosophers, personal ministry, counsel to a rich man, help to neighbors, and trips about Rome. (132:0.3)

Prologue — Preparing Thirty-Two Religious Leaders

The most valuable of his experiences in Rome was his contact with the religious leaders of the capital. Before the end of the first week he had sought out and befriended the leaders of the Cynics, the Stoics, and the mystery cults, especially the Mithraic group. Foreseeing that his messengers would soon come to Rome to proclaim the kingdom of heaven, he set about preparing the way for their message to be well received. He chose five Stoics, eleven Cynics, and sixteen mystery-cult leaders—thirty-two in all—and associated with them intimately for almost six months. Thirty of them later became pivotal individuals in establishing Christianity in Rome. (132:0.4, 132:0.5)

Never once did he attack their errors or even mention the flaws in their teachings. (132:0.4)

His method was to select the truth in what each person already taught, and then to embellish and illuminate that truth until it crowded out the associated error in a short time. The work was entirely personal: he never met more than three of them at once, and most often he taught them singly. It was possible because these men and women were not bound by tradition. The passage invites us to reconsider our own habit of beginning with someone’s mistakes when we want to change them. (132:0.4, 132:0.9)

Neither Stephen nor the thirty ever realized that they had once talked with the man whose name became the subject of their teaching. Shortly before his death Paul concluded that the “tentmaker of Antioch” was also the “scribe of Damascus,” and Peter once surmised that this scribe might have been Jesus but quickly dismissed the idea. (132:0.9, 132:0.10)

I. True Values — To Angamon the Stoic

Jesus had an all-night talk with Angamon, the leader of the Stoics. Its gist was that the standard of true values must be sought in the spiritual world and on the levels of eternal reality. The scientist as such is limited to discovering the relatedness of material facts; the moment he asserts materialism or idealism, he is already doing philosophy. (132:1.1, 132:1.2)

Unless the moral insight and the spiritual attainment of mankind are proportionately augmented, the unlimited advancement of a purely materialistic culture may eventually become a menace to civilization. (132:1.3)

Scientists and religionists alike are on trial before the bar of human need. Rather than fight each other, they must justify their survival by serving human progress, and any false science or religion must either purify itself or give way to something truer. The teaching prompts us to ask whether the maturity of our minds keeps pace with the speed of our technology. (132:1.4)

II. Good and Evil — To Mardus the Cynic

Mardus, the leader of the Cynics, talked with Jesus day after day. Jesus told him that good and evil are words symbolizing relative levels of human comprehension of the universe. The lazy may take social usage or religious tradition as their standard of good, but the surviving soul must make a living choice by the standard set by the indwelling spirit. (132:2.1, 132:2.2)

III. Truth and Faith — To Nabon, the Mithraic High Priest

Nabon, high priest of Mithraism, had thought to make a convert of Jesus, but in fact he himself was being prepared to become one of the early converts to the gospel of the kingdom. Jesus taught him that truth cannot be defined with words, only by living. (132:3.1, 132:3.2)

There is never conflict between true knowledge and truth. Conflict arises between knowledge and human beliefs colored by prejudice or distorted by fear. Out of mental laziness and fear of the unknown, man tends to crystallize science and dogmatize truth. (132:3.3, 132:3.4)

But truth can never become man’s possession without the exercise of faith. (132:3.5)

Faith releases the activities of the divine spark within the mind. Plants and animals survive in time by passing on identical particles to the next generation, but the human soul survives death through identity association with the immortal spark that indwells it. The saying that perfect spiritual self-restraint equals complete freedom lets us see self-control not as bondage but as liberation. (132:3.6, 132:3.10)

IV. Personal Ministry — Opening with Questions, Then Listening

Jesus spent much time getting to know people of every race and class. He had a double purpose: to learn how they reacted to the life they were living, and to say or do something to make that life richer. He usually began conversations by asking questions, and to those he taught the most, he said the least. The overburdened and anxious found relief in unburdening their souls to a sympathetic listener. (132:4.1, 132:4.2)

In this way he came into affectionate contact with more than five hundred people, and he always regarded these six months as one of the richest periods of his earth life. The only place in Rome he refused to visit was the public baths, because of the sexual promiscuity that prevailed there. (132:4.3, 132:4.5)

V. Counseling the Rich Man — Where Did This Wealth Come From?

A wealthy Stoic citizen introduced by Angamon asked whether he should keep his wealth or give it away. Jesus stressed more than once that his advice was for this man alone and that he must not become a dictator to other rich men; then he urged him first to analyze honestly the sources of his fortune. (132:5.2, 132:5.14)

At the heart of this counsel lies brotherhood. If we recognize man as our brother and honestly want to do by him as we would have him do by us, the commonplace dictates of justice, honesty, and fairness will guide us through economic problems. The wealthy Roman promised to begin administering all his wealth according to this counsel the very next day. (132:5.20, 132:5.25)

VI. Social Ministry — The Lost Child and the Widow’s Home

The Creator of a universe spent several hours bringing a lost, crying boy back home. Jesus told Ganid that most people are like that child, crying in fear when they are only a short distance from safety. From that day Ganid watched for lost children for the rest of his life. (132:6.1)

all those who know the way of truth and enjoy the assurance of knowing God should esteem it a privilege, not a duty, to offer guidance to their fellows in their efforts to find the satisfactions of living. (132:6.1)

The two helped a widow raising five children alone, and they even found a position for her eldest son. When Gonod complained good-naturedly that Jesus was making his son a philosopher or philanthropist, Jesus answered that perhaps he could be all four; and when Gonod urged him to write a book, Jesus replied that his mission was to live a life. (132:6.2, 132:6.3)

VII. Trips about Rome — The Orphan Prophet and a New Religion

On the way to the northern Italian lakes, Jesus did not engage a certain pagan in conversation, because the man was not hungry for truth. You cannot reveal God to those who do not seek him, he taught; our real business is so to live that the Father may reveal himself in our lives. (132:7.1, 132:7.2)

In the mountains of Switzerland, when Gonod asked about Buddha, Jesus acknowledged him as a great man and even a prophet, yet called him an “orphan prophet” who had lost sight of the Father in heaven. His ship of salvation ran aground just outside the harbor because of faulty charts of navigation. (132:7.3, 132:7.4)

When man goes in partnership with God, great things happen. (132:7.9)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

In Paper 132 Jesus is not a preacher before crowds but a friend who meets people one by one. He did not attack the errors of others but nurtured the truth within them; to philosophers he gave values and truth, to the rich man stewardship, and to a lost child and a widow a helping hand. His work was not to write a book but to live a life. (132:0.4, 132:6.3)
