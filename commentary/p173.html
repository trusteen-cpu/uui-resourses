Introduction — “What Will the Master Do Today?”

Early on Monday morning Jesus and the apostles gathered at Simon’s home in Bethany and set out for Jerusalem. The apostles, not yet recovered from the previous day, were strangely silent. The Master’s sudden change of tactics, together with his instruction that they do no public teaching throughout the Passover week, had left them with a certain feeling of detachment. (173:0.1)

As they went down Mount Olivet, every apostle but Judas had one question uppermost in mind: What will the Master do today? Judas was absorbed by a different question: Should he go on with Jesus or withdraw, and if he withdrew, how should he break away? Walking the same road while holding such different questions, the group casts a tension over the whole paper. (173:0.2)

Arriving at the temple about nine o’clock, Jesus mounted a teaching platform in the large court where he so often taught, and the apostles stood a little way off to watch. The day unfolds in the cleansing of the temple, the dispute over authority, and three parables. (173:0.3)

I. Cleansing the Temple — A House of Prayer Turned Marketplace

The narrator first explains in detail how the temple courts had become a market. Sacrificial animals had to be without “blemish,” and many worshipers had suffered the humiliation of having their animals rejected by the temple inspectors. So the custom of buying animals at the temple took hold, and the larger part of the enormous profits went to the ruling high-priestly families. (173:1.1, 173:1.2)

As Jesus was about to begin his address, three things converged: a heated argument over the alleged overcharging of a Jew from Alexandria, the bellowing of a drove of bullocks being driven across the court, and a simple-minded Galilean being ridiculed and jostled by supercilious Judeans. Together they produced one of those strange and periodic uprisings of indignant emotion in Jesus’ soul. (173:1.6)

Jesus stepped down from the platform, took a whip of cords from the lad driving the cattle, drove the animals out, and opened the gates of every pen. The pilgrims, electrified, began overturning the money-changers’ tables, and in less than five minutes all commerce had been swept from the temple. By the time the Roman guards arrived, all was quiet. The apostles, astonished, never lifted a hand. (173:1.7, 173:1.10)

He was not alone in his indignation. The common people, especially pilgrims from foreign provinces, heartily resented this profiteering desecration of their national house of worship. For the rest of the day guards set by the people stood at every archway and would not let anyone carry even an empty vessel across the courts, and the temple was quiet and peaceful. The chief priests feared him all the more, yet because of the crowds they could not act. (173:1.5, 173:1.8, 173:1.9)

‘My house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations, but you have made it a den of robbers.’ (173:1.7)
Have you never read, ‘Out of the mouths of babes and sucklings has praise been perfected’? (173:1.8)

The narrator spells out the meaning of the episode. It reveals Jesus’ attitude toward the commercializing of religion and his detestation of every form of unfairness and profiteering at the expense of the poor and the unlearned. It also shows that he did not approve of refusing to use force to protect the majority when unjust minorities entrench themselves behind political, financial, or ecclesiastical power. (173:1.11)

This cleansing of the temple discloses the Master’s attitude toward commercializing the practices of religion as well as his detestation of all forms of unfairness and profiteering at the expense of the poor and the unlearned. (173:1.11)

II. Challenging the Master’s Authority — Where Did John’s Baptism Come From?

Both the triumphal entry and the cleansing of the temple had the effect of postponing his arrest. The leaders feared that the multitude might turn on them in fury, and that the Roman guards might be called in to quell a popular uprising. At its noon session the Sanhedrin agreed unanimously that Jesus must be speedily destroyed, but it could not settle on how, and so it sent out five groups to try to entangle him in his teaching. (173:2.1, 173:2.2)

About two o’clock, just as Jesus had begun his discourse on “The Liberty of Sonship,” a group of elders interrupted to ask by what authority he did these things. The narrator grants that the question itself was proper. Only the Sanhedrin could ordain a teacher, and public teaching without such authority was regarded as either ignorant presumption or open rebellion. (173:2.2, 173:2.3)

Ordination had to take place before at least three persons previously ordained, and it conferred the title “rabbi” along with the right to act as a judge, “binding and loosing” the matters brought before him. The prophets had always stirred up trouble precisely because they taught without such authority. The question, then, was more than a quibble: it was the moment when institutional authority and spiritual authority met face to face. (173:2.3)

Jesus answered with a question of his own: Was John’s baptism from heaven or from men? Finding either answer dangerous, they said they did not know, and Jesus in turn declined to tell them by what authority he acted. This was no evasion. Jesus never meant to rest his authority on John; it lay in himself and in his Father. (173:2.4, 173:2.5, 173:2.6)

Jesus’ authority was in himself and in his Father’s eternal supremacy. (173:2.6)

Even the common folk could tell the difference between the moral majesty of the Master and the designing hypocrisy of his enemies. Yet the cleansing of the temple had brought the Sadducees over to the side of the Pharisees in perfecting the plan to destroy Jesus, and the Sadducees now made up a majority of the Sanhedrin. (173:2.8)

III. Parable of the Two Sons — Saying and Doing

Before the silent Pharisees Jesus told the parable of the two sons. One said he would not go to work in the vineyard, but afterward repented and went; the other said, “Yes, my father, I will go,” and did not go. The people answered with one accord that the first son had done his father’s will. (173:3.1, 173:3.2)

you, who make great pretensions of serving the Father in heaven while you refuse to do the works of the Father. (173:3.2)
It was not you, the Pharisees and scribes, who believed John, but rather the publicans and sinners (173:3.2)

The narrator stresses that Jesus did not despise the Pharisees and Sadducees personally. What he sought to discredit was not the men but their systems of teaching and practice. What was taking place was the inevitable clash between a new and living religion of the spirit and the older religion of ceremony, tradition, and authority. (173:3.3)

All this while the twelve stood near the Master without taking any part. Each was reacting in his own way to the events of these closing days, yet each obeyed the instruction to refrain from public teaching. Standing silently at his side was, at times, a form of obedience. (173:3.4)

IV. Parable of the Absent Landlord — The Rejected Stone Becomes the Cornerstone

Jesus told the crowd another parable. A householder let out his vineyard to tenants and went away; when he sent servants to collect the fruits, the tenants beat and killed them, and at last they cast his son out of the vineyard and killed him too. The people answered that the lord would destroy those men and let the vineyard out to honest farmers. (173:4.2, 173:4.3)

Some who perceived that the parable referred to the nation’s treatment of the prophets and its coming rejection of the gospel said in sorrow that they hoped such things would not go on. Turning to the leaders who were approaching, Jesus warned that if they kept rejecting the gospel, the kingdom of God would be given to a people who would bring forth the fruits of the spirit. (173:4.3, 173:4.4)

whoso falls upon it, while he is thereby broken in pieces, shall be saved (173:4.4)

The leaders wanted to seize him but, fearing the crowd, withdrew; that night the Sadducees and Pharisees joined hands in a plan to entrap him the next day. The parable can be read less as a condemnation of a particular group than as a warning to any heart that withholds the fruits of what has been entrusted to it. (173:4.5)

V. Parable of the Marriage Feast — The Garment Freely Provided

After the leaders had withdrawn, Jesus spoke the parable of the wedding feast. The invited guests went their ways, one to the farm, another to the pottery, others to their merchandise, and some even mistreated and killed the messengers. So the king sent his servants into the highways and gathered everyone they found, good and bad, rich and poor, until the wedding chamber was filled. (173:5.2, 173:5.3)

When a believer asked how they could be ready and what sign he would give, Jesus pointed to his own body and said, “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.” Even the apostles did not understand, though after his resurrection they remembered his words. Climbing Olivet that night, the twelve sensed as never before that something tragic was about to happen. (173:5.4, 173:5.6)

The apostles’ suspense reached its height. The morning’s cleansing of the temple had raised their hopes that the Master would at last assert his power, but the afternoon’s events all pointed to the certain rejection of his teaching by the authorities. They felt that something tremendous was about to happen without knowing what to expect, and even the Alpheus twins realized that events were moving swiftly toward their final culmination. (173:5.6)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 173 records the day Jesus acted most decisively. He was indignant at the exploitation of the poor in the name of religion, and even when challenged about his authority he took no unfair advantage of his enemies. Through three parables he taught doing over saying, the duty to render the fruits of what has been entrusted to us, and the willingness to put on the grace freely given. (173:1.11, 173:2.7)
