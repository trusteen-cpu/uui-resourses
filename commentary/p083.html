Introduction — Men and Women Will Not Live Without Each Other

Paper 83 tells how the mating mores examined in the previous paper became an institution. Marriage began as the loose association of the horde and, after many variations, culminated in pair marriage, the union of one man and one woman to establish a home of the highest social order. Marriage has often been in jeopardy and has drawn heavily on property and religion for support, but the force that has safeguarded it all along is something simpler. (83:0.1, 83:0.2)

the simple and innate biologic fact that men and women positively will not live without each other, be they the most primitive savages or the most cultured mortals. (83:0.2)

The author sees the sex urge as the unrecognized agency that civilized the savage. A relationship begun in pursuit of self-gratification inevitably brings self-denial, altruistic duty, and the responsibilities of the home, and this process makes people think and finally leads them to love. Beneath this paper lies the paradox that an impulse rooted in selfishness becomes a school of unselfishness. (83:0.3)

I. Marriage as a Societal Institution

Marriage is the mechanism society designed to regulate the many relationships that grow out of the fact of bisexuality. It regulates individual sex relations and, in its older and original function, also regulates descent, inheritance, succession, and social order. The family and the property mores stabilize marriage, and pride, chivalry, duty, and religious convictions help. (83:1.1, 83:1.3, 83:1.4)

Marriage is an institution of society, not a department of the church. (83:1.4)

The author draws a clear line: religion should mightily influence marriage but should not try to control and regulate it exclusively. Primitive marriage was chiefly industrial, with little romantic love; husband and wife grew fond of each other by living and working together. Marriage became mutual, romantic, and ethical only gradually. (83:1.4, 83:1.5)

II–III. Courtship and Betrothal; Purchase and Dowry

Marriage was originally a group affair; then a family matter; only recently has it become an individual adventure. (83:2.1)

The primitive approach to marriage was force, not attraction, and just as raiding preceded trading, marriage by capture preceded marriage by contract. Mock elopements and mock captures are relics of that age, and the paper traces the custom of carrying the bride over the threshold to it as well. For a long time women had no say, but as civilization advanced they took a larger part in every stage of courtship, and love, romance, and personal choice were contributions of the Andites to the world. (83:2.2, 83:2.3, 83:2.4, 83:2.5)

The ancients did not trust love and promises, so they sought to secure the union with property. The purchase price of a wife was a deposit that the husband forfeited in case of divorce or desertion. As civilization advanced, fathers disliked the appearance of selling their daughters and began giving the couple presents of equal value, and this became the dowry. (83:3.1, 83:3.3)

IV. The Wedding Ceremony — Community Witness and Rites Against Ghosts

The wedding ceremony arose because marriage was not a decision of two people alone but an affair of the community. In an age without records, many witnesses were needed to publicize property and the status of children. What began as a mere public notice of living together grew into a ceremony of eating together, and the red man was the first to develop elaborate wedding celebrations. (83:4.1, 83:4.2, 83:4.3)

Childlessness was greatly feared, and barrenness was attributed to the machinations of spirits. Efforts to ensure fertility therefore tied marriage to magic and religious ritual. People used charms, consulted astrologers about the stars, and chose lucky days such as Thursday or the full moon; for a time the weddings of the wealthy were even accompanied by human sacrifice. The priest’s blessing of the marriage bed is far older than any formal wedding rite. (83:4.4, 83:4.5, 83:4.8)

That so much of a beautiful ceremony arose from fear invites us to reconsider how we treat ritual. The form may remain while its meaning changes. When today’s wedding becomes not a device for fooling ghosts but an occasion for promising responsibility before the community, its original communal meaning comes back to life. (83:4.9)

V. Plural Marriages — The Long Stairway from Horde to Pair

The forms of marriage did not reach monogamy all at once. The first step away from the promiscuity of the horde was that a woman belonged to only one man at a time; then came group marriage, plural wives, and the concubine mores. Because the regulation of sex and marriage ensured the survival of more children, family life advanced slowly but surely. (83:5.1, 83:5.2, 83:5.12)

The polygamous system recognized four kinds of wives: ceremonial or legal wives, wives of affection, concubines, and slave wives. The wife with status was not necessarily the wife who was loved. Taboos during pregnancy and nursing, hard labor that aged women early, and high infant mortality all encouraged plural wives, and an older wife sometimes asked for a younger one herself. (83:5.5, 83:5.11, 83:5.13, 83:5.14)

Human customs evolve, but very slowly. (83:5.15)

The story of the chief who dismissed his harem and was killed by the outraged relatives of his wives is a vivid example of this sentence. Even in the right direction, overturning customs in haste provokes fierce resistance. Reform needs time and wisdom as much as it needs ideals. (83:5.15)

VI. True Monogamy — Pair Marriage

The author does not romanticize monogamy. It is monopoly, a cultural and social product that is not natural to evolving man. The earliest monogamy arose from poverty. Yet it was wholly natural to the Nodites and Adamites, and it has been of great cultural value to all advanced races. (83:6.1, 83:6.2)

Monogamy always has been, now is, and forever will be the idealistic goal of human sex evolution. (83:6.6)

Because this ideal requires self-denial, it often fails for lack of that acme of human virtues, rugged self-control. Monogamy fosters refined sentiment, moral character, and spiritual growth, and the paper adds that a woman cannot be an ideal mother while she must constantly compete for her husband’s affection. Above all, it is unquestionably best for the children. (83:6.1, 83:6.6, 83:6.7)

Notice the paper’s concern for those left outside this ideal. People who do not find a mate are easily left in the chill of a solitary existence, so the more fortunate majority should always treat them with kindness and consideration. Holding the ideal high and embracing those who do not reach it must go together. (83:6.4, 83:6.5)

Marriage, which began in crude coercion, is gradually evolving into a magnificent institution of self-culture, self-control, self-expression, and self-perpetuation. (83:6.8)

VII. The Dissolution of Wedlock — Conditions That Lead to Divorce

Among primitive peoples only about half of all marriages were satisfactory, and the most common reason for divorce was barrenness. Wife purchase and the dowry greatly reduced divorce by attaching property penalties to failure. In modern times, love, a more idealistic but extremely individualistic motive, suddenly replaced the older property motive, and marriage became temporarily unstable. (83:7.2, 83:7.3, 83:7.5)

Marriage has advanced steadily over the centuries, yet dissatisfaction has spread most widely precisely where the new freedom of personal choice operates most strongly. In every age the real test of marriage has been the continuous intimacy that family life makes unavoidable. Two people brought up to expect only self-gratification will find it hard to succeed in a lifelong partnership of compromise, devotion, and child rearing. (83:7.4, 83:7.6)

The great inconsistency of modern society is to exalt love and to idealize marriage while disapproving of the fullest examination of both. (83:7.9)

VIII. The Idealization of Marriage — Humanly Divine

Marriage which culminates in the home is indeed man’s most exalted institution, but it is essentially human (83:8.1)

The author considers it unfortunate that marriage has been called a sacrament or compared to the relation of the Adjuster and man, or of Michael and his brethren. The belief that marriages are consummated by divine action leads to the idea that they can never be dissolved, but the very fact that dissolution occurs shows that God was not the party who made the union. Spiritual progress can occur in marriage, but it occurs just as well along other paths. (83:8.2, 83:8.3, 83:8.4)

None of this lowers the ideal of marriage. On the headquarters of the local systems the Material Sons and Daughters actually portray the ideal of the union of man and woman, and the ideal mortal marriage is humanly sacred. The young person’s idealization of marriage is a picture of the future that should not be crushed, but it should be tempered by learning about reality. Nor should new forms of marriage go to the extreme of a mutual monopoly of personality and individuality. (83:8.5, 83:8.7, 83:8.8)

youthful idealization should be tempered with some degree of premarital disillusionment. (83:8.6)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 83 does not wrap marriage in a veil of mystery; it shows it plainly as a human institution. Doing so actually makes the responsibilities of marriage clearer. Heaven will not guard it for us; the self-control, devotion, and preparation of two people are what guard a marriage. Marriage began in crude coercion, but it can grow into a magnificent institution of self-culture.
