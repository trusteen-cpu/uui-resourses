Introduction — A Training Tour for the Evangelists

The second preaching tour of Galilee began on October 3, A.D. 28, and ended on December 30. Jesus, the twelve, and a newly recruited corps of 117 evangelists visited Gadara, Ptolemais, Megiddo, Scythopolis, and many other cities. The narrator explains that the whole tour was principally an effort to give the new evangelists practical experience. (149:0.1, 149:0.3)

Asked to give the final charge before departure, Jesus declined, saying it was not his province to do what others could acceptably perform, and James Zebedee gave the charge instead. Handing over what can be handed over is the way of a leader who develops people. (149:0.2)

I. The Widespread Fame of Jesus — Unconscious Healing

By now Jesus’ fame as a healer had spread through all of Palestine and Syria. On this tour he did not deliberately perform any so-called miracles, yet more than a hundred people were beneficiaries of his unconscious healing. He told them to “tell no man,” but they went home and spread his fame still further. (149:1.1, 149:1.2)

The Master never explained to his apostles how these healings were effected, other than that on several occasions he merely said, “I perceive that power has gone forth from me.” (149:1.3)

Offering it expressly as an opinion, the narrator explains that such healings came about when three associated influences were present together. (149:1.4)

Source 149:1.5
InfluenceWhat it involves
1. The creature’s faithStrong, living faith that persistently sought healing, desired for its spiritual benefits more than for physical restoration
2. The Creator’s sympathyThe great sympathy and compassion of the mercy-dominated Creator Son, with almost unlimited healing powers
3. The Father’s willIf the Father did not will otherwise, the need and the power became one, and healing occurred

The narrator therefore believes that, in Jesus’ presence, profound human faith literally compelled the creative forces and personalities of the universe to manifest healing. By the record, Jesus often allowed people to heal themselves in his presence by their powerful personal faith. Worth noticing is that the initiative in healing lay partly with the faith of the one receiving it. (149:1.7, 149:1.8)

By contrast, a rich widow of Tyre followed Jesus around offering more and more money, with no interest in the gospel. Treating God’s power as something the highest bidder could buy, she shows clearly the difference between faith and selfish desire. (149:1.9)

II. Attitude of the People — What Was Added to the Gospel

The narrator says that, had Jesus’ teachings been left as he presented them, all nations and religions would soon have embraced them. The well-meant efforts of his early followers to restate the teachings for particular groups only made them less acceptable to everyone else. So Christianity contains more of the Master’s gospel than any other religion, yet also much that Jesus did not teach. (149:2.1, 149:2.2)

Source 149:2.3
Two great mistakesThe narrator’s explanation
1. Tying the gospel directly to Jewish theologyThe doctrine of the atonement—a sacrificed Son satisfying the Father’s stern justice and appeasing divine wrath—confused and alienated many honest souls
2. Organizing the teaching around the person of JesusOveremphasis on his personality obscured his teachings and made them harder for Eastern religionists to accept
Learn to approach the miracle through Jesus, but do not make the mistake of approaching Jesus through the miracle. (149:2.7)

The narrator advises approaching other religions by recognizing the truths held in common rather than stressing differences. He calls Jesus’ attitude toward women, taking them along as teachers of the gospel, the most revolutionary feature of his mission, and the one shameful thing about the religion bearing his name that it lacked the courage to follow this example. (149:2.5, 149:2.8, 149:2.9)

Jesus was free from superstition and prejudice, and he dared to teach that natural catastrophes are not divine judgments. Yet he was no militant revolutionary; he tore down what was only when he could offer at the same time the better thing that ought to be. (149:2.10, 149:2.11)

He was not a militant revolutionist; he was a progressive evolutionist. (149:2.11)

His fame at first rested on healing, but more and more people sought him for spiritual help: victims of moral enslavement, fathers asking about their sons, mothers about their daughters, and he always helped them. He did not exact obedience and was never dictatorial, yet he commanded confidence. Only those who had not met him feared him. (149:2.6, 149:2.12, 149:2.13)

And the better they knew him the more they loved him. (149:2.14)

III. Hostility of the Religious Leaders — Closed Hearts

While the common people received Jesus gladly, the leaders at Jerusalem were becoming well-nigh frantic over young Abraham’s conversion and the desertion of three of the spies. The Pharisees had built a systematic theology, whereas Jesus taught as occasion served, in parables. The narrator adds that each parable was meant to illustrate a single feature, and that many wrong ideas come from turning the parables into allegories. (149:3.1, 149:3.2)

The only uniform thing about men is the indwelling spirit. (149:3.3)

Jesus taught that only through appeal to this spirit can mankind attain unity and brotherhood. But when men shut off the spirit’s appeal, little can be done to change their attitude. From this time on the leaders never ceased plotting, convinced that Jesus must be executed as a religious offender. (149:3.2, 149:3.3)

IV. Progress of the Preaching Tour — Anger and a Balanced Character

When a young evangelist asked about anger, Jesus said that it measures the failure of the spiritual nature to control the intellectual and physical natures. Anger shows a lack of tolerant brotherly love and of self-control; it depletes the health and handicaps the spirit teacher of the soul. He urged them to let their hearts be dominated by love. (149:4.1, 149:4.2)

What impressed his associates was less the teaching than a life that exemplified it. He never wavered amid stress and storm, and even when his enemies used every unfair method, he never resorted to ignoble tactics. The narrator adds that, though most must specialize to earn a living, it is wholly desirable to cultivate a wide familiarity with life as others live it. (149:4.5, 149:4.6)

V. Lesson Regarding Contentment — The Vale of Soul Making

When Simon Zelotes asked why some people are happier than others, Jesus answered that much depends on one’s willingness to be led by the Father’s indwelling spirit. Much human sorrow comes from disappointed ambition, wounded pride, and fear. Having sincerely done our best, we should cheerfully accept our lot and use ingenuity in making the most of it. (149:5.1, 149:5.2, 149:5.3)

Seek not, then, for false peace and transient joy but rather for the assurance of faith and the sureties of divine sonship (149:5.4)

Jesus hardly regarded this world as a “vale of tears.” To him it was the birth sphere of eternal spirits, the “vale of soul making.” The same reality yields more or less contentment depending on how we see it. Rereading a painful day as a time when the soul is growing is the practical use of this teaching. (149:5.5)

VI. The “Fear of the Lord” — From Fear to Love

At Gamala Philip asked how to reconcile the Scripture’s command to “fear the Lord” with Jesus’ teaching to look to the Father without fear. Jesus explained that the phrase had meant different things in successive ages, and that his mission was to lead men up from reverence to love. (149:6.1, 149:6.2, 149:6.3)

Source 149:6.3
StageAttitude toward God
The pastFear → anguish and dread → awe and reverence
The way Jesus leadsReverence → recognition, realization, and appreciation → love
The old commandmentFear God and keep his commandments
The new commandmentLove God and learn to do his will
The goodness of God leads to repentance; the beneficence of God leads to service; the mercy of God leads to salvation; while the love of God leads to intelligent and freehearted worship. (149:6.4)

Jesus said the kingdom has no high and mighty king; it is a divine family. He pointed out that false humility and hypocrisy grow out of wrong ideas of God. Humility before God belongs in the depths of the heart, but attention-craving humility is childish, for there is a divine dignity in those whom the spirit indwells. (149:6.8, 149:6.9, 149:6.10)

You do well to be meek before God and self-controlled before men (149:6.11)

Jesus also explained why he so often used the little child as an example: not for innocence, but to stress sincerity and perfect trust in the heavenly Father’s love and guidance. And he recalled the prophets’ warnings against being near God in the mouth but far from him in the heart, flattering with the lips while the heart is given to double-dealing. (149:6.11, 149:6.12)

VII. Returning to Bethsaida — The Test of Experience

On the rainy evening of December 30 all the apostles and evangelists gathered at Zebedee’s house. Even the older teachers were edified by the young preachers’ accounts of their experiences. Yet of the 117 evangelists, only about seventy-five survived the test of actual experience and were ready for further service. During the two-week recess Jesus conferred with four apostles about extending the kingdom. (149:7.1, 149:7.2, 149:7.3)

The record states plainly that more than a third of those trained did not remain. Learning is tested in real experience, and those who pass the test become the workers of the next stage. That is exactly why the evangelists were sent into the field on this tour. (149:0.3, 149:7.3)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 149 shows what happens when the gospel passes through human hands: unconscious healings and fame, doctrines added along the way, closed hearts, and workers who pass the test. In the midst of it all, Jesus taught and lived a balanced character and a relationship with God that is love without fear. (149:4.5, 149:6.3)
