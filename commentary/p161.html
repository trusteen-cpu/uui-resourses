Introduction — Two Apostles Stay Behind at Magadan

On September 25, A.D. 29, Jesus surprised everyone by announcing that early the next morning he and the twelve would set out for Jerusalem to attend the feast of tabernacles. The evangelists were told to visit the believers in Galilee, and the women’s corps to return for a while to Bethsaida. (161:0.1)

Nathaniel and Thomas, however, had not finished their discussions with Rodan, and with the Master’s permission they stayed on at Magadan for a few more days. During the previous week the two had taken turns presenting the gospel to the Greek philosopher, and Rodan found that what he had learned at Alexandria from a former apostle of John the Baptist fitted well with Jesus’ teachings. This paper records the conversation about the two questions that remained: the personality of God and the divinity of Jesus. (161:0.2)

I. The Personality of God — A Philosopher’s Objection and Nathaniel’s Argument

Rodan readily accepted everything presented to him about the attributes of God, but he insisted that God cannot be a person as man conceives personality. The text adds a nice touch of wit: the apostles found it hard to prove that God is a person, but Rodan found it still harder to prove that he is not. (161:1.1)

Rodan’s reasoning ran like this. Personality consists in full and mutual communication between beings of equality. But the infinite and eternal Creator has no equals, so God may be the source of personality while himself standing beyond it. (161:1.2)

God indeed may be the source of all personality, but as such he is transcendent to personality, even as the Creator is above and beyond the creature. (161:1.2)

The two apostles asked Jesus to come to their rescue, but he declined to enter the discussion. He only told Thomas that there was something more important than the idea itself. (161:1.3)

It matters little what idea of the Father you may entertain as long as you are spiritually acquainted with the ideal of his infinite and eternal nature. (161:1.3)

Thomas argued that God communicates with man and is therefore a person, and Nathaniel appealed to his own experience of God. Rodan granted that he had recently had similar experiences, but he maintained that experience proves only the reality of God, not his personality. By Monday night Thomas gave up; but by Tuesday night Nathaniel had changed Rodan’s mind through five steps of reasoning. (161:1.4, 161:1.5)

It was a philosopher’s rigor that led Rodan to accept the reality of the experience while questioning the personality behind it. To have experienced God is one thing; to explain who God is is another. The distinction is a good example for anyone who treasures religious experience yet does not want to turn it hastily into doctrine. (161:1.4)

Source 161:1.6
StepNathaniel’s argument
1. The TrinityThe Father enjoys equality of communication with the Eternal Son and the Infinite Spirit, who are fully equal to him
2. Jesus’ manifestationThe Son, equal with the Father, revealed personality to his earth children — settling whether God and man can communicate
3. The mediator of communicationJesus kept understanding communication with both God and man — two things related to the same thing are related to each other
4. The highest conceptPersonality is man’s highest concept of human reality, God his highest concept of divine reality — God is an infinite personality
5. Origin and destinyAs Creator and destiny of all personality, God must be a personality

In the first argument, once Rodan considered the doctrine of the Trinity, he had to concede that the Universal Father might be a person. The text adds that later reflection on these discussions gave the twelve apostles an enlarged conception of the Trinity. A debate meant to persuade a philosopher ended by widening the apostles’ own understanding. That teaching becomes learning is something faith communities still discover today. (161:1.6)

The heart of the third argument is that Jesus stands in the middle. He was one with the Father and at the same time in perfect communication with men. Since both God and man understood the symbols of his communication, both must possess the attributes of personality. The argument that Jesus’ personality demonstrates God’s personality while proving the presence of God in man puts into philosophical language the truth that knowing Jesus is the way to know God. (161:1.8)

On the fifth argument, the text tells us that Rodan had been tremendously influenced by Jesus’ teaching, “Be you therefore perfect, even as your Father in heaven is perfect.” If the Creator and destiny of personality were not himself a person, the call for personal beings to become perfect like him would lose its meaning. Because the goal toward which we move is a Person, the path of growth becomes a path of relationship. (161:1.10)

I am now convinced that, while God must be infinitely more than a personality, he cannot be anything less. (161:1.11)

Rodan’s conclusion is a model of honest intellect reconciled with faith. He confessed God as a person, qualifying the word with such values as transcendent, infinite, and universal, and he accepted Jesus as the personal revelation of the Father and the satisfaction of everything left unsatisfied in philosophy. To believe that God is personal is not to pull him down to the human level; it is to be assured that we can have a true relationship with him. (161:1.11)

II. The Divine Nature of Jesus — The Witness of Those Who Lived with Him

The last question was the teaching about Jesus’ divine nature, which had only recently been announced publicly. Nathaniel and Thomas presented their views together, and the text gives them in nine condensed and rearranged points. The strength of this testimony lies not in abstract argument but in years of watching a life at close range. (161:2.1)

Source 161:2.2
PointJesus as the two apostles saw him
1. His own admissionHe admitted his divinity, and some events can be understood only by believing it
2. FriendshipThe most unselfish friend, who reproves yet truly loves — only a divine being could be such a friend
3. A blameless lifeHe makes no mistakes and has nothing to repent of; he prays for us but never asks us to pray for him
4. A unique characterPerfect emotional control and compassion that answers every kind of suffering
5. One who knows heartsHe understands men’s thoughts and longings; he prays like a man but performs like a God
6. Superhuman knowledgeHe asks questions to draw us out, not to gain information
7. AuthorityHe speaks with the authority of a divine teacher; brave yet free from pride
8. Partnership with God“Before Abraham was, I am”; he speaks of being one with the Father with childlike naturalness
9. His prayer lifeHe prays as if talking with God face to face
The better you know him, the more you will love him. (161:2.3)

It is worth noticing that friendship came first among the apostles’ reasons. They said Jesus was the friend even of sinners, that he dared to love his enemies, and that while he did not hesitate to reprove them, he truly loved them. Through all the years they failed to grasp his mission, he remained a faithful friend. That the first evidence of his divinity was not a miracle but a friendship tells us that the surest way God is revealed is through a relationship of love. (161:2.3)

They said too that his compassion was moved alike by physical suffering, mental anguish, and spiritual sorrow, and that he was quick to recognize and generous to acknowledge faith and every other grace in others. He did not crave the support of the multitude, was indifferent to the opinions of men, and was brave yet free from pride. The picture of him speaking the most astounding claims with childlike naturalness shows that true authority appears not in display but in naturalness. (161:2.5, 161:2.8, 161:2.9)

He prays like a man but performs like a God. (161:2.6)

This short sentence sums up the whole of the two apostles’ witness. He seemed to know men’s thoughts and understand their longings; he possessed all human emotions, but magnificently glorified. He loved goodness strongly and hated sin equally; he was kind yet brave, and never faltered in doing his duty. Here the human and the divine are blended in one personality without conflict. (161:2.6)

Our piety springs from repentance, but his piety springs from righteousness. (161:2.4)

The two apostles said that Jesus was either what he professed to be or else the greatest hypocrite and fraud the world had ever known, and they were persuaded of the former. They knew he was human, and they were almost equally sure that he was also divine. (161:2.4, 161:2.10)

We are convinced that he is the Son of Man and the Son of God. (161:2.10)

When their talks were over, the two reached Jerusalem on Friday, and the other apostles learned much from their account of the experience. Rodan returned to Alexandria, where he long taught in the school of Meganta, and he gave up his life in Greece when the persecutions were at their height. One week of earnest discussion changed a philosopher’s whole life. (161:2.11, 161:2.12)

III. Jesus’ Human and Divine Minds — The Narrator’s Confession

In the final section the narrator speaks directly. Jesus’ consciousness of divinity grew gradually until his baptism; after that he seems to have had the power to limit, in various ways, his human consciousness of his divinity. It appears that he could freely choose whether to depend only on the human mind or to use the knowledge of both the human and the divine minds. (161:3.1)

Source 161:3.3
Mode the narrator observedWhat it looked like
Self-limiting divine consciousnessAt times he used only the information resident in the human intellect
Concealing foreknowledge and thought discernmentHe foreknew events and read thoughts but kept his associates from knowing this too fully
The divine mind at workHe conferred with the directors of the celestial hosts
The combined personality at workActs activated by the perfect union of the human and divine minds — witnessed on almost numberless occasions

Jesus held back his powers because he did not wish to go too far beyond the concept of the human held in his apostles’ minds. It was a kind of consideration that shows others only as much as they can bear. The narrator candidly admits that he could not always tell the two methods apart. (161:3.2, 161:3.3)

This section fits with the apostles’ testimony before it. The superhuman knowledge and foreknowledge they saw were the working of the divine mind; his praying like a man was the working of the human mind. Jesus lived as a combined personality that cannot be reduced to either one. That he often chose to act with the human mind alone shows that his life on earth was truly a human life. (161:3.1, 161:3.3)

This is the limit of our knowledge of such phenomena; we really do not actually know the full truth about this mystery. (161:3.3)

When even a superhuman narrator confesses that he does not know, we are taught humility before mystery. To reason faithfully as far as we can, and then leave what lies beyond as mystery, is a virtue needed both in Rodan’s philosophy and in the apostles’ faith.

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 161 is a conversation in which faith and reason meet head on. Rodan objected with logic, Nathaniel answered with logic, and the two apostles bore witness from a life lived together. And Jesus reminded them that spiritual acquaintance with the Father’s nature matters more than any idea about him. (161:1.3)

PracticeThe teaching behind it
Take questions of faith into honest discussionThe debate between Rodan and the apostles (161:1.1)
Seek to know God spiritually rather than win arguments about ideas“It matters little what idea of the Father you may entertain” (161:1.3)
Approach God as a person you can have a relationship with“He cannot be anything less” (161:1.11)
Come to know Jesus through the witness of his lifeThe better you know him, the more you love him (161:2.3)
Long for a piety that springs from righteousness, beyond repentance“His piety springs from righteousness” (161:2.4)
Have the humility to say “I don’t know”The narrator’s confession of mystery (161:3.3)
