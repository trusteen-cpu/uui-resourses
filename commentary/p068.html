Introduction — Civilization Is Learned, Not Inherited

Paper 68 opens a series of papers on the origin of human institutions. Its author, a Melchizedek once stationed on Urantia, announces that he will tell the story of mankind’s long struggle from a state little better than animal existence to a civilization that is real, though imperfect. (68:0.1, 68:6.12)

The starting point is clear. Civilization is a racial acquirement, not a biological inheritance. The achievements of science, philosophy, and religion are not passed on through the blood, so every generation must be taught anew and the social heritage must be consciously preserved. This one paragraph explains why education and tradition matter so much. (68:0.2)

Civilization is a racial acquirement; it is not biologically inherent; (68:0.2)

The co-operative social order was begun by the Dalamatia teachers, and for three hundred thousand years mankind was nurtured in the idea of group activities. The text notes that different groups benefited unequally from those teachings. In keeping with the spirit of the text, this is best read as a historical observation about how much contact each people had with the teaching, not as a judgment of any people’s worth. (68:0.3)

I. Protective Socialization — Co-operation Learned Through Fear

Primitive man was not naturally overflowing with brotherly feeling. People learned by sad experience that “in union there is strength.” Going about alone, without a mark of group membership, was fatal, so association early became the price of survival. The author notes that this lack of natural brotherly attraction still stands in the way of the brotherhood of man today. (68:1.1, 68:1.2)

Civilization has become man’s insurance against violent death, while the premiums are paid by submission to society’s numerous law demands. (68:1.2)

Society was born not of a mere gathering of numbers but of the organization of intelligent co-operators. Man learns to co-operate first through fear, and later because he discovers that it is most beneficial. That is why peoples who organized early survived better, and why civilization has kept advancing despite many blunders. (68:1.4, 68:1.5)

The author points to primitive social conditions that survived into his own day to show that modern cultural society is a fairly recent development. The point is not to belittle any group but to show that man’s natural individualism cannot compete with the power of organization and association. The passage invites us to imagine what the world might be like without the teaching of the Planetary Prince’s staff and the Adamic uplifters. (68:1.6)

The section also warns against the romance of “back to nature.” The author calls it a delusion, a belief in a fictitious “golden age,” and says the only historical basis for that legend is Dalamatia and Eden. Rather than idealize the past, we are urged to look at what is still to be built. (68:1.7)

II. Factors in Social Progression — Four Forces That Drew People Together

The herd instinct alone cannot explain today’s social organization; much of man’s sociability is acquired. The author names four forces that first drew people together and held them there. (68:2.4)

The home was the first place where these forces met. The presence of a helpless baby divided the work of men and women, and from earliest times, where woman was has been regarded as the home. The text does not hide the hard lot of women in primitive times. Yet it stresses that the family was the first successful peace group, where man and woman learned to adjust their antagonisms and taught the pursuits of peace to their children. (68:2.6, 68:2.7, 68:2.8)

Marriage is explained in the same light. In evolution its purpose is the insurance of race survival rather than personal happiness, and self-gratification is incidental. Yet the arts of civilization keep increasing the pleasures of marriage and the satisfactions of family life. An institution that began for survival grows over time into a place of joy. (68:2.9)

Almost everything of lasting value in civilization has its roots in the family. (68:2.8)

The key warning of this section is the distinction between self-maintenance and self-gratification. Society advanced as it lessened risk and pain, but when its goal tilts toward self-gratification it moves toward extinction. The author addresses this warning directly to his own age: the twentieth-century Western civilization groaning under an overload of luxury, in which pleasure-want has superseded hunger-want. (68:2.2, 68:2.5, 68:2.11)

Self-maintenance builds society; unbridled self-gratification unfailingly destroys civilization. (68:2.11)

III. Socializing Influence of Ghost Fear — Superstition That Prepared for Awe

The author names the ghost dream as probably the greatest single factor in the evolution of human society. Animals do not imagine survival after death. Fear of the spirits of the dead was a new terror reaching beyond the basic needs of the individual, and it drove loose bands into more disciplined and better controlled groups. (68:3.2, 68:3.3)

Strikingly, even this superstition is said to have served a purpose. Baseless fear prepared the mind for the later discovery that “the fear of the Lord” is the beginning of wisdom, and it was designed to be replaced by the awe that revelation inspires. Even in its clumsy way, evolution was reaching toward spirituality. (68:3.3)

The baseless fears of evolution are designed to be supplanted by the awe for Deity inspired by revelation. (68:3.3)

But these emotions alone cannot bear the strain of society. Hunger, love, vanity, and fear draw people together, yet they also plunge them into war and bloodshed. The section concludes that the peace tendency of the human race is not a natural endowment; it comes from revealed religion, from accumulated experience, and above all from the teachings of Jesus, the Prince of Peace. (68:3.4, 68:3.5)

IV. Evolution of the Mores — Custom as the Thread of Continuity

All modern social institutions grew out of primitive customs. What habit is to the individual, custom is to the group, and customs become folkways and traditions—the mores. The mores were man’s first social institution, and like language their origin was unconscious and is shrouded in mystery. Ghost fear held the mores firmly in place through the belief that the dead were jealous of the ways by which they had lived. (68:4.1, 68:4.2, 68:4.3)

Primitive man was a slave to usage, compelled to do everything from morning to night in the tribe’s way. Yet the author does not treat custom as simply evil. Its inertia was a biologic safety brake against a civilization advancing too fast, and it was the thread that held civilization together. (68:4.4, 68:4.5, 68:4.6)

A society’s survival depends on the progressive evolution of its mores. New ideas are put forward, competition follows, and time and circumstance select the fitter group. But the author firmly rejects the idea that every change has been for the better. Progress is not a straight line, and discerning retrogression is itself part of progress. (68:4.7)

V. Land Techniques — Four Stages in the March of Civilization

Land is the stage of society; men are the actors. The evolution of the mores always depends on the land-man ratio, and man’s land techniques plus his standards of living equal the sum total of the mores. The author divides the march of civilization, which began along the rivers of the Eastern Hemisphere, into four stages. (68:5.1, 68:5.2)

Gains and losses are recorded at each stage. Herding gave leisure but broke down the earlier co-operation of the sexes and lowered woman’s dignity. Agriculture settled people at home and fostered a more peace-loving temperament. The text also notes that Adam and Eve were gardeners, not shepherds. (68:5.8, 68:5.9, 68:5.11)

The growing of plants exerts an ennobling influence on all races of mankind. (68:5.9)

On the age in which industry and cities supplement agriculture, the author speaks plainly: even the highest social developments must rest on a sound agricultural basis, and an industrial era that forgets this cannot hope to survive. (68:5.13)

VI. Evolution of Culture — The Land-Man Ratio

Man is a creature of the soil, and his basic struggle has always been for land. Society is governed by a law: population varies directly with the land arts and inversely with the standard of living. When land was plentiful, human life was valued; when land was scarce, life was cheapened. A shrinking yield brings the worst traits of human nature to the surface, and a growing yield fosters the better side. (68:6.1, 68:6.3, 68:6.4)

Standards of living have determined the quality rather than the mere quantity of a population. The higher the standard, the smaller the family, and standards that become too complicated or luxurious soon become suicidal. Cities multiply the power of their inhabitants for good or for evil, and the fine arts, science, and spiritual culture have thrived best in large centers supported by a suitable agricultural and industrial population. (68:6.5, 68:6.6, 68:6.7)

The text records frankly the harsh practices, such as infanticide and exposure, by which primitive tribes limited their numbers. Then it adds that very few children were killed once they had been suckled. Even in a dark and brutal age, a mother’s love proved stronger than custom. (68:6.8, 68:6.9)

very few children were ever destroyed after having once been suckled—maternal affection is too strong. (68:6.9)

Finally the author foresees that if war lessens and science controls disease, population may become a great test of the wisdom of world leadership. Each change in the conditions of civilization brings new responsibilities with it. (68:6.11)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 68 shows that civilization was not simply handed to us. People banded together out of fear, lived bound by custom, and wrestled with the land to win a little leisure. Along the way the home became the first school of peace, and even ghost fear became a preparation for true reverence. The order we enjoy is a heritage kept by countless generations, and a task we must teach again to the next.
