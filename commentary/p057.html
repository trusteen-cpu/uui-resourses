Introduction — Why Read a Planet’s Family Tree?

Paper 57 opens Part III of The Urantia Book, “The History of Urantia.” Its author is a Life Carrier, a member of the original Urantia Corps, who draws on the archives of Jerusem to tell the story of our planet’s origin. The narrative begins with the birth of a nebula and ends with seas made ready for the planting of life. (57:0.1, 57:8.27)

The author first explains how he will handle dates. The records hold exact years, but he does not give them; he uses the nearest whole numbers instead, and he reckons backward from the early decades of the twentieth century of the Christian era. The figures in this paper are therefore best read not as a precise timetable but as milestones marking a vast movement. (57:0.1, 57:0.2)

Although it is written in the language of astronomy and geology, the paper leaves a feeling that is deeply religious. The ground beneath our feet is no stray lump of rock that happened to roll into place. It is a sphere shaped, through hundreds of billions of years of preparation and supervision, into a home ready to welcome life.

I. The Andronover Nebula — The Work of the Force Organizers

Urantia came from our sun, and our sun is one of the many offspring of the Andronover nebula. That nebula, in turn, took its origin in the universal force-charge of space in the superuniverse of Orvonton. Yet the first scene of the story features not stars but persons. A force organizer reports that space conditions are favorable, a permit is issued, and the organizer and his staff set out on a long journey. (57:1.1, 57:1.3, 57:1.4)

II–IV. The Four Lives of a Nebula — From Circular Cloud to Sun Dispersion

Every evolving material creation is born of a circular, gaseous nebula, and Andronover followed that path. At first it was an enormous gas cloud shaped somewhat like a flattened spheroid. After reaching its maximum of mass it began to contract and spin faster and faster, until it became a spiral nebula with two great arms. When its rotation reached the critical point, suns began to be thrown off into independent orbits. (57:2.1, 57:2.4, 57:3.3, 57:3.5)

All evolutionary material creations are born of circular and gaseous nebulae (57:2.1)

V. Origin of Monmatia — The Visit of Angona

The solar system was not born in the usual way. The young sun was a variable star whose brightness rose and fell in cycles of three and one-half days, and this pulsation made it highly responsive to outside influences. Just then, 4.5 billion years ago, the Angona system approached; at its center was a dark giant of space, solid, highly charged, and possessing tremendous gravity pull. Planetary systems born this way are rare: fewer than one per cent of those in Orvonton. (57:5.2, 57:5.3, 57:5.4)

Less than one per cent of the planetary systems of Orvonton have had a similar origin. (57:5.3)

When Angona made its closest approach, the sun underwent a partial disruption, and a vast column of solar gases—pointed at both ends and bulging at the center—was drawn away. That column became the ancestor of the twelve planets. Gas thrown off from the opposite side later condensed into meteors and space dust. Angona drew the material away, but it did not take any of it for itself. (57:5.6, 57:5.7, 57:5.8)

Retrograde motion, we are told, is always accidental and appears only in systems containing masses of diverse origin. Even a small irregularity in the sky holds the memory of a long history. (57:5.12, 57:5.14)

VI. The Solar System Stage — The Planet-Forming Era

The planets did not stop growing once the solar system had formed. The condensing nuclei of the ten planets took shape, smaller satellites merged, and by 3 billion years ago the solar system was functioning much as it does today. About this time it was placed on the physical registry of Nebadon and given the name Monmatia. Every planet and satellite is still growing by capturing meteors. (57:6.6, 57:6.8, 57:6.9)

This section explains the power of tides in some detail. Tidal friction slows rotation until one face is always turned toward the larger body, as with Mercury and the moon; and a smaller body that comes within two and one-half times the radius of a larger one is torn apart. Saturn’s rings are the remains of a shattered satellite, and the asteroids are the fragments of an ancient fifth planet that came too close to Jupiter. In the far future, the moon too will be drawn near the earth and meet a similar fate. (57:6.2, 57:6.3, 57:6.4, 57:6.5)

Saturn’s rings are the fragments of a disrupted satellite. (57:6.5)

VII. The Meteoric Era — The Volcanic Age — A Planet of Fire

With no atmosphere to protect it, the young planet was struck directly by meteors, and its heavier elements sank toward the center. By 2 billion years ago Urantia had grown large enough to hold a primitive atmosphere, and definite volcanic action began. Later the whole earth became a veritable fiery inferno. Yet even then a crust of comparatively light granite was quietly forming. (57:7.1, 57:7.2, 57:7.5)

The stage is being set for a planet which can someday support life. (57:7.5)

The primitive atmosphere held water vapor, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen chloride, but little or no free oxygen. As it cooled, rain began to fall, and for thousands of years a vast blanket of steam covered the planet so that the sun never shone on its surface. Oxygen built up in significant quantities only after seaweeds and other plant life appeared. The author also notes that the radioactive materials open to our study come from the surface and are relatively recent acquisitions, so every estimate of the planet’s age by the radium clock is too short. (57:7.3, 57:7.6, 57:7.7, 57:7.9)

The author adds that all this violent activity is a normal part of making an evolutionary world of the Urantia type. Collision and destruction, capture and growth are not signs of disorder; they are the astronomic preliminaries to the life adventures of time. Some 2.5 billion years ago Urantia had only about one tenth of its present mass, but it was growing rapidly by taking in meteors. The passage reminds us that a process that looks like chaos may, within a larger purpose, be a time of preparation. (57:6.10, 57:6.11)

VIII. Crustal Stabilization and the First Continent — Seas Waiting for Life

One billion years ago marks the actual beginning of Urantia history. The planet had reached about its present size and was placed on the physical registries of Nebadon under the name Urantia. As the crust cooled, the first ocean formed: a world-wide covering of fresh, not salty, water, averaging more than a mile deep. Then the floor of the Pacific sank, and the first continent rose from the world ocean. (57:8.1, 57:8.3, 57:8.4)

Administrative preparation moved forward side by side with the physical. The section carefully records how one small sphere was entered, step by step, in the records of the universe. (57:8.6, 57:8.10)

From 800 million years ago, the land rose until almost a third of the earth’s surface was one continental body, and the first climatic differences appeared. When the continent began to break apart 750 million years ago, the ocean pushed into the land as long fingerlike seas, forming shallow waters and sheltered bays; by 650 million years ago those waters were nearing the saltiness essential to life. These inland seas would later write the record of life on pages of stone. (57:8.21, 57:8.24, 57:8.25, 57:8.26)

These inland seas of olden times were truly the cradle of evolution. (57:8.26)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 57 is crowded with numbers, but what they point to is a single quality: patience. More than eight hundred billion years passed between the force organizer’s initiation of the nebula and the inland seas reaching the saltiness life requires. Along the way Michael chose this nebula as the site of his universe, and a small, insignificant sphere was entered in the registries, destined to become the “world of the cross.” Our own lives, too, unfold on a stage that was prepared over a very long time.
