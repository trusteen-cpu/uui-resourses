Introduction — A Young Man’s Record in Ten Parts

Paper 131 is not a story of events but the presentation of a document. During the stay in Alexandria, Ganid spent much of his time, no small sum of his father’s money, and the labor of more than threescore translators gathering what the world’s religions taught about God and his relation to man. He arranged the material in Alexandria and Rome, and the manuscript was preserved in India for hundreds of years after his death. (131:0.1, 131:0.2)

The narrator makes plain that the monotheistic teachings in this record were largely derived, directly or indirectly, from the missionaries of Machiventa Melchizedek. Going out from Salem, they sought to spread the doctrine of one God—the Most High—to the ends of the earth. This paper should therefore be read not as a full assessment of each religion but as an anthology of the traces of that common teaching surviving in many traditions. (131:0.1)

I. Cynicism — The Fullest Preservation of the Melchizedek Teaching

The Cynic passages Ganid chose portray God as the Most High of heaven and earth, the perfected circle of eternity, one God who is compassionate and merciful. He is not far away but omnipresent, the record says, and he lives in the heart of the man who fears his holy name. (131:1.2, 131:1.5)

We search for the Most High and then find him in our hearts. (131:1.5)

The record also distinguishes knowledge from wisdom: man gains knowledge from human experience but derives wisdom from contemplating the Most High. The weary soul finds eternal rest in his arms, and there is a promise of justice—what fruit we do not reap from our plantings in this world we shall receive in the next. (131:1.4, 131:1.9)

It teaches that the one who knows God looks on all men as equal brothers, and that rich and poor are brothers with one Father. It includes the principle that we should not do to others the evil we would not have done to us, and the teaching that prayerful worship shuns evil. (131:1.6, 131:1.7, 131:1.8)

II. Judaism — God the Creator and Shepherd

The section on Judaism is the longest in the paper. The passages chosen by Jesus and Ganid celebrate the one God who made the heavens and the earth, the Lord to be loved with all one’s heart, soul, and might, the One who heals the brokenhearted and dwells with the humble in spirit. In turn come the inescapable divine presence, God as shepherd, and the forgiveness that makes scarlet sins as white as snow. (131:2.2, 131:2.4, 131:2.7, 131:2.10)

Love your neighbor as yourself; bear a grudge against no man. (131:2.11)

The selection includes many sayings on humility and quietness: pride goes before destruction; he who rules his own spirit is mightier than he who takes a city; those who wait upon the Lord shall renew their strength. At its heart stands the promise, “Fear not, for I am with you.” (131:2.6)

The selection closes with a promise to those who love the Lord supremely and their neighbors as themselves: they are called sons of the living God and are invited to dwell forever with him in Paradise. Here Ganid must have seen commandment and promise bound together by love alone. (131:2.12, 131:2.13)

III–IV. Buddhism and Hinduism — The Peace of Faith and the God Within

Ganid was shocked to discover how near Buddhism came to being a great and beautiful religion without a personal and universal Deity. The sayings he chose speak of the fearless peace that faith gives, of gladness springing from a pure heart, and of the truth that the greatest warrior is the one who subdues himself. With them stands a sober warning that every act shall receive its reward. (131:3.1, 131:3.2, 131:3.5, 131:3.6)

Faith is man’s true wealth; it is the endowment of virtue and glory. (131:3.2)

From the Hindu records Ganid gathered passages confessing God as the creator and controller of the universe of universes, the Light of Lights, the lamp of wisdom dwelling within the soul. Prominent are the teaching that to know God releases the soul and cuts the cords of death, and the prayer to be saved from lust, wrath, and avarice. (131:4.2, 131:4.3, 131:4.7)

From this unreal world lead us to the real! From darkness lead us to the light! From death guide us to immortality! (131:4.3)

The Hindu record calls God the Lord of life, the helper of the distressed, the Good Shepherd of the human flocks, and finally confesses him as our father, brother, and friend. It closes that passage with a longing to know this God in our inner being. (131:4.4)

V–VI. Zoroastrianism and Suduanism (Jainism) — The Lord of Lights and Self-Conquest

Zoroaster was in direct contact with the descendants of the early Melchizedek missionaries, and their doctrine of one God became central to the religion he founded in Persia. Ganid’s record confesses one Supreme God, the Lord of Lights, who is farthest from us and yet nearest because he dwells within our souls. It also teaches that when we confess our wrongs and resolve not to repeat them, forgiveness takes away the bonds of sin. (131:5.1, 131:5.3, 131:5.5)

We claim mercy because we aspire to attain perfection; we would be like God. (131:5.5)

The prayers of this tradition go on to ask that we be taught how to live this life in the flesh while preparing for the next life of the spirit. The line Ganid chose declares that the religion is right which leads to union with righteousness. (131:5.4)

The tradition then known as the Suduanists, and known today as Jainism, was the third group in India to preserve the Melchizedek teaching. Its record teaches that self is man’s invincible foe, appearing as his four greatest passions: anger, pride, deceit, and greed. (131:6.1, 131:6.2)

Man’s greatest victory is the conquest of himself. (131:6.2)

VII–IX. Shinto, Taoism, and Confucianism — The Heaven of East Asia

The Shinto record was the one world religion Ganid had never heard of. Its sayings tell of a Lord who regards even the prayer of the poorest man, warn that every hair of pride shuts off saving light, and call the worshiper to come with a clean heart free from falsehood and hypocrisy. (131:7.1, 131:7.2, 131:7.3)

The teachings gathered from the founder of Taoism portray a Supreme One who is pure and tranquil and yet powerful. He seeks no credit for his bestowals; he guides without self-assertion and ministers progression without domination. True goodness, the record says, is like water: it blesses everything and seeks the lowest places. (131:8.2, 131:8.3)

Always remember that God does not reward man for what he does but for what he is (131:8.5)

The Taoist record further teaches that the wise man regards all parts from the point of view of the whole, that those who aspire to greatness must learn to humble themselves, and that injury should be recompensed with kindness. Its conclusion is to help one’s fellows without thought of reward. (131:8.4, 131:8.5)

Confucianism is introduced as the least God-recognizing of the great religions, yet Ganid’s summary speaks of the One God who rules man from on high and of a Heaven that makes no mistakes. It calls benevolence Heaven’s choicest gift to men and teaches that when we find ourselves in the wrong, we should confess our error without hesitation and quickly make amends. (131:9.1, 131:9.2, 131:9.3)

Set side by side, these records show certain teachings recurring across many traditions, though with different wording and emphasis. The table below lists only the common points found within the passages Ganid chose; it does not summarize the full doctrine of any tradition. (131:0.1)

X. “Our Religion” — Ganid’s Confession

After the arduous labor of compiling the world’s religious records, Ganid wrote down separately the belief he had reached through Jesus’ teaching. He called it “our religion.” His confession opens with loving the one Lord with all one’s mind and heart while loving all his children as oneself. (131:10.1, 131:10.2)

Though we cannot see God, we can know him. (131:10.2)

He wrote that we cannot search out God by knowledge, but we can know him in our hearts by personal experience. The greatest change was that he no longer believed only that God is the Father of all his people, but that he is also his own Father. And he came to the conclusion that to rejoice in God’s fatherhood one must also accept the brotherhood of man. (131:10.3, 131:10.5, 131:10.7)

He wrote that when men begin to feel after God, that is evidence that God has already found them. This echoes what Jesus told Ezra at Syracuse in the previous paper. The insight that the very longing for God is a sign that God has come first is a comfort to anyone who feels lost at the beginning of faith. (131:10.4, 130:8.2)

I do not see how I can rejoice in the fatherhood of God while I refuse to accept the brotherhood of man. (131:10.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 131 shows that truth about God was never the monopoly of one people or one scripture. The seed sown by the Melchizedek missionaries grew in its own form in different languages and cultures, and Ganid was surprised to find how much the best authors agreed about the eternal God. At the same time he learned from Jesus the personal experience of knowing as his own Father the God to whom all those teachings pointed. (130:3.6, 131:10.6)
