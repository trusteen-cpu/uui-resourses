Introduction — Choosing Palestine as the Stage

Having closely studied people and nations on the Mediterranean journey, Jesus reached his final decision about the rest of his life. He reconsidered and finally approved the plan by which he had been born of Jewish parents in Palestine, and he resolved to finish his mortal career in the very land where he had entered human experience as a helpless babe. (134:0.1, 134:0.2)

His Urantia career began among the Jews in Palestine, and he chose to terminate his life in Palestine and among the Jews. (134:0.2)

I. The Thirtieth Year (A.D. 24) — Settling the Family and Departing

Returning by way of Babylon and Damascus, Jesus turned over the little house in Capernaum to his brother James. His family believed he had spent the time studying in Alexandria, and he neither confirmed nor denied it. In Nazareth he had his first long talks with Ruth, the youngest, now nearly fifteen. (134:1.1, 134:1.2, 134:1.3)

Simon and Jude had waited for their eldest brother’s blessing and were married at a double wedding. Then Jesus volunteered to take the place of a caravan conductor who had fallen ill, on a trip lasting a year, and arranged for his mother and Ruth to move into the house in Capernaum. Mary lived there for the rest of her life. (134:1.4, 134:1.6)

These were the in-between times, the transition stage of that being who began life as God appearing as man, and who was now making ready to complete his earth career as man appearing as God. (134:1.7)

This year great progress was made in the working harmony between his human mind and the indwelling Thought Adjuster. The Adjuster was reorganizing his thinking and rehearsing his mind for the great events ahead. (134:1.7)

II. The Caravan Trip to the Caspian

The caravan went from Jerusalem by way of Damascus and Lake Urmia, through Assyria, Media, and Parthia, to the southeastern Caspian region. As its executive, Jesus was responsible for the goods and for the safe conduct of the travelers, and he discharged these duties faithfully and wisely. Scores of people along the route were made better by meeting this extraordinary conductor. (134:2.1, 134:2.2, 134:2.4)

This trip carried Jesus nearest to the Orient. He made intimate contact with every surviving race of Urantia except the red, and Europeans and Asiatics alike listened to his words of hope and eternal life. After his return he made Capernaum his home, though he never again lived with his family; he stayed with the Zebedees. (134:2.3, 134:2.5)

III. The Urmia Lectures — A Temple of the Philosophy of Religion

On an island in Lake Urmia stood a lecture amphitheater dedicated to the “spirit of religion,” built by the merchant Cymboyton and his three sons. About seventy-five teachers from more than thirty religions lectured and debated there. Only one doctrine was required for a seat on the faculty: each teacher must represent a religion that recognized God—some sort of supreme Deity. (134:3.1, 134:3.2, 134:3.6, 134:3.7)

Any teacher who showed intolerance or a contentious spirit was dismissed at once. As an independent teacher, Jesus stayed two weeks on his return and gave twenty-four lectures on “The Brotherhood of Men,” with twelve evening sessions of discussion. This was the most systematic and formal of all his teaching on Urantia. (134:3.4, 134:3.5)

Here the narrator discloses an unusual circumstance. The seraphim of the churches and the seraphim of progress disagreed about including these teachings in the revelation, and in the end the Melchizedek chairman appointed three secondary midwayers to restate Jesus’ teaching for twentieth-century conditions. The sections that follow are therefore not Jesus’ exact words but a statement of his teaching as applied to the modern world. (134:3.8)

IV. Sovereignty—Divine and Human

The brotherhood of men rests on the fatherhood of God, and the kingdom of heaven is a spiritual relationship between God and man. Religions can remain at peace so long as they recognize the spirit sovereignty of God the Father. Intolerance and persecution begin when one religion claims exclusive authority over all others. (134:4.1, 134:4.2, 134:4.3)

God alone is spirit sovereign. (134:4.4)

The kingdom of heaven creates unity, not uniformity. Spiritually all men are equal, and in it there are no castes or classes. Yet the concept of equality brings peace only when all mutually recognize some supersovereignty above themselves. The Urmia teachers lived in peace because they acknowledged God spiritually and Cymboyton’s authority socially. (134:4.6, 134:4.7, 134:4.9, 134:4.10)

V. Political Sovereignty — War Is a Symptom

The midwayers state in advance that in their presentation on political sovereignty they have departed more widely from the substance of the Urmia teaching. In Jesus’ day there were only two great world powers, whereas the twentieth century is a peculiarly critical stage in the evolution of political sovereignty. (134:5.1)

War on Urantia will never end so long as nations cling to the illusive notions of unlimited national sovereignty. (134:5.2)

On an inhabited world there are only two levels of relative sovereignty: the spiritual free will of the individual and the collective sovereignty of mankind as a whole. Every grouping in between has value only so far as it enhances the welfare and progress of man and mankind. The rule of the Most Highs is not for any chosen people but for the greatest good of the greatest number for the greatest length of time. (134:5.2, 134:5.4)

As sovereignty passes to larger groups, minor wars decrease but the potential for greater wars increases. The obstacle at every stage is the loyalty already mobilized: the loyalty that makes the tribe possible makes the nation difficult, and the patriotism that makes the nation possible complicates the government of mankind. A league of nations can prevent minor wars, but not world wars among the great powers. (134:5.7, 134:5.8, 134:5.10)

Citizens are not born for the benefit of governments (134:5.16)

The text points to the forty-eight American states, which no longer war among themselves since surrendering their sovereignty and self-determination to the federal government. Just as little Rhode Island has as many senators as great Texas, in a world state the small nations will be as powerful as the great. (134:5.13, 134:5.14)

VI. Law, Liberty, and Sovereignty

If one man is to be absolutely free, another must become an absolute slave. Freedom is therefore relative, possible only under laws that secure the same degree of freedom for all. Religion makes brotherhood spiritually possible, but mankind government is needed to regulate the social, economic, and political problems connected with it. (134:6.1, 134:6.2)

War is not man’s great and terrible disease; war is a symptom, a result. The real disease is the virus of national sovereignty. (134:6.7)

Under world government the nations do not lose sovereignty but create, for the first time, a real one, and individuals actually enjoy more liberty. The end of the Urmia story, however, is bitter. After Cymboyton’s death, the Christian teachers sent by Abner sought to make their religion dominant over the other beliefs, and five years later the school closed. A faith that had lost its tolerance undid the very teaching it had received. (134:6.8, 134:6.10, 134:6.15, 134:6.16)

VII. The Thirty-First Year (A.D. 25) — Last Journeys as a Private Individual

Jesus knew that his world travels were about finished. He lived for over two months in Antioch, working three weeks as a tentmaker, then went down the coast through Caesarea, Joppa, and Gaza to Beersheba. From Beersheba to Dan he made his final tour through the heart of Palestine as a private individual. At Bethlehem he saw his birthplace. (134:7.1, 134:7.3, 134:7.4, 134:7.5)

VIII. The Sojourn on Mount Hermon — Winning the Sovereignty of His Universe

In mid-August of A.D. 25 Jesus left his supplies with a lad named Tiglath and climbed the lonely slopes of Mount Hermon. He asked his Father to send his guardian seraphim back to be with Tiglath, and he went into his last struggle alone, with only his indwelling Thought Adjuster. The beings he wrestled with on the mountain were not phantasms of a starving mind but real beings, his archenemies in the system of Satania. (134:8.1, 134:8.2, 134:8.3)

During this time he finished the mortal task of achieving the circles of mind-understanding and personality-control. In the last week he asked to meet Satan and Caligastia as Joshua ben Joseph. This temptation had nothing to do with food or temple pinnacles; it concerned the sovereignty of a universe. (134:8.4, 134:8.6)

The will of my Father in Paradise be done. (134:8.8)

To every compromise and makeshift, Jesus made only this one reply. On an afternoon in late summer, Michael of Nebadon won the unquestioned sovereignty of his universe, and the Lucifer rebellion and Caligastia secession were virtually settled. The so-called “great temptation” therefore took place before his baptism, not after it. The lesson of the scene is to hold to the single standard of the Father’s will rather than argue at length with temptation. (134:8.7, 134:8.9)

IX. The Time of Waiting — Until the Hour Comes

Coming down from the mountain, Jesus said, “I must return to my Father’s business,” and he was a much changed man. At the day of atonement services in Jerusalem he saw the ritual as a misrepresentation of his Father’s character, but his faithful Monitor reminded him that his hour had not yet come. During the feast of tabernacles he left John behind and withdrew alone to the hills. (134:8.10, 134:9.3, 134:9.5)

It behooves me to keep busy while I wait for my hour to come. (134:9.6)

Back in Capernaum he put on his apron and worked for several months beside James on the interior finishing of large boats. He wasted little time on trifles but was painstaking about the essentials. Even as reports of John the Baptist reached him, he kept working, until in January of A.D. 26, when John had come near Pella, he laid down his tools and declared, “My hour has come.” He filled even the waiting before his great mission with faithful labor. (134:9.7, 134:9.8)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 134 is the bridge from the private Jesus to the public Jesus. He fulfilled his responsibilities as a caravan conductor, taught at Urmia the sole sovereignty of God and the brotherhood of men, and on Mount Hermon, choosing the Father’s will alone, won the sovereignty of his universe. Because that long transformation of mind and spirit was complete, few of those he had once helped ever recognized him when he became a public teacher. (134:9.9)
