Introduction — The Materialist’s Night and One Step of Faith

Paper 102 opens with a dark picture. To the unbelieving materialist, man is an evolutionary accident, his hope of survival a figment of imagination, and even the devoted labors of the best of men are doomed to vanish into the night of oblivion. The author, a Melchizedek of Nebadon, calls this vision the cry of despair of a soul lost in spiritual darkness. (102:0.1, 102:0.2, 102:8.8)

And all this doom of darkness and all this destiny of despair are forever dispelled by one brave stretch of faith on the part of the most humble and unlearned of God’s children on earth. (102:0.2)

When is this saving faith born? When man’s moral consciousness realizes that human values can be translated from the material to the spiritual, from the human to the divine, from time to eternity. The whole paper examines, step by step, what this faith rests on and why it does not waver. (102:0.3)

I. Assurances of Faith — Loving in Order to Know

The Thought Adjuster transforms man’s primitive sense of duty into a higher faith in the realities of revelation. The starting point is a hunger for perfection, and whoever chooses to do the divine will shall know the way of truth. Here the usual order of knowing is reversed. (102:1.1)

It is literally true, “Human things must be known in order to be loved, but divine things must be loved in order to be known.” (102:1.1)

This does not make doubt a sin. Honest doubts and sincere questions merely mean delay on the journey. Childlike trust gains entrance to the kingdom of ascent, but progress depends on the robust faith of the full-grown person. The certainties of science come from the intellect; the certitudes of religion spring from the foundations of the whole personality. (102:1.1, 102:1.4)

The more of science you know, the less sure you can be; the more of religion you have, the more certain you are. (102:1.3)

The paper admits that on Urantia the revelation of truth has often been mixed with transient cosmologies. Teachings about the physical world change year by year, but eternal truth should not be slighted because it is found in company with outdated ideas. God is not proved by miracles or material evidence; we know him because we trust him. (102:1.3, 102:1.5)

II. Religion and Reality — A Religion That Lives and Acts

Religion needs no definition; we recognize it in the lives of others. Believers live as if immortality were already within their grasp, showing a stability and tranquillity that physiology and sociology cannot explain. Genuine assurance is firm, yet it never conveys the slightest impression of self-assertion. (102:2.1, 102:2.2, 102:2.3)

Knowledge is an eternal quest that yields only an increasing probability of approximation, but the spiritually illuminated soul knows, and knows now. Even so, such a person does not lose interest in the progress of science. Philosophy must be founded on material facts, but the soul of its dynamics is spiritual insight. (102:2.4, 102:2.6)

Even scientific discoveries do not become real in experience until the mind unravels and correlates them into meaning. Mind is unity, and so man seeks to identify the energy unity of his science with the spirit unity of his religion. But this unification is possible only when the mind is firmly aware of material things, intellectual meanings, and spiritual values alike; only in that harmony does personality find the satisfaction of cosmic consistency. (102:2.5)

The paper warns against two kinds of people who use religion as a refuge. The indolent hide in stereotyped doctrines and dogmas; the timid use sentimental religion to escape the demands of living. Yet the mission of religion is to prepare man to face the changes of life bravely, even heroically. (102:2.7, 102:2.8)

Intellectual crystallization of religious concepts is the equivalent of spiritual death. (102:2.7)
but always and ever religion does something; it is dynamic! (102:2.9)

III. Knowledge, Wisdom, and Insight — Four Kinds of Consciousness

Educational poverty handicaps higher religious attainment, but overdevelopment of the intellectual factors can also hinder it. Religious speculation is inevitable, yet it always falsifies its object. Religion will therefore always be marked by paradox, because we lack the morontia mota that would connect the material and spiritual levels. This section sets science, philosophy, religion, and revelation side by side. (102:3.1, 102:3.2, 102:3.5)

Religious desire is the hungry search for divine reality; religious experience is the consciousness of having found God. The joy of that discovery overflows not in boasting but in loving service to one’s fellows. Real religion leads to increased social service. (102:3.4)

Evolution tends to make God manlike; revelation tends to make man Godlike. (102:3.14)

IV–V. The Fact of Experience; The Supremacy of Purposive Potential

Because the Adjuster is present in our minds, knowing the mind of God is no more mysterious than knowing any other mind. Experience is the interplay of a questioning self with an external reality, and faith translates our natural awareness of other minds into religion. If God were not a personality, he could not become a living part of such experience. (102:4.1, 102:4.2, 102:4.3)

The error in religious experience is directly proportional to the materialism mixed into it. The paper also observes that modern religions have overemphasized prayer and neglected the more essential communion of worship. (102:4.4, 102:4.5)

Prayer may enrich the life, but worship illuminates destiny. (102:4.5)

Revealed religion is the unifying element of human existence. Revelation unifies history and coordinates the sciences from geology and astronomy to sociology and psychology. Spiritual experience is the real soul of man’s cosmos. (102:4.6)

In the time universes the potential is always supreme over the actual. The transformation of primitive man’s craven fear into living faith is evidence of it. The same principle appears in the relation between morality and religion. (102:5.1, 102:5.2)

VI. The Certainty of Religious Faith — From Probability to Certainty

The advance of science destroys false gods and may cloud spiritual vision for a time, but in the end it clears away the superstition that hid the living God. To isolate one part of life and call it religion distorts both life and religion; this is why the God of worship claims all allegiance or none. The God in whom the mature religionist has faith is not a hypothesis or an idealization but a God of love. (102:6.1, 102:6.3)

Faith transforms the God of philosophic probability into the saving God of certainty in personal experience. When a doubter objects that this certainty is not supported by logic, the believer need only ask in return: how do you know that I do not know? (102:6.4, 102:6.5)

The God-knowing soul dares to say, “I know,” (102:6.5)
Belief may not be able to resist doubt and withstand fear, but faith is always triumphant over doubting (102:6.7)

Reason can always question faith, but faith can always supplement reason. God is the first truth and the last fact; as truth he can be known, but to explain him one must explore the universe of universes. Only living faith can bridge the gulf between the experience of the truth of God and our ignorance of the fact of God. (102:6.6)

The evidence of this certainty is not argument but the social fruits of the spirit that believers bear. Organic evolution is a fact; purposive evolution is the truth that makes that fact consistent. Materialism, which keeps referring the higher back to the lower, has gone bankrupt, and it cheapens human life. (102:6.7, 102:6.9, 102:6.10)

VII. The Certitude of the Divine — No Effects without Causes

The universe is everywhere changing, and a changing universe is a dependent one. The universe and God are not identical: one is effect, and the other is the one self-caused fact. The fact of God is changeless, but the truth of God—his relation to the universe—is ever adapting to the evolving universe. (102:7.1, 102:7.2)

Those who would invent a religion without God are like those who would gather fruit without trees, have children without parents. (102:7.3)

A person can deny God and still be morally good, loyal, and honest. But the humanistic branches grafted onto such a life bear only social fruits, not spiritual ones, and have no survival value. It takes no great intellect to pick flaws and raise objections; it takes brilliance to answer them, and faith certainty is the greatest technique for dealing with such superficial contentions. (102:7.4, 102:7.6)

If science or philosophy becomes dogmatic in challenging faith, the God-knowing person may answer with the more farseeing dogmatism of the certainty of experience. The charge of being “unproved” applies equally to the facts of science and the beliefs of philosophy, for they too are experiences in someone’s consciousness. In the end only an absolute can consistently be dogmatic. (102:7.7, 102:7.8, 102:7.9)

VIII. The Evidences of Religion — The Experience of Entrusting

The highest evidence of religion lies in experience itself: that man, naturally fearful and suspicious, is willing to entrust his deepest interests to the power and person he calls God. A religion’s standing is best judged by its moral judgments and ethical standards rather than by its civilization, and many of the greatest religious teachers were virtually unlettered. (102:8.1, 102:8.2)

Personal religion runs ahead of moral evolution, but institutional religion has always lagged behind. Nor is religion ever enhanced by appeals to the miraculous. The highest religion is one person’s experience of seeking God and finding him—the earth life of Jesus of Nazareth. (102:8.6, 102:8.7)

The prophets have usually led the people in religious development; the theologians have usually held them back. (102:8.6)
The quest for miracles is a harking back to the primitive religions of magic. (102:8.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 102 presents faith not as guesswork filling a gap in knowledge but as the whole personality’s encounter with reality. Its assurance shows itself not in winning arguments but in the fruits of love and service. Because God is the most loving of all friends, we have the right to be most certain of him. (102:7.10)
