Introduction — A God Made Less Human but Not Abstract

Paper 97 follows the concept of Yahweh after Moses, the subject of the preceding paper, as it grew in the hands of the Hebrew prophets. Its author, a Melchizedek of Nebadon, credits the spiritual leaders of the Hebrews with something no one before them had achieved. They stripped their God concept of its human form without turning it into an abstraction that only philosophers could grasp. (97:0.1, 97:10.9)

Even common people were able to regard the matured concept of Yahweh as a Father, if not of the individual, at least of the race. (97:0.1)

The thread running through the whole paper is the concept of the personality of God. Clearly taught at Salem in the days of Melchizedek, it had grown vague and hazy by the time of the flight from Egypt. From Moses to Malachi it then grew almost without a break, until it was heightened and glorified at last in Jesus’ teaching about the Father in heaven. (97:0.2)

I. Samuel—First of the Hebrew Prophets — The Changeless God

Pressure from the surrounding peoples drove the Hebrew tribes to unite under a centralized government, and this gave Samuel, who came from a long line of Salem teachers, his field of work. He was a man of action more than of preaching, rough enough to overthrow a score of Baal sites in a single day. His success was only partial: he won back to the higher concept of Yahweh only the more intelligent half of the Hebrews. (97:1.1, 97:1.2, 97:1.3)

Samuel’s great contribution lay elsewhere. People of his time pictured Yahweh as a fitful God of jealous whims, forever regretting what he had done. Samuel declared instead that Yahweh was changeless, the source of all truth, stability, and constancy. He also proclaimed that the Lord raises the poor out of the dust, a word that shocked those who measured divine favor by material prosperity and gave new hope to thousands of the despairing poor. (97:1.4, 97:1.6)

The Strength of Israel will not lie nor repent, for he is not a man, that he should repent. (97:1.4)

His limits were real as well. Samuel did not progress very far beyond the concept of a tribal god, and his idea of mercy at first extended only to those who were themselves merciful. His successors could not keep his pace, and the keynote of the era was divine power, preached in support of the king upon the Hebrew throne. (97:1.7, 97:1.8, 97:1.10)

II–III. Elijah and Elisha; Yahweh and Baal — A Struggle over the Land

After the nation split into two kingdoms, Elijah restored to the northern kingdom a concept of God comparable with that of Samuel’s day, and Elisha, with the help of the little-known Micaiah, kept that light alive. The author sums up the period, however, not as a time of progress in the concept of Deity but as one of recovery, regaining ground that had been lost. (97:2.1, 97:2.2, 97:2.3)

Especially striking is the paper’s reading of the long contest between Yahweh and Baal. At first, it explains, this was less a difference in religious belief than a socioeconomic clash over the ownership of land. (97:3.1)

When Ahab had the Naboths murdered in order to seize their land, Elijah made a moral issue out of the olden land mores. From then on the land question became a religious one, Yahweh against Baal, and the prophet who began as an agrarian reformer ended by exalting Deity. (97:3.5, 97:3.6)

Baals were many, Yahweh was one—monotheism won over polytheism. (97:3.6)

IV. Amos and Hosea — The God of Justice, the God of Love

Amos was no mere restorer; he was a discoverer of new concepts of Deity. He proclaimed that Yahweh would no more tolerate crime and sin among his own people than among any other, and so, for the first time since Melchizedek, denounced the double standard of national justice and morality. This struck directly at the idea of the “chosen people” as a privilege. He warned that ritual must not take the place of righteousness, and he was stoned to death for his courage. (97:4.2, 97:4.4)

Hosea followed Amos’s God of justice and revived the Mosaic concept of a God of love. He preached forgiveness through repentance, not by sacrifice, and his message was always one of hope. The author contrasts the two: Amos quickened the national conscience to justice, while Hosea struck the opening notes of the divine compassion that Isaiah would later sing. (97:4.5, 97:4.6, 97:4.7)

I will heal their backsliding; I will love them freely, for my anger is turned away. (97:4.6)

V–VI. The First Isaiah; Jeremiah the Fearless — Beyond Ritual, Beyond Nation

The first Isaiah preached the eternal nature of God, his infinite wisdom, and his unchanging reliability, and he carried the promise that sins as scarlet would become white as snow. Micah and Obadiah, who followed him, fearlessly attacked the priest-ridden sacrificial system. Micah’s message still reads today as a short summary of what religion is about. (97:5.2, 97:5.4)

what does the Lord require of you but to do justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God? (97:5.6)

Jeremiah took the next step in the internationalization of Yahweh. He declared that Yahweh did not take the side of the Hebrews in their wars but was God of all the earth and of all nations. For counseling surrender during the siege of Jerusalem he was cast into a miry pit, yet through him the concept of Yahweh rose to a level of planetary and even cosmic dignity. (97:6.1, 97:6.2, 97:6.4)

Yes, I have loved you with an everlasting love; therefore with loving-kindness have I drawn you. (97:6.3)

VII. The Second Isaiah — The Highest God Concept before Jesus

In the Babylonian captivity the priests reshaped the national history to restore the nation and glorify its traditions. The paper fairly adds that they did not claim inspiration or profess to be writing a sacred book; they were preparing a textbook to bolster the courage of their fellow captives. Into this setting came a young prophet, the second Isaiah, whose writings survive in chapters forty to fifty-five of the book of Isaiah. (97:7.1, 97:7.3, 97:7.4)

The author judges that no teacher from Machiventa to the time of Jesus reached a concept of God as high as the second Isaiah’s. He proclaimed the Creator of the universe as the affectionate Father of all mankind, and in doing so effectively eclipsed the nationalistic Yahweh. (97:7.5, 97:7.13)

His God was no small, man-made deity. God’s ways and thoughts are as far above ours as the heavens are above the earth, yet this same God calls each person by name and engraves his children on the palms of his hands. Preaching by the river in Babylon to listeners of many nations, the second Isaiah worked to correct racially egoistic ideas of the Messiah’s mission, though the priests’ nationalism kept him from full success. (97:7.5, 97:7.7, 97:7.14)

Can a woman forget her suckling child that she should not have compassion on her son? Yes, she may forget, yet will I not forget my children, (97:7.7)
I dwell in the high and holy place, also with him who is of a contrite and humble spirit. (97:7.12)

VIII–IX. Sacred and Profane History; Hebrew History — A Rewritten Record

The author points out that the custom of treating one people’s record as sacred history and the rest of the world’s as profane history has caused much confusion. According to the paper, the priests of the Babylonian exile, after preparing their new record, destroyed the existing accounts of the doings of the kings. Five hundred years of foreign rule, and the conflicting promises of deliverance offered by the prophets, then threw the honest Jew into deep perplexity. (97:8.1, 97:8.3)

At the root lay an oversimplified view of Providence: righteousness is rewarded and sin is punished. The paper reads the drama of Job as a protest against this philosophy and the pessimism of Ecclesiastes as a worldly wise reaction to it. The disappointment born of hopes dashed again and again finally left the leaders unable to recognize the One who came as the Son of Man. (97:8.2, 97:8.4)

The fact that the “Most Highs rule in the kingdoms of men” does not convert secular history into so-called sacred history. (97:8.5)

Section nine rereads the main points of Hebrew history in this light, and some of its examples are listed below. The author’s conclusion is that this history is not a chronicle of miracles but ordinary profane history. That does not belittle the people’s spiritual contribution: Judaism was the soil out of which Christianity grew. (97:9.29)

The captivity shocked the remnant into monotheism. In Babylon the Jews concluded that they must convert the gentiles, and from this came a new concept of destiny: that they were to become the chosen servants of Yahweh. The doctrine of immortality also took form there, and religion began to take shape as a system of thought more and more separate from politics and economics. (97:9.26, 97:9.27, 97:9.28)

X. The Hebrew Religion — What Kept It Alive

Their leaders taught that to be chosen meant not a monopoly of divine favor but the special service of carrying the truth of the one God to every nation. Yet after the return from exile the people chose the rules and rituals of the priesthood and rejected the magnificent concept of the second Isaiah. Even so, an unbroken line of faithful teachers passed the monotheistic torch from Moses to John the Baptist. (97:10.1, 97:10.2, 97:10.4)

The remark about institutions is especially worth pondering today. Religion finds it hard to survive as the private practice of isolated individuals. Rather than tearing down all ritual on seeing the evils of institutional religion, it is better to reform it, and in this the author considers Ezekiel wiser than his contemporaries. (97:10.7)

In place of destroying all ritual, they would do better to reform it. (97:10.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 97 calls the change from the barbaric idea of the jealous spirit of the Sinai volcano to the concept of the loving and merciful Father of all mankind the greatest feat in the evolution of religion ever achieved on Urantia. It came not through a single revelation but through generations of teachers each taking one step further. Since a person’s own understanding of God grows in much the same way, this paper is both the story of a people and a map of our own growth. (97:10.8)
