Introduction — Inclusion, Subordination, Exclusion

Paper 96 describes how the concept of God grew among the Hebrew people, into whom the many streams of the Levant flowed. The author first lays out, in three stages, the general path by which man conceives of Deity. Not only the Hebrews but also the Hindus and Mesopotamians followed a similar road, and ideas of monotheism matured all over the world not long after Melchizedek appeared at Salem. (96:0.1)

The Salem religion survived as a tradition among the Kenites and other Canaanite tribes, and when the Hebrews adopted it, it was enriched by Egyptian morals, Babylonian theology, and Iranian ideas of good and evil. This had a clear purpose: Michael could hardly come to Urantia until there existed a people believing in the Universal Father among whom he could appear. (96:0.2, 96:0.3)

Factually the Hebrew religion is predicated upon the covenant between Abraham and Machiventa Melchizedek (96:0.3)

I. Deity Concepts among the Semites — Many Names, One Stream

The early Semites believed that everything was indwelt by a spirit, and Melchizedek’s teaching never fully destroyed this belief. The Hebrews’ progress from polytheism through henotheism to monotheism was not unbroken; it suffered many retrogressions. To avoid confusion, the author first defines the various titles of Deity. (96:1.1, 96:1.2)

Until about 2000 B.C. Mount Sinai was an intermittently active volcano, and its fire, smoke, and thunder made the Bedouins greatly fear Yahweh. For a long time the Hebrews believed other gods existed but held them subordinate to Yahweh. Yet this concept went through a remarkable development. (96:1.11, 96:1.14)

The idea of Yahweh has undergone the most extensive development of all the mortal theories of God. (96:1.15)

As the Buddha concept led at last to the Universal Absolute, the Yahweh concept led finally to the idea of the Universal Father. Strikingly, while the Jews changed their view from the tribal god of Mount Horeb to the loving and merciful Creator Father, they never changed his name; they went on calling him Yahweh. The passage shows that understanding can keep growing under the same name. (96:1.15)

II. The Semitic Peoples — The Making of the Clans of Israel

Racially the Semites were among the most blended of peoples, carrying hereditary factors from almost all of the nine world races. The Arabian Semites repeatedly fought their way into the northern lands and were repeatedly driven out, and during a severe famine they entered Egypt as contract laborers, only to undergo the bitter experience of enslavement. (96:2.1, 96:2.2)

The text makes clear that Abraham was not the racial father of all the Hebrews. His offspring coming out of Egypt formed the nucleus, but most of those who joined the clans of Israel had never been in Egypt; they were fellow nomads who chose to follow Moses. During the captivity the Melchizedek covenant was largely forgotten, and the religion of the common people was the old Yahweh ritual of magic and sacrifice. (96:2.3, 96:2.4, 96:2.5)

III. The Matchless Moses — Organizer of a Forlorn People

Moses’ mother was of the royal family of Egypt, and his father was a Semitic liaison officer between the government and the Bedouin captives. His ancestry was so blended that he cannot be classified in any one race, and that very versatility and adaptability enabled him to lead a diverse horde. He chose to leave the culture of the Nile kingdom and cast his lot with his father’s people. (96:3.1, 96:3.2)

No leader ever undertook to reform and uplift a more forlorn, downcast, dejected, and ignorant group of human beings. (96:3.3)

Moses first sought freedom through diplomacy. He made a compact with the king for a peaceable departure, but when the king repudiated it, Moses was not discouraged; he bided his time. When the Egyptian army was tied up resisting Libyan and Greek attacks at once, he led his people out in a carefully planned night flight. Patience in waiting for the right moment after a setback was one side of his leadership. (96:3.4, 96:3.5)

IV. The Proclamation of Yahweh — A New Concept in an Old Name

Moses had heard Melchizedek’s teachings from his parents, was raised to believe in El Shaddai, and through his Kenite father-in-law came to believe in El Elyon. From all these he formed an enlarged concept of Deity, and judging that the slaves would never grasp the idea of El Elyon, he wisely decided to proclaim it as an expanded concept of their old tribal god, Yahweh. (96:4.2, 96:4.3)

This left Moses always in an awkward predicament, trying to present a new and higher idea under an ancient name long symbolized by the golden calf. During the long stay before Sinai the ten commandments were given and the ceremonial worship refined, and the violent eruption of Horeb in the third week impressed upon the people a God who was a devouring fire. (96:4.3, 96:4.4, 96:4.5)

Moses made a heroic effort to uplift Yahweh to the dignity of a supreme Deity when he presented him as the “God of truth and without iniquity, just and right in all his ways.” (96:4.7)

Under Moses the tribal nature god Yahweh became the Lord God of Israel, and later, through the Babylonian captivity, the concept was freed to take on the monotheistic role of the God of all nations. The author finds the most unique feature of Hebrew religious history in this continuous evolution from the primitive god of Mount Horeb to the loving and merciful Creator Father proclaimed by the Isaiahs. (96:4.8, 96:4.9)

V. The Teachings of Moses — A Lofty Vision and the People’s Level

He was the most important individual world teacher and leader between the times of Machiventa and Jesus. (96:5.1)

The Hebrews had no written language at the time of the exodus, so the record of Moses’ deeds was drawn from traditions more than a thousand years after his death. Much of his advance over neighboring religions was due to the Kenite traditions of Melchizedek’s time. Moses and his father-in-law Jethro gathered up those remnants, joined them with Egyptian learning, and organized the Hebrew ceremonial system of worship. (96:5.2, 96:5.3)

The author says it was truly pitiful to watch this great mind trying to adapt his sublime concept of El Elyon to the understanding of the ignorant. Moses feared to proclaim the mercy of Yahweh and preferred to awe his people with the fear of God’s justice. As a result, when he passed on, the tribes quickly reverted to the worship of the golden calf. Even so, he had welded thousands of wanderers into a self-sustaining nation and handed them over to Joshua. (96:5.5, 96:5.6, 96:5.8, 96:5.9)

VI. The God Concept after Moses’ Death — An Opportunity Nearly Lost

After Moses died, his lofty concept rapidly deteriorated. The leaders kept the tradition, but the people slipped back into the old desert ideas, and the backward drift continued under the Judges. The change from nomadic herders to settled farmers demanded a change in their concept of God, and in that transition the Hebrews nearly lost monotheism altogether. (96:6.1, 96:6.2)

This was not merely one people’s loss. They nearly lost the opportunity to be the vital link that would preserve the Melchizedek teaching until the incarnation of a bestowal Son. Joshua preached a stern gospel to stem the tide, and even in this dark age solitary teachers arose to proclaim again the Mosaic concept of God. (96:6.2, 96:6.3, 96:6.4)

“Can you by searching find out God? Can you find out the Almighty to perfection? (96:6.4)

VII. Psalms and the Book of Job — A Light Kept at Ur

Once settled in Palestine, the Hebrews were contaminated by less advanced Canaanite practices, and their idea of God fell far below the concepts maintained by surviving Salem groups in Egypt and Mesopotamia. Those higher concepts are recorded in some of the Psalms and in the Book of Job. (96:7.1)

Source 96:7.2

The Book of JobMore than a score of Mesopotamian teachers over almost three hundred years — the idea of a real God preserved near Ur in the dark days
The worshipful spirit of this collection of hymns transcends that of all other sacred books of the world. (96:7.4)

In Palestine the wisdom and all-pervasiveness of God were often grasped, but seldom his love and mercy. Only at Ur did a voice arise—Elihu, prophet of Ur and priest of the Salem believers—to proclaim a God gracious to the repentant, salvation by faith. Not since Melchizedek had the Levant heard so cheering a message. (96:7.6, 96:7.7)

“He shall pray to God and shall find favor with him and shall see his face with joy, for God will give to man divine righteousness.” (96:7.7)

Thus the remnants of the Salem missionaries in Mesopotamia kept the light of truth burning while the Hebrews were disorganized, until the long line of Israel’s teachers arose, building concept upon concept until they reached the ideal of the Universal and Creator Father of all. (96:7.8)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 96 shows that the concept of God was not completed all at once but grew little by little through a people’s experience and the labors of its leaders. Moses had a lofty vision yet had to meet the people at their level, and he left behind both the cost and the value of that compromise. The central message of the paper is that, even under the same name, our understanding of God can grow from fear into love.
