Introduction — Why Galilee Rather Than Jerusalem

Jesus and the apostles returned to Capernaum on March 17 and spent two weeks at the Bethsaida headquarters. The apostles taught by the seaside, while Jesus spent much time alone in the hills and made two secret trips to Tiberias with James and John. There were a good many believers among the household of Herod. (147:0.1, 147:0.2)

These believers explained to Herod that the kingdom was spiritual in nature and not a political venture, and Herod raised no objection to Jesus’ work. The narrator makes clear that the greatest danger to Jesus lay not in Herod but in the religious leaders at Jerusalem, which is why most of the public preaching took place in Galilee. This paper records the exceptional visit to Jerusalem, the “interlude.” (147:0.2)

I. The Centurion’s Servant — The Faith of a Gentile

On the day before the Passover journey, Mangus, a Roman centurion stationed at Capernaum, asked the rulers of the synagogue to request healing for his sick servant. The elders pleaded with Jesus, saying the centurion loved their nation and had even built their synagogue. As Jesus went with them, the centurion sent friends to say that he was not worthy to have the Master under his roof; Jesus need only speak the word. (147:1.1, 147:1.2)

I marvel at the belief of the gentile. Verily, verily, I say to you, I have not found so great faith, no, not in Israel. (147:1.3)

From that hour the servant began to mend and was eventually restored to health. Yet the narrator honestly adds that they never knew just what happened; whether invisible beings ministered healing was not revealed even to those who accompanied Jesus. The record stops at the fact of the servant’s complete recovery. This restraint, saying plainly what is not known, adds to the record’s trustworthiness. (147:1.3, 147:1.4)

II. The Journey to Jerusalem — A Quiet Passover

Setting out on March 30, the party went by way of the Jordan valley and reached Bethany on April 2. Passing through Jericho, Judas deposited some of their common funds in the bank of a family friend, and that deposit was left undisturbed until their last journey to Jerusalem. Once at Bethany, they were so thronged by people seeking healing and comfort that they pitched tents at Gethsemane. (147:2.1, 147:2.2)

The twenty-four were not all together again until shortly before the seventy were commissioned. That the two groups nonetheless co-operated shows that workers with different methods can labor under one purpose. (147:2.4)

III. At the Pool of Bethesda — Words of Comfort Instead of a Miracle

On a Sabbath afternoon John, the youngest apostle, led Jesus to the pool of Bethesda. Its reddish-tinged hot spring bubbled up at intervals because of gas in the caverns beneath, and people believed that the first to enter the water afterward would be healed. John hoped that the sight of so many sufferers would move the Master to work a miracle, and that all Jerusalem would then believe the gospel. (147:3.1, 147:3.2)

John, why would you tempt me to turn aside from the way I have chosen? (147:3.2)

Instead of a miracle, Jesus gathered the sick and spoke words of good cheer. Suffering has many causes: years of wrong living, the accidents of time, the mistakes of forebears, the imperfect conditions of temporal existence. But, he said, it is even greater to be cleansed of spiritual disease and healed of moral infirmity than to be freed from bodily affliction, and as God’s children they would all find not only justice but an abundance of mercy. (147:3.3)

You are all God’s children; you are the sons of the heavenly Father. The bonds of time may seem to afflict you, but the God of eternity loves you. (147:3.3)

Many who heard believed the gospel, and some were so spiritually revived that they went about proclaiming they had been cured of their physical ailments as well. Jesus left before the chief priests could take offense, and John never told the other apostles about the visit. The miracle he had hoped for did not come; what he saw instead was the power of words to raise people up. (147:3.4, 147:3.6)

One man had waited for years for someone to help him and had never once entertained the idea of helping himself. Gladdened by Jesus’ words, he took up his bed and went home. The narrator explains that taking up his bed and walking was the one thing he had to do in order to recover. The scene shows that what a person trapped in helplessness needs is sometimes not an outward miracle but an inward rising. (147:3.5)

IV. The Rule of Living — Six Levels of the Golden Rule

That evening Nathaniel asked what happens when a man with evil desires twists the rule of doing to others as we would have them do to us. Jesus answered that he had spoken to men of high ideals, and, disappointed that they so often failed to give his plain teachings a spiritual interpretation, he taught them the levels at which the rule may be understood. (147:4.1, 147:4.2, 147:4.3)

Source 147:4.4
LevelHow the rule is interpreted
1. The fleshA purely selfish and lustful interpretation
2. The feelingsSympathy and pity enhance the interpretation
3. MindThe reason of mind and the intelligence of experience; the highest idealism of profound self-respect
4. Brotherly loveUnselfish devotion to the welfare of one’s fellows, growing out of the consciousness of the fatherhood of God
5. The moral levelViewing the problem as a wise and impartial third person would
6. The spiritual levelThe divine command to treat all men as we conceive God would treat them
I would, therefore, that you should do to all men that which you know I would do to them in like circumstances. (147:4.9)

The narrator notes that nothing Jesus had said up to that time had ever more astonished the apostles. The same sentence becomes an entirely different rule depending on the level at which it is read. Readers can ask themselves at which level they are actually practicing the golden rule. (147:4.10)

V. Visiting Simon the Pharisee — The Love of One Forgiven Much

Simon, a Pharisee and a halfhearted believer, invited Jesus and three apostles to a meal. By custom the doors were left open, and among those who came in from the street was a woman who had believed the gospel and closed her brothel. She anointed Jesus’ feet with perfumed lotion, wet them with her tears of gratitude, and wiped them with her hair. (147:5.1, 147:5.2, 147:5.3)

When Simon silently judged her a sinner, Jesus answered with the story of two debtors, one owing five hundred denarii and the other fifty. Then he pointed out, one by one, how the woman had supplied the hospitality Simon had neglected. (147:5.4)

Simply that her many sins have been forgiven, and this has led her to love much. (147:5.4)
You have indeed repented of your sins, and they are forgiven. Be not discouraged by the thoughtless and unkind attitude of your fellows; go on in the joy and liberty of the kingdom of heaven. (147:5.4)

As he left, Jesus told Simon that he knew how he was torn between faith and doubts, distraught by fear and troubled by pride. And he declared that no one can close the doors of the kingdom even to the most flagrant sinner on earth, if such a one sincerely seeks entrance. (147:5.6)

V (continued). The Evening Address — Direction Over Status

That evening Jesus spoke on the relative value of status with God and progress in the eternal ascent. Humanly speaking, the transformed woman stood on a level far below Simon’s, yet her soul was in progressive motion; the Pharisees, meanwhile, were caught in the false progress of meaningless ceremonial circles. (147:5.7, 147:5.8)

What you are becoming day by day is of infinitely more importance than what you are today. (147:5.7)
Source 147:5.8
What mattersWhat matters less
The certainty of progressThe rapidity of progress
A Godward directionPresent actual achievement
What you are becoming day by dayWhat you are today
A small but living and growing faithA great intellect with its dead stores of worldly wisdom and spiritual unbelief

At the same time Jesus warned against the folly of presuming on the Father’s love. The heavenly Father is not a lax, indulgent parent ready to condone sin, and he does not tolerate self-destructive acts that ruin moral growth. Jesus thus kept his emphasis on direction from becoming an excuse for license. (147:5.9)

VI. Returning to Capernaum — The Spies and the Sabbath

The leaders at Jerusalem decided that Jesus must be tried by the Sanhedrin on a religious charge and appointed six secret spies, who joined the party at Jericho under the pretense of wanting to become disciples. When Jesus set out on the Sabbath, the spies thought they had their charge, but Jesus instructed Andrew to go only one thousand yards, the legal Sabbath day’s journey. (147:6.2, 147:6.3)

As they passed through the wheat fields, some apostles rubbed out grain and ate it, and the spies protested that this broke the Sabbath. Jesus acknowledged their zeal but said it would be better to guard the health and well-being of their fellows. (147:6.4)

I declare that the Sabbath was made for man and not man for the Sabbath. (147:6.4)

The narrator observes that Jesus’ antagonism to tradition and ceremonial was always positive: it consisted in what he did and what he affirmed, and he spent little time in negative denunciation. Showing a better way rather than criticizing remains a sound approach for readers today. (147:6.6)

VII–VIII. Back in Capernaum; The Feast of Spiritual Goodness

On May 3 the party came by boat to Bethsaida to escape the crowds, but by the next day many, the spies included, had found Jesus again. At a question-and-answer session the leader of the spies asked why he never commanded his disciples to fast. Jesus answered with John’s saying that the sons of the bridechamber do not fast while the bridegroom is with them, and with the parable of new wine in fresh wine skins: prayer is natural for the children of light, but fasting is not part of the gospel of the kingdom. (147:7.1, 147:7.2)

Source 147:7.3
What we meetHow to respond
What is old and also trueLet it abide
What is new but falseReject it
What is new and also trueHave the faith and courage to accept it

That night Jesus quoted Isaiah to teach the true fast: not afflicting one’s soul, but loosing the bonds of wickedness, letting the oppressed go free, and sharing bread with the hungry. Until after two in the morning he explained that what made them secure in the kingdom was faith, not affliction of soul or fasting of body. (147:8.3, 147:8.5, 147:8.6)

Grow in grace by means of that living faith which grasps the fact that you are the sons of God while at the same time it recognizes every man as a brother. (147:8.5)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 147 gathers many scenes into a short visit to Jerusalem, but one current runs beneath them all. Jesus chose words of comfort over miracles, levels of interpretation over the letter of a rule, the direction of progress over present status, and service over formal fasting. Every one of these choices aimed to set people not under the law but within a living faith. (147:6.6, 147:8.5)
