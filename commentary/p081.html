Introduction — Evolution That Cannot Be Stopped

Paper 81 brings the long series of papers on the history of Urantia to a close at the threshold of modern civilization. It was sponsored by an Archangel of Nebadon. The missions of Caligastia and Adam did not turn out as planned, yet the basic organic evolution of the human species kept carrying the races forward. The violet race was smaller in number than intended, but its influence produced an advance in civilization since Adam’s day that far exceeded mankind’s progress during the almost one million years before. (81:0.1, 81:0.2)

Evolution can be delayed but it cannot be stopped. (81:0.1)

The first sections describe in turn agriculture and tools, cities and commerce, the mixed races, and cultural society; the final section sets out fifteen conditions on which the maintenance of civilization depends. It is a paper in which historical narrative turns into reflection on the society we live in today.

I. The Cradle of Civilization — What Climate Accomplished

For some thirty-five thousand years after Adam, the cradle of civilization was southwestern Asia, stretching from the Nile valley through Mesopotamia to Turkestan, and the decisive factor was climate. When the land elevations and climatic changes ended around 15,000 B.C., civilization came to a world-wide standstill. Then climatic evolution accomplished what all other efforts had failed to do: it compelled the hunter to become a herder and a farmer. (81:1.1, 81:1.2, 81:1.3)

The farmer was looked down on for a long time. Because the early agriculturists so generally used slaves, tilling the soil was thought menial, and even in the days of Cain and Abel the sacrifices of the pastoral life were held in greater esteem than the offerings of agriculture. The paper corrects this prejudice. (81:1.4)

the idea that soil toil is a curse, whereas it is the greatest of all blessings (81:1.4)

In regions with a large infusion of violet blood, whole tribes sometimes passed directly from hunting to agriculture. In the highlands of northern Mesopotamia the improved wheat and barley of the Garden were cultivated, and a diet that combined wheat and rice, vegetables and meat, greatly increased the health and vigor of the ancient peoples. (81:1.5, 81:1.7, 81:1.8)

II. The Tools of Civilization — From Toil to Leisure for Thought

The growth of culture is predicated on the development of the tools of civilization, and tools are effective only as they release human energy for higher tasks. The paper reminds today’s reader that our early ancestors had almost none of the leisure we now enjoy. (81:2.1, 81:2.2)

Just as the simple story of how fire began gave way to the legend of Prometheus, people have long attached supernatural explanations to whatever they could not understand. But the frank and fearless search for true causes gave birth to modern science. (81:2.8, 81:2.9)

It turned astrology into astronomy, alchemy into chemistry, and magic into medicine. (81:2.9)

Shelter advanced from caves to huts of wood and stone, and then to the making of bricks. The mud-plastered hut suggested basketry and pottery, and when such a hut accidentally burned, the art of baking was discovered. The paper adds that although machines may create temporary problems, their ultimate benefits are beyond reckoning. (81:2.14, 81:2.15, 81:2.18)

III. Cities, Manufacture, and Commerce

As the grasslands of Turkestan began to dry out from about 12,000 B.C., the higher types of Andites turned to trade and manufacture. The era of independent cities opened about twelve thousand years ago, but the paper warns us not to picture these early cities as refined places. Refuse piled up so that the ground level rose a foot or two every twenty-five years. (81:3.1, 81:3.3)

A bronze culture older than 9000 B.C. has been found in Turkestan, and the mixing of copper and tin was discovered by an Adamsonite. The stone, bronze, and iron ages were not sharply divided; they existed side by side in different regions. By 5000 B.C. horses and wheeled vehicles were widely used in commerce and in war. (81:3.4, 81:3.5, 81:3.6)

The traveling trader and the roving explorer did more to advance historic civilization than all other influences combined. (81:3.7)

Military conquest, colonization, and missionary enterprise also spread culture, but they were secondary to trade. Trade brought different peoples into contact and speeded the cross-fertilization of cultures. The insight that meeting and exchange make civilizations grow is as true now as it was then. (81:3.2, 81:3.7)

IV. The Mixed Races — From Five to Three

By the dawn of historic times, Eurasia, northern Africa, and the islands of the Pacific were covered by composite races formed by the repeated blending of the five basic stocks. The paper notes that the entire skeletal structure shows racial origin better than the skull alone, and explains that the five original types can be observed today in roughly three classes. (81:4.1, 81:4.3, 81:4.10)

This is a rough classification for the study of skeletons. The paper itself adds that these types appear mixed in northern China, the Levant, India, and South America, and that the Adamites and Nodites are now so blended that they can be identified only as a generalized group. The passage can be read as a picture of humanity as one intertwined family. (81:4.9, 81:4.14)

V. Cultural Society — A Guild of Earth Workers

Biologic evolution and cultural civilization do not always go together, yet over the long course of history they are linked as cause and effect. Adam brought no strange arts to Urantia, but his inheritance raised the inherent abilities of the races and hastened progress. And the paper defines society this way: it is not a great club of inherited privilege into which everyone is born with free membership. (81:5.1, 81:5.3)

an exalted and ever-advancing guild of earth workers (81:5.3)

Social association is a kind of survival insurance. People pay the premiums of self-sacrifice and restriction of personal liberty, and in return they receive the protection of the group. Rights carry duties, and group privileges call for group service. (81:5.4, 81:5.6)

VI. The Maintenance of Civilization — Fifteen Conditions

The descendants of the pure Adamic stock have all been dispersed, and there are no more Andites to stimulate civilization anew and no new sources of culture. Present civilization must therefore be maintained and cultivated. The paper names fifteen factors on which it rests. (81:6.1, 81:6.2)

Several of these items speak directly to the social problems of our own day. The paper says that knowledge can be had by education, but wisdom, which is indispensable to true culture, can be secured only through experience. It advises that citizens learn one or more ordinary trades they can use in times of temporary unemployment before being trained in a specialty, and it warns that no civilization can long survive the maintenance of large classes of unemployed. The machine age becomes a calamity only for nations that fail to adjust wisely to the difficulties of transition. (81:6.13, 81:6.22, 81:6.32)

The greatest weight in this list falls on factors that cannot be seen. The paper observes that the twentieth century brought great advances in travel and communication, but that education and ethical appreciation have not kept pace. Just as no stream rises higher than its source, no civilization rises higher than the ideals its society holds. (81:6.25, 81:6.27)

modern civilization is at a standstill in spiritual development and the safeguarding of the home institution. (81:6.25)
Intelligence may control the mechanism of civilization, wisdom may direct it, but spiritual idealism is the energy which really uplifts and advances human culture from one level of attainment to another. (81:6.27)

The paper foresees that humanity’s struggle will move from survival to a standard of living, and then to the quality of thinking. It also reminds us that society is not a divine institution but a phenomenon of evolution, so we should neither despise something because it is old nor embrace it uncritically because it is new. Great changes should not be attempted suddenly, yet moral and spiritual adjustments can be made at once, and this applies just as fully to the individual life. (81:6.28, 81:6.39, 81:6.40)

At first life was a struggle for existence; now, for a standard of living; next it will be for quality of thinking, the coming earthly goal of human existence. (81:6.28)

The remarks on leadership also deserve attention. Wisdom, insight, and foresight are indispensable to the survival of nations, and civilization is never really imperiled until able leadership vanishes. Yet such wise leadership has never exceeded one per cent of the population. That is why it matters so much that each of us become a wise and co-operative citizen in our own place. (81:6.37, 81:6.42)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 81 sums up the long struggle since Adam and tells us that modern culture is the fruit of that hard-won evolution. Since the invention of printing, humanity has raced ahead, quickly taking up the achievements of earlier generations. But what the paper finally stresses is people over devices and ideals over institutions. (81:6.44)

High civilizations are born of the sagacious correlation of material wealth, intellectual greatness, moral worth, social cleverness, and cosmic insight. (81:6.38)
