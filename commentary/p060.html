Introduction — The End of the Sea-Only Age

Paper 60 follows the marine-life era with the early land-life era, the hundred million years that geologists call the Mesozoic. Rising land, cooling crust and oceans, shrinking seas, and a great increase of land in the northern latitudes worked together to change the climate in every region far from the equator. In this way the age when life existed only in the sea came to an end. (60:0.1, 60:4.6)

The era of exclusive marine life has ended. (60:0.1)

The starting point was meager. Very few types had outlived the biologic tribulation at the close of the preceding era; the frogs survived in greatly reduced numbers, and even the spore-bearing plants were nearly extinct. From that thin beginning, the paper traces in three periods how dinosaurs overran the land, then vanished, and how flowers and birds appeared. (60:0.2)

I. The Early Reptilian Age — Reptiles Appear Suddenly

The gypsum and red layers in the deposits of this period show that the climate of America and Europe was arid. Fossils are scarce, but many sandstone footprints of land reptiles remain, and the life of land animals continued unbroken only in certain parts of Africa. The Palisades of the Hudson River, the dolomite limestone of the southern Alps, and Carrara marble are traces of this time. (60:1.1, 60:1.2, 60:1.3, 60:1.4)

When the era opened 150 million years ago, the eastern and central parts of North America, most of Europe, and all of Asia stood well above water. North America was geographically isolated for the first time, though the Bering Strait land bridge soon reappeared to join it to Asia. Great troughs such as the eastern-Connecticut fault formed and were later filled with erosion deposits, and the petrified forests of many regions belong to this epoch. Life did not fare well, but it did better than at the harsh close of the previous era. (60:1.5, 60:1.6, 60:1.7)

Then, 140 million years ago, with only the hint of two prereptilian ancestors that had developed in Africa, the reptiles appeared suddenly in full-fledged form. They soon gave rise to crocodiles, scaled reptiles, sea serpents, and flying reptiles, and the dinosaurs became the monarchs of the age. The early dinosaurs were small and carnivorous, and they walked kangaroolike on their hind legs. (60:1.9, 60:1.10)

They were egg layers and are distinguished from all animals by their small brains, having brains weighing less than one pound to control bodies later weighing as much as forty tons. (60:1.10)

Several million years later the first mammals appeared, but they were nonplacental and soon failed; none survived. The author records this plainly as an experiment that did not succeed. In the seas, hosts of beautiful ammonites flourished, and on the Californian coast alone over a thousand species of them developed. (60:1.11, 60:1.12, 60:1.13)

This was an experimental effort to improve mammalian types, but it did not succeed on Urantia. (60:1.11)

II. The Later Reptilian Age — The Dead End of Sheer Size

The great event of the Jurassic, which began 120 million years ago, was the evolution and decline of the dinosaurs. They came in every size, from species less than two feet long to huge plant-eaters seventy-five feet long. But the larger they grew, the less active and strong they became; they needed enormous amounts of food, and the land was so overrun with them that in the end they starved. (60:2.1, 60:2.3)

they literally starved to death and became extinct—they lacked the intelligence to cope with the situation. (60:2.3)

The Jurassic was also a fresh-water age of many inland lakes, as the rich fresh-water fossils of the Morrison beds of Colorado, Montana, and Wyoming show. The sea’s invasion was greatest in Europe, where the beautiful lithographic stone of southern Germany was laid down. The returning seas improved the weather: corals spread to European waters, ammonites dominated the invertebrate life of the oceans, and a sturgeon type of fish and the modern crustaceans appeared. (60:2.5, 60:2.6, 60:2.8, 60:2.9)

fossils, such as the most delicate wings of olden insects, are preserved as of but yesterday. (60:2.6)

This section also shows that evolution does not always move forward. Two species of dinosaurs, crowded off the land, took to the water and became sea serpents; two other types were driven into the air and became pterosaurs. Neither line lasted, for their brains were far too small for their bodies. (60:2.10, 60:2.11, 60:2.12)

By one hundred million years ago the reptilian age was drawing to a close. At this point the author declares that the direction of evolution is changing. This short sentence is the key to the whole of Paper 60. (60:2.14)

Henceforth, evolution will follow the growth of brains, not physical bulk, and the development of brains will characterize each succeeding epoch of animal evolution and planetary progress. (60:2.14)

III. The Cretaceous Stage — The Flowering-Plant Period — The Age of Birds

The Cretaceous takes its name from the chalk-making foraminifers that dominated the seas. In this period the drifting continents met the deep floor of the Pacific, and the great north-south range from Alaska to Cape Horn began to rise, opening the modern mountain-building stage of geologic history. When the westward drift halted 80 million years ago, its momentum crumpled up the Pacific shore line, producing the circumpacific uplift more than twenty-five thousand miles long—the greatest surface distortion since life appeared. (60:3.1, 60:3.2, 60:3.3, 60:3.11)

The strata of this period left other gifts as well. Layers of pure clay, now used for making earthenware, were laid down along the Atlantic coast, together with chalk, greensand marl, lignite, and oil-bearing deposits. Porous layers permeated with chalk take in water at their outcrops and carry it downward, supplying water to many of today’s arid regions. When the Bering Strait closed 85 million years ago, the marine life of the Atlantic-Gulf waters and the Pacific became uniform. (60:3.5, 60:3.8, 60:3.9, 60:3.10)

Continental drift ended 75 million years ago. The Pacific coast ranges from Alaska to Cape Horn were complete, though as yet with few peaks. The backthrust of the halted drift kept raising the western plains of North America, and when the Rocky Mountain region reached its maximum elevation 70 million years ago, Cambrian rocks in British Columbia were thrust fifteen miles out over Cretaceous layers. Places where older strata lie on top of younger ones call for care when reading the earth’s history. (60:3.12, 60:3.13, 60:3.14)

Ninety million years ago the angiosperms appeared suddenly, along with fig trees, magnolias, and tulip trees, and soon overran the continents. Many of today’s trees—beech, oak, maple, and others—appeared for the first time, and fruits, grasses, and cereals became abundant. The author compares the significance of these seed-bearing plants to the appearance of man. (60:3.7, 60:3.19)

they were second in evolutionary importance only to the appearance of man himself. (60:3.19)

Two attempts to produce flying creatures had failed. The wading and swimming prebirds and the flying dinosaurs both died out, having too little brain for the size of their bodies. Then, 55 million years ago, a small pigeonlike true bird suddenly appeared, springing directly from the reptilian group. It became the ancestor of all bird life. (60:3.21, 60:3.22)

The climate was still warm and uniform; even the arctic regions enjoyed weather like that of central and southern North America today. Sixty million years ago the dinosaurs were still monarchs of the land, but now the smaller, agile carnivorous types took the lead, and with the appearance of grasses, new plant-eating dinosaurs with two horns and a shoulder flange multiplied rapidly. Land turtles twenty feet across, the modern crocodile, and true snakes also appeared. (60:3.18, 60:3.20)

IV. The End of the Chalk Period — Mountains Born and the Next Stage Set

The close of the Cretaceous ended the great sea invasions of the continents. North America had undergone just twenty-four great inundations, with land and sea alternating in dominance in million-year cycles. These rhythmic crustal movements will continue, but with diminishing frequency and extent. (60:4.1)

The chief factor determining where a mountain range forms is not continental pressure but a pre-existing lowland or trough filled with lighter deposits. When the crust is put under pressure, these lighter areas are the first to crumple and rise. Today’s Rocky Mountains are not the original elevation; that land was leveled by erosion and then raised again, and Pikes Peak and Longs Peak span two or more generations of mountain lives. (60:4.2, 60:4.4)

At the end of this period the ammonites declined rapidly, and the fern forests gave way to pines and other modern trees, including the giant redwoods. There were still no placental mammals, but the stage was fully set for the ancestors of the future mammals to appear. (60:4.5)

the biologic stage is fully set for the appearance, in a subsequent age, of the early ancestors of the future mammalian types. (60:4.5)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

The central lesson of Paper 60 is that intelligence matters more than size. Dinosaurs that ruled forty-ton bodies with brains of less than a pound flourished until they covered the land, yet they vanished because they lacked the intelligence to meet their situation. From then on, evolution follows the growth of brains rather than bulk. The paper also does not hide failed experiments—the first mammals and two attempts at flying creatures—and it shows that the true birds and the flowering plants came after them.
