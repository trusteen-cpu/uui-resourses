Introduction — The Most Organized Five Months

From May 3 to October 3, A.D. 28, Jesus and the apostles lived at the Zebedee home in Bethsaida. By the seaside nearby rose a tented city of five hundred to fifteen hundred people, under the general supervision of David Zebedee. The camp was a model of order and sanitation, and the sick were cared for separately under a Syrian physician named Elman. (148:0.1)

The narrator calls this the longest settled and best-organized period of the Master’s teaching. Several thousand came and went, from every part of the Roman Empire and from lands east of the Euphrates. This paper records the school and hospital founded in those five months, and the deep conversations held in the garden. (148:0.4)

I. A New School of the Prophets — Learning and Doing

Peter, James, and Andrew passed on applicants, and the students represented the peoples of the Roman world and the East as far as India. The school was run on the plan of learning and doing: what the students learned in the morning they taught by the seaside in the afternoon, and in the evening they discussed both together. (148:1.1)

Most striking is that the teaching was not made uniform. Each apostle presented his own interpretation of the gospel, and Jesus upheld this diversity, harmonizing the various views at his weekly question hours. (148:1.2)

They made no effort to teach just alike; there was no standardized or dogmatic formulation of theologic doctrines. (148:1.2)

The hundred and more evangelists trained here became the material from which the later seventy were drawn. Allowing the same truth to be told in the language of different experiences reminds faith communities today of the worth of living diversity over uniformity. (148:1.3)

II. The Bethsaida Hospital — Healing Without Miracles

With a corps of twenty-five young women and twelve men, Elman ran for four months what should be regarded as the kingdom’s first hospital. They treated the sick by all known material methods together with the spiritual practices of prayer and faith encouragement. Jesus visited at least three times a week and made personal contact with each sufferer. (148:2.1)

As far as we know, no so-called miracles of supernatural healing occurred among the one thousand afflicted and ailing persons who went away from this infirmary improved or cured. (148:2.1)

Even so, the patients kept saying that Jesus had healed them. The narrator explains that these were transformations of mind and spirit in expectant, faith-filled people under the influence of a strong and beneficent personality whose ministry banished fear and anxiety. It is an insight that healing happens when medicine, faith, and personal care work together. (148:2.2)

Elman and his associates tried to correct the near-universal belief that sickness was caused by unclean spirits, with little success. Jesus himself did not reveal techniques of treatment or the unknown causes of disease, keeping to the instructions Immanuel had given him before the incarnation. Still, those caring for the sick learned much by watching how he inspired faith and confidence in the suffering. (148:2.3, 148:2.4)

III. The Father’s Business — Time Alone

During this period Jesus led public services fewer than a dozen times, and not since his baptism had he been so much alone. Asked why he was so often absent, he always answered that he was “about the Father’s business.” Each time he took along any two apostles who were free, so that all twelve had the chance of close association with him. (148:3.1, 148:3.2, 148:3.3)

The narrator infers that during these hours Jesus was in direct association with his chief directors of universe affairs. This is offered only as an inference: the apostles nearby saw his features change rapidly but heard no words and saw no celestial beings. That the busiest season, with crowds pressing in, was also the season of his longest solitude suggests that the depth of outward work comes from quiet hours. (148:3.4, 148:3.5)

IV. Evil, Sin, and Iniquity — Thomas’s Question

Thomas asked why one must be born again and what evil is. Jesus first warned him not to confuse evil with “the evil one,” the high administrator who went into deliberate rebellion. Then he distinguished three concepts by their relation to the Father’s will. (148:4.1, 148:4.2)

Men are, indeed, by nature evil, but not necessarily sinful. (148:4.8)
Evil is inherent in the natural order of this world, but sin is an attitude of conscious rebellion which was brought to this world by those who fell from spiritual light into gross darkness. (148:4.6)

Jesus pointed out that Thomas was confused by the idea that mankind fell from a perfect state. Man is not descending toward destruction; by entering the kingdom he is ascending to divine perfection. Imperfection of natural endowment is not sin, nor does it make man an estranged stepchild of the Father. This distinction gives an accurate self-understanding back to anyone weighed down by guilt over his or her shortcomings. (148:4.6, 148:4.8, 148:4.9)

V. The Purpose of Affliction — Nathaniel’s Question

Nathaniel asked why a loving Father permits his children to suffer so many afflictions. Jesus explained that the natural order of this world has been upset many times by the sinful adventures of rebels, and that setting things right will take many ages. (148:5.1, 148:5.2)

But of one thing you may be sure: The Father does not send affliction as an arbitrary punishment for wrongdoing. (148:5.3)

The imperfections of evil are inherent, the penalties of sin inevitable, and the destroying consequences of iniquity inexorable. We should not, then, blame God for the natural results of the life we choose. It is the Father’s will that we work persistently to better our estate, and intelligent application can overcome much earthly misery. We should also drop the habit of laying on God everything we fail to understand. (148:5.3, 148:5.4)

VI. The Misunderstanding of Suffering — Discourse on Job

That same evening John asked about the suffering of the apparently innocent, and Jesus answered through the story of Job. He first corrected the time-honored notion that material prosperity is evidence of divine favor: the Father loves the poor just as much as the rich. Men do reap what they sow, but not all suffering is punishment for past sin. (148:6.1, 148:6.2, 148:6.3)

Job found no philosophical answer, but after a great struggle between faith and doubt he began to see the light of life. Jesus said that it was his mission to show the human God Job longed for, a divine Being who knows man’s mortal estate. The Father does not speak from the whirlwind but within the human heart as a still, small voice. (148:6.7, 148:6.9, 148:6.10)

John pondered these sayings for many days, and his whole later life was changed by them. In later times he did much to help the other apostles change their views about the source and purpose of human afflictions. (148:6.12)

VII. The Man with the Withered Hand — Doing Good on the Sabbath

On a Sabbath before the second tour, the leader of the spies induced a man with a withered hand to ask whether it was lawful to be healed on the Sabbath. Jesus asked whether one would lift a sheep out of a pit on the Sabbath, and, declaring how much more valuable a man is than a sheep, proclaimed it lawful to do good. When the man stretched out his hand, it was made whole. (148:7.1, 148:7.2, 148:7.3)

I proclaim that it is lawful to do good to men on the Sabbath day. (148:7.2)

The narrator explains that this was the first miracle Jesus performed in response to his enemies’ challenge, done not as a display of power but as a protest against turning the Sabbath into a bondage of meaningless restrictions. When the people were minded to turn on the Pharisees, Jesus calmed them: permission to do good is no license to do harm. (148:7.3, 148:7.4)

VIII–IX. Last Week at Bethsaida; Healing the Paralytic — The Camp Disbands

Much happened in the last week. Abraham, a young member of the Sanhedrin, was baptized by Abner and gave his goods to the apostolic treasury, helping to send out the evangelists. An invitation from Alexandria was courteously declined. Jesus allowed Kirmeth, a self-styled trance prophet from Bagdad, freedom to speak for a few days, and the people soon recognized for themselves that his teaching was unsound. (148:8.1, 148:8.2, 148:8.3, 148:8.5)

During the final meeting on October 1, a paralytic from Capernaum had his friends loosen the roof tiles and lower him on his couch before Jesus. He said he wished to be made whole in order to serve in the kingdom. Seeing his faith, Jesus declared his sins forgiven, and before those who called this blasphemy he told the man to rise, take up his bed, and go home. (148:9.1, 148:9.2, 148:9.3)

I would be made whole that I might serve in the kingdom of heaven. (148:9.2)

Just then messengers came summoning the spies back to the Sanhedrin. Their leader and two others returned, but the other three Pharisees confessed faith in Jesus and were baptized by Peter. Even among those sent to watch, some opened their hearts to the truth. (148:9.4)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

At the heart of Paper 148 is a corrected way of seeing suffering. God does not send affliction as punishment, and we are not stepchildren estranged from the Father because of our shortcomings. The paper also offers practical models: teaching without uniformity, healing joined with personal care, and time spent alone with the Father. (148:4.8, 148:5.3)
