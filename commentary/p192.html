Introduction — Back to Galilee

By the time the apostles left for Galilee, the Jewish leaders had grown quiet. Since Jesus appeared only to believers and the apostles were in hiding, the rulers concluded that the gospel movement had been crushed. They were troubled by the spreading rumors of resurrection, but they counted on the bribed guards to say that the followers had removed the body. (192:0.1)

From this time Peter became the recognized head of the apostolic group. Jesus had never given him this authority, nor had his fellows formally chosen him. Because he was the chief preacher, he took the place naturally and by common consent, for public preaching had now become the apostles’ chief work. Later Matthias, who took Judas’s place, became treasurer. (192:0.2)

When the apostles set out on Monday morning, young John Mark followed them secretly and only openly joined them well beyond Bethany. They stopped several times along the way to tell of the risen Master and did not reach Bethsaida until late Wednesday night. (192:0.4, 192:0.5)

I. Appearance by the Lake — Not a Miracle but Foreknowledge, and a Charcoal Fire

At Peter’s suggestion the apostles fished all night but caught nothing. They did not mind, for they had much to talk about. About six o’clock on the morning of Friday, April 21, as they were coming back to shore, a man standing by a fire called to them to cast the net on the right side. When the net filled, John, quicker of perception, recognized the Master, and Peter plunged at once into the water. It was the first appearance in Galilee and the thirteenth in all. (192:1.1, 192:1.2, 192:1.3)

The apostles did not fully understand why Jesus wanted to meet them in Galilee rather than Jerusalem. The text explains that he wished to meet them away from the confined surroundings charged with memories of fear, betrayal, and death, out in the open nature where they had once lived together. He knew the power of a place to heal wounded hearts. (192:1.2)

There was a simple reason they did not recognize him at first. Asking fishermen on the beach what they had caught was the customary greeting of the fish dealers from Tarichea who came down to buy the fresh catch. The text paints even this resurrection scene in the texture of ordinary life. (192:1.9)

There were 153 large fish. The text says plainly that it is a mistake to call this another miraculous catch. (192:1.7)

There was no miracle connected with this episode. It was merely an exercise of the Master’s preknowledge. (192:1.7)

John Mark, seeing eleven men instead of ten, ran ahead and knelt at the Master’s feet. Unlike the solemn greeting in Jerusalem, Jesus welcomed him in commonplace tones. Peter, meanwhile, was shaken at the sight of the charcoal fire on the beach, which brought back the fire in Annas’s courtyard on the night he denied his Master. But he gathered himself and fell at the Master’s feet. (192:1.4, 192:1.6)

Well, John, I am glad to see you again (192:1.4)

Jesus had them cook the fish and break the bread, and at the end he himself served the lad. Then he talked of all they had been through together by that lake. That the risen Master’s first act was to give breakfast to weary men shows how concrete and human his ministry was. And for Simon Zelotes, who had gone home discouraged, Jesus sent Peter and Andrew to bring him back that very day. (192:1.8, 192:1.10, 192:1.11)

II. Visiting with the Apostles Two and Two — A Different Question for Each

After breakfast Jesus walked along the beach with the apostles two by two. He did not say the same thing to everyone. Some he asked whether they loved him; others, whether they believed in him, served him, or obeyed him. And he gave each counsel fitted exactly to his weaknesses and his future. (192:2.1, 192:2.7, 192:2.9, 192:2.11)

Peter was asked the same question three times. The third time he was grieved, feeling that the Master seemed to distrust him, but each time Jesus answered with a commission to care for his flock. The man who had denied him three times received three confessions and three commissions. The last charge shows clearly the pattern of the shepherd. (192:2.2, 192:2.3, 192:2.4)

Love the flock as I have loved you and devote yourself to their welfare even as I have devoted my life to your welfare. (192:2.4)

When Peter asked what would become of John, Jesus told him not to be concerned about his brethren’s future. This saying spread as meaning that John would not die before the Master’s return, and that interpretation actually did much to bring Simon Zelotes back to service. (192:2.5, 192:2.6)

The words to Andrew were for one who had laid down a place of leadership. Once in charge of directing his brethren, he now had to trust others more, even his brother Peter. Foreseeing that when persecution scattered the apostles, his brother James would bear a burden his experience could hardly carry, Jesus asked Andrew to become his counselor. It shows that great service is possible even from a place one has stepped back from. (192:2.7)

Only make sure that you follow me. (192:2.5)
Admix friendship with your counsel and add love to your philosophy. (192:2.10)

Thomas was told to believe like a child but to stop acting childishly. The twins were told that even if their outward work changed, their allegiance must not waver. This charge is the one nearest to most believers, who live ordinary everyday lives. (192:2.9, 192:2.13)

Philip and Matthew had long looked after money, supplies, and the material needs of their brethren. Jesus told them they were now as free as the others to proclaim the good news; the only law they had to obey was the command to go forth and preach the gospel. James Zebedee he told to weigh the results of his words and deeds and to pray for calmness of spirit, and he foreshadowed the time when James would drink the cup of sacrifice. (192:2.8, 192:2.11, 192:2.12)

Nothing which a son of God does can be common. (192:2.13)

III. On the Mount of Ordination — A Pledge Renewed

At noon on Saturday, April 22, the eleven gathered on the hill near Capernaum where they had first been ordained. It was the fourteenth appearance. They knelt in a circle about the Master, and Jesus repeated the original charges and re-enacted the ordination scene. (192:3.1, 192:3.2)

Everything was as they remembered it except the prayer. It was spoken in tones of majesty and power such as the apostles had never heard. The eleven never forgot this experience of rededication. (192:3.2)

To return to the place of their first ordination and renew the same pledge was to confirm that, even after failure and scattering, their calling had not been withdrawn. On the path of faith too, going back to the place of one’s first dedication and renewing one’s heart can give great strength. (192:3.2)

Their Master now spoke with the rulers of the universes as one who, in his own universe, had had all power and authority committed to his hand. (192:3.2)

For a whole week afterward no one saw Jesus. Unsure whether he had gone to the Father, the apostles stayed at Bethsaida and did not even go fishing for fear of missing him. During that time Jesus was occupied with the morontia creatures in the affairs of his morontia transition. (192:3.3)

IV. The Lakeside Gathering and After — The Center of the Message Shifts

As news of the appearances spread through Galilee, Peter announced a public meeting for the Sabbath afternoon. On Saturday, April 29, more than five hundred believers gathered at Bethsaida to hear his first public sermon since the resurrection. Just as he finished his declaration, the Master appeared beside him, wished them peace, and vanished. It was the fifteenth appearance. (192:4.1, 192:4.2, 192:4.3)

We affirm that Jesus of Nazareth is not dead (192:4.3)

The apostles returned to Jerusalem, and John Mark lost his father, Elijah Mark, a few hours before they arrived. The assurance of the resurrection comforted them, but they felt deeply the loss of a staunch friend. The upper room of the Mark home remained their headquarters until after Pentecost. (192:4.4, 192:4.5)

That Thursday night the apostles, except Thomas, Simon Zelotes, and the twins, pledged themselves to proclaim publicly the new gospel of the risen Lord. Nathaniel objected to shifting the center of the message, but he could not withstand Peter’s eloquence and the enthusiasm of the believers. (192:4.7)

The text calls those who did this well-meaning representatives; it was enthusiasm, not ill will, that drove the change. Here the reader may ask himself or herself: am I holding beliefs about Jesus, or am I living the faith that Jesus lived? (192:4.8)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

The Jesus of Paper 192 chooses breakfast over miracles and questions fitted to each person over rebuke. In nine walks he named each apostle’s weakness precisely, yet wrapped it in a promise for the future. And the paper ends by quietly recording the crossroads at which the center of the gospel began to shift. (192:2.1, 192:4.8)

What the reader gains from this paper is respect for individuality. Jesus asked each person a different question, and each question pointed exactly to where that person most needed to grow. We too can consider what he would ask us: Do you love me? Do you trust me? Do you serve me? Do you obey me? (192:2.1, 192:2.9)
