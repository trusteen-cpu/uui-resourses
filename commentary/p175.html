Introduction — The Place of the Last Appeal

Shortly after two o’clock on Tuesday afternoon, Jesus arrived at the temple with eleven apostles, Joseph of Arimathea, the thirty Greeks, and other disciples, and began his last address in its courts. In the morning various groups had questioned him; that afternoon no one asked him anything. The money-changers and merchants had not dared to return since being driven out the day before, and the court was quiet and orderly. (175:0.1, 175:0.2)

The narrator presents the discourse as having two sides: it was the last appeal to the Jewish people and the final indictment of the religious leaders who sought to destroy him. Before he began, Jesus looked down tenderly on his audience. One thing must be kept in mind throughout this paper: the indictment was aimed not at a whole people but at particular leaders of that day and their practices. (175:0.1, 175:0.2)

I. The Discourse — A Door Left Open to the End

Jesus recalled how long he had proclaimed the Father’s love, and how many had seen the light and entered the kingdom. He and his apostles had done their utmost, in every way consistent with the Father’s will, to live in peace with their brethren and to conform to the reasonable requirements of Moses’ laws and Israel’s traditions. But the leaders would not have peace. (175:1.1, 175:1.2)

Even now it is not too late for this people to receive the word of heaven and to welcome the Son of Man. (175:1.3)

The first part of the discourse is closer to invitation than to warning. Jesus said he had offered the joy and liberty of sonship even to the very men who now sought his destruction, and that even now the Father would receive them if they turned to him. At the same time he solemnly warned that if this last chance were rejected, the role of standard-bearer of truth would pass to others. (175:1.3, 175:1.5, 175:1.6)

Jesus said that generation after generation the Father had sent prophets to teach and warn, and that generation after generation these teachers had been rejected. He reminded his hearers that this people had been called to become the light of the world, to show forth the spiritual glory of a God-knowing race. Beneath the rebuke lies respect for that calling and deep sorrow at seeing it cast aside. (175:1.4, 175:1.5)

He noted that many Jews, Samaritans, and even gentiles had believed the gospel. And he urged the crowd to co-operate with the elders: not to join in any plan to destroy the Son of Man, but in everything related to the peace of Israel to be subject to them and observe the essentials of the law—only not to pattern after their evil works. (175:1.7, 175:1.8)

Remember, this is the sin of these rulers: They say that which is good, but they do it not. (175:1.8)

Jesus then restated the principle of humility and service. Call no man Father in the spiritual sense, for there is one Father, God. Do not seek to lord it over your brethren; whoever would be greatest must become the server of all. These words criticize the leaders, but they also hold up a mirror to everyone who hears them. (175:1.10)

Seek in your daily lives, not self-glorification, but the glory of God. (175:1.10)

I (continued). The Discourse — An Indictment of Hypocrisy

Before his indictment Jesus made it plain that he bore no malice toward the chief priests, scribes, and Pharisees. He knew too that many in his audience believed in secret and would openly profess their allegiance when his hour came. What he challenged was not persons but an attitude: professing to talk with God while rejecting the one who came to reveal the Father. (175:1.11)

I bear no malice toward these chief priests and rulers who even now seek my destruction (175:1.11)

The figure of the cup and the platter yields the most practical lesson of the whole indictment: cleanse the inside first, and what spills over will of itself cleanse the outside. The danger of conforming outward performance to the letter of the law while neglecting the soul belongs to every religion and every age. (175:1.18)

Most of the charges aim at the gap between outside and inside: piety performed to be seen, scrupulous attention to small rules while mercy and faith are neglected, and the contradiction of adorning the prophets’ tombs while rejecting present truth. When Jesus said that if the blind lead the blind both will fall into the pit, he was warning that a leader’s hypocrisy ends by harming those who follow. (175:1.15, 175:1.17, 175:1.20)

Do you not recognize how much better it would be first to cleanse the inside of the cup, and then that which spills over would of itself cleanse the outside? (175:1.18)

Yet even at the end of these severe words, Jesus held out mercy once more. Even now, he said, he offered mercy and forgiveness in the Father’s name and proffered the loving hand of eternal fellowship. Then he lamented that he would have gathered Jerusalem’s children as a hen gathers her chickens, but they would not; he declared that his work in the temple was done and beckoned his followers to depart. (175:1.22, 175:1.23, 175:1.24, 175:1.25)

II. Status of Individual Jews — The Narrator’s Solemn Charge

Immediately after recording the discourse, the narrator stops the story to address the reader directly. The fact that certain spiritual leaders and religious teachers of that time rejected Jesus and conspired to bring about his death does not in any way affect the standing of any individual Jew before God. This section is the key to reading the whole paper rightly. (175:2.1)

And it should not cause those who profess to be followers of the Christ to be prejudiced against the Jew as a fellow mortal. (175:2.1)

The narrator reminds us that Jesus himself was a Jew by natural birth, and that those who first accepted his gospel and died for its truth—Peter, Philip, Matthew, and others—were Palestinian Jews. He goes so far as to say that a shudder of horror passes over the onlooking celestial beings when professed followers of Jesus persecute these men’s descendants. (175:2.1, 175:2.2)

The charge exposes the contradiction of justifying hatred in the name of one who taught his disciples to love even their enemies. The lesson for the reader is clear: no group or individual today may be condemned on the basis of a scene from history, for God is no respecter of persons. (175:2.3)

III–IV. The Fateful Sanhedrin Meeting and the Night in Jerusalem

At eight o’clock that evening the Sanhedrin was called to order. It had informally decreed his death before, but it had never resolved to arrest him and bring about his death at any and all costs. Just before midnight it voted unanimously to impose the death sentence on both Jesus and Lazarus, ordering that Jesus be taken early the next morning but not in public—in secret, preferably suddenly and at night. (175:3.1, 175:3.3)

The passing of death sentence (even before his trial) upon the Son of God was the Sanhedrin’s reply to the last offer of heavenly mercy ever to be extended to the Jewish nation, as such. (175:3.1)

The narrator interprets this decision as the Sanhedrin’s reply to the last offer of heavenly mercy extended to the nation as such, and says that from then on the nation was left to finish its national life on a purely human footing among the nations. This is a statement about the nation as a sociopolitical group, and, as the preceding section stressed, it is distinct from the spiritual standing of any individual. (175:3.1, 175:3.2, 175:2.1)

The Sanhedrin adjourned near midnight, agreeing to meet at ten the next morning at the home of Caiaphas the high priest to formulate the charges. A small group of Sadducees actually proposed assassination, but the Pharisees utterly refused to countenance it. The narrator fairly records that even within the opposing camp there was a line some would not cross. (175:4.13, 175:4.14)

Judas did not hear the merciful first half of the discourse; he had been conferring with Sadducean relatives and friends about how best to break away from the apostles. Listening to the indictment in the second half, he finally and fully made up his mind to forsake the gospel movement. Meanwhile, at the home of Nicodemus, more than thirty prominent Jews who believed in secret agreed to declare their allegiance openly the moment they heard of his arrest. (175:4.1, 175:4.3)

All present agreed that they would make open acknowledgment of their allegiance to the Master in the very hour they should hear of his arrest. And that is just what they did. (175:4.3)

That night, while all Jerusalem buzzed with the question “What will they do with Jesus?”, a vast concourse of celestial beings hovered over the scene, longing to help their beloved Sovereign but restrained by their commanding superiors from acting. (175:4.2, 175:4.15)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 175 contains some of the sternest words Jesus ever spoke, yet they are framed on both sides by mercy. He indicted without malice, and after indicting he held out his hand again. And the narrator insists that this scene must never be used to justify hatred of any people or person. To read this paper rightly is to take its warning against hypocrisy not as a story about others but as an occasion for self-examination. (175:1.11, 175:1.22, 175:2.3)
