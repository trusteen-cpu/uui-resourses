Introduction — The Universe Has a Purpose

Paper 4 deals with the relation God sustains to the universe. Its starting point is that the universe is not a machine rolling along without purpose. God created the universes of his own free and sovereign will, in accordance with an all-wise and eternal purpose, and he is executing that purpose throughout all time. The author humbly adds that it is doubtful whether anyone besides the Paradise Deities and their highest associates really knows very much about that eternal purpose. (4:0.1)

The perfect universe of Havona exists primarily for the pleasure and satisfaction of the Creators, while also serving as the pattern for all other universes and as the finishing school for pilgrims bound for Paradise. The ascension plan that perfects evolutionary mortals is one of the chief concerns of the superuniverses, but it is by no means the only occupation of the celestial hosts. This passage quietly invites us to let go of the idea that we are the center of the universe. (4:0.2, 4:0.3)

I. The Universe Attitude of the Father — What Providence Is

The first section corrects misunderstandings about providence. Providence is real, but it is not the childish, arbitrary, and material ministry that many have imagined. It consists in the interlocking activities of celestial beings and divine spirits who, in accordance with cosmic law, labor for the honor of God and the spiritual advancement of his children. And providence and evolutionary progress are not in conflict. (4:1.1, 4:1.2)

Can you not advance in your concept of God’s dealing with man to that level where you recognize that the watchword of the universe is progress? (4:1.2)

God is not one who created and then withdrew. If the Father should retire as the upholder of creation, there would immediately occur a universal collapse. The universe is constantly being renewed, and the Father unceasingly pours forth energy, light, and life. Even amid apparent instability, there is an underlying order and security. (4:1.5, 4:1.6)

The universe is not wound up like a clock to run just so long and then cease to function (4:1.6)

In this section the Divine Counselor confesses that even he does not understand everything. He is well versed in the forces, minds, and spirits of the universes, yet he is constantly perplexed by the workings of the three Absolutes of potentiality. He explains the amazing co-ordination of apparently unrelated events as the living presence of the Supreme Being and the providential function of the Conjoint Actor. Much of what we call “accident” is, in the larger view, part of the drama the Infinite is unfolding. (4:1.7, 4:1.8, 4:1.10, 4:1.12)

II. God and Nature — Nature Is Not God

Nature is, in a limited sense, the physical habit of God. God acts according to unchanging law throughout the master universe, but he modifies the patterns of his action to fit the evolutionary plans of each local universe and planet. On our world, moreover, the operation of those laws has been further affected by the errors and insurrections of beings belonging to our system of Satania. (4:2.1, 4:2.2)

Source 4:2.3
Factor shaping natureWhat it is
The first factorThe immutability, perfection, and rectitude of Paradise Deity
The second factorThe experimental plans, executive blunders, insurrectionary errors, incompleteness of development, and imperfection of wisdom of extra-Paradise creatures
The resultA nature stable underneath, yet of changing mood and varied from one local universe to another

So the paper says that nature can never be a faithful portrayal of God. It mixes the perfect and the partial, and its face has been scarred by the rebellion and misconduct of myriads of creatures. To worship nature because God in some limited sense pervades it is a travesty. (4:2.4, 4:2.5, 4:2.6)

No, nature is not God. Nature is not an object of worship. (4:2.8)

Yet the defects of nature do not imply defects in God. The paper likens nature’s imperfections to the “stop-moments” in a moving reel of infinity picturization. It is these very interruptions that allow the finite mind to catch a fleeting glimpse of divine reality. God appears defective only when we look at nature with natural eyes, unaided by revelation. This is a viewpoint worth holding on to when disaster and suffering tempt us to doubt God’s goodness. (4:2.7)

III. God’s Unchanging Character — Neither Jealous Nor Wrathful

All too long has man thought of God as one like himself. God is jealous of no one. The sight of man bowing before his baser passions and his idols stirs God to be jealous for man, but never of him. Nor is God capable of wrath and anger in the human sense; such feelings are utterly foreign to the Father’s perfect nature. (4:3.1, 4:3.2)

As a major reason for our difficulty in understanding God, the paper names the Lucifer rebellion and the Caligastia betrayal. On worlds not segregated by sin, the evolutionary races form far better ideas of the Father. Our distorted picture of God is bound up with the particular history of our planet. (4:3.3)

The end of the section offers a clue to why relative evil exists. The Father’s infinite goodness is beyond the comprehension of finite minds, so relative goodness can be exhibited only in contrast with comparative evil. Here the paper is careful to add that this is not sin. And because God’s character is infinitely superhuman, it must be personalized, as in the divine Sons, before we can grasp it by faith. (4:3.6, 4:3.7)

IV. The Realization of God — Changeless but Not Immobile

Since God is self-existent, he is absolutely independent and changeless. But this changelessness does not mean immobility. God can pass from simplicity to complexity, from infinity to finitude, from the divine to the human, modifying the manifestations of his absoluteness. This is possible because God is will. (4:4.1, 4:4.2)

God has will—he is will. (4:4.2)

Personality and goodness God shares with other beings, but infinity of will is his alone. Yet the whole of his absolute nature is subject to the relationship of the Creator to his creature family. Trinitarian justice may be precise, but in his family relationship with the creatures of time God is governed by divine sentiment. That is why the author says that of all possible titles, he was instructed to portray God as the Universal Father. (4:4.4, 4:4.5)

Source 4:4.7
ViewpointGod is

RevelationShows that this First Cause is the God of religion, full of mercy and goodness and pledged to the eternal survival of his children

The paper says that God’s free will is neither ruled by power nor guided by intellect alone. The divine personality consists in spirit and shows itself to the universes as love. God’s tender nature finds its greatest satisfaction in loving and being loved. What we worship, too, is not the abstract concept of the Infinite but the experience-idea of God—our grasp of the personality and divinity of our highest concept of Deity. (4:4.6, 4:4.8)

God is a Father in the highest sense of the term. (4:4.6)

This section sums up the victory of faith beautifully. Faced with the awful spectacle of human limitations, faith declares: even if I cannot do this, a part of the Father lives in me who can and will. That is the faith that overcomes the world. (4:4.9)

Even if I cannot do this, there lives in me one who can and will do it, a part of the Father-Absolute of the universe of universes. (4:4.9)

V. Erroneous Ideas of God — No God Needs Appeasing

The last section begins with the limits of religious tradition. Tradition is the imperfectly preserved record of the experiences of God-knowing people of past ages, and it has invariably been altered because primitive man was a mythmaker. Especially confusing has been the failure of sacred books to distinguish between different divine beings, gathering them all under the single name “God.” (4:5.1, 4:5.2)

Source 4:5.2

Messages of subordinate personalitiesMessages from Life Carriers and various orders of angels recorded as coming from God himself

The gods who rampage in the storm, shake the earth in their wrath, and judge men with famine and flood are the gods of primitive religion, not the Gods who rule the universes. The same is true of the idea of appeasing an angry God through sacrifice, penance, and the shedding of blood. The paper declares it an affront to God to teach that innocent blood must be shed to win his favor. Yet it fairly acknowledges that Moses made a distinct advance when he forbade human sacrifice and substituted the sacrifice of animals. (4:5.3, 4:5.4, 4:5.5)

It is an affront to God to believe, hold, or teach that innocent blood must be shed in order to win his favor or to divert the fictitious divine wrath. (4:5.4)

In the same vein the paper explains Michael’s bestowal afresh. The bestowal was not needed to win God’s favor; it was inherent in the situation of closing a planetary age, and it was the final personal act of a Creator Son in earning the experiential sovereignty of his universe. The teaching that the Father’s mercy was withheld until he saw blood shed on the cross is a gross distortion of God’s character. (4:5.6)

But the inhabitants of Urantia are to find deliverance from these ancient errors and pagan superstitions respecting the nature of the Universal Father. (4:5.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 4 helps us put in order the picture of God we carry in our minds. God has not left the universe but upholds it even now; he is not identical with nature; he is neither jealous nor wrathful; and he is no deity to be appeased with blood. He is will, and above all he is Father. The task this paper leaves us is to lay down the ideas of God born of fear and to receive the Father whom Jesus revealed.

Paper 5 carries this line of thought forward to God’s relation to the individual. How the Father who upholds the universe draws near to each one of us, and how we in turn come to him, is the subject of the next paper.
