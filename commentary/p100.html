Introduction — From the Ordinary Person to a Personality of Power

Where Paper 99 dealt with religion’s place in society, Paper 100 turns to its starting point, the inner life of a single person. Its author, a Melchizedek of Nebadon, says that dynamic religious living transforms the mediocre individual into a personality of idealistic power. Religion serves the progress of all by fostering the progress of each, and love supplies the soil in which that growth takes place. (100:0.1, 100:0.2, 100:7.19)

religion ennobles the commonplace drudgery of daily living. (100:0.2)

I. Religious Growth — The Loyal Tadpole

A child judges experience by its pleasure; maturity is measured by how far personal pleasure gives way to higher meanings. Some people are too busy to grow, and the chief hindrances to growth are prejudice and ignorance. A child should be given the chance to grow his own religious experience rather than having a ready-made adult experience forced on him. What children carry with them permanently is not precept or even example, but the loyalty of the adults around them. (100:1.1, 100:1.2, 100:1.3, 100:1.4)

The quickest way for a tadpole to become a frog is to live loyally each moment as a tadpole. (100:1.4)
Religion cannot be bestowed, received, loaned, learned, or lost. (100:1.7)

This does not mean we should simply sit and wait. The paper names, quite concretely, the soil that growth requires and the habits that favor it. The factors may be intentional, but the growth itself is always unconscious—and “unconscious” here means not the subconscious but creative activity on the superconscious levels of mind. (100:1.5, 100:1.8, 100:1.9)

Religious experience is strongly influenced by physical health, inherited temperament, and social environment. Yet none of these can block the inner progress of a soul dedicated to doing the Father’s will. Every normal mortal has an innate drive toward growth, and the sure way to foster it is to maintain an attitude of wholehearted devotion to supreme values. (100:1.6)

II. Spiritual Growth — The Unassailable Citadel of the Spirit

Spiritual development depends on two things: keeping a living connection with true spiritual forces, and bearing spiritual fruit by passing on to others what we have received. Growth begins with an awakening to needs, moves on to a discernment of meanings, and then to a discovery of values. This whole experience is the reality of religion, as distinct from mere theological belief. (100:2.1, 100:2.2)

Spirituality becomes at once the indicator of one’s nearness to God and the measure of one’s usefulness to fellow beings. (100:2.4)

Religion grown in this way works on three levels of personality: upon the mind, in the evolving soul, and with the indwelling spirit. Actual spiritual status is measured by attunement with the Thought Adjuster, and its goal is the maximum of Godlikeness. The paper calls eternal life an endless quest for infinite values. (100:2.3, 100:2.5)

Mortals are entitled to physical pleasures and human affections, but these are not the eternal foundations on which an immortal personality is built. That is why the God-knowing person is not shaken when worldly securities collapse. Spirit-born individuals can calmly stand by while their fondest hopes crash, knowing such catastrophes to be redirections that prepare for more enduring realities. (100:2.6, 100:2.7, 100:2.8)

Temporal securities are vulnerable, but spiritual sureties are impregnable. (100:2.7)

III. Concepts of Supreme Value — Meaning, Value, and Growth

Religion is not a technique for attaining a static, blissful peace of mind; it is an impulse that organizes the soul for dynamic service. Human likes and dislikes do not determine good and evil. This section draws careful distinctions among the concepts that surround value. (100:3.1, 100:3.2)

Man cannot cause growth; he can only supply favorable conditions for it. Love grows in just this way. Social growth cannot be secured by legislation, nor moral growth by improved administration. Man’s sole contribution to growth is the mobilization of all the powers of his personality—living faith. (100:3.7)

IV. Problems of Growth — Through Conflict, by Understanding, to Love

New meanings emerge only amid conflict. There is no growth without psychic conflict and spiritual agitation, and the slothful animal mind rebels at the effort of wrestling with cosmic problems. The great task of religious living is to unify the personality by the dominance of love, and the highest happiness is inseparably linked with spiritual progress. (100:4.1, 100:4.2, 100:4.3)

The most practical passage of this section concerns love. When someone irritates you, the paper advises, try sympathetically to understand his viewpoint and the reasons behind his conduct. The picture of the cave dweller makes the point: a snarling man with a raised club, once you see the saber-toothed tiger before him and the woman and children behind him, becomes the beginning of the nobility of protecting one’s family. (100:4.4, 100:4.5)

V. Conversion and Mysticism — The River Bed Is Not the River

The world is full of souls lost in the sense of direction. The paper says that few have learned to put a philosophy of living in the place of religious authority, yet it adds that the symbols of socialized religion should not be despised, for though the river bed is not the river, it is a channel of growth. Growth leads from stagnation through conflict to coordination, and from the bondage of fear to the liberty of divine sonship. (100:5.1, 100:5.2)

Conversion may come gradually or suddenly at a moment of crisis. The paper cites both Paul on the Damascus road and Gautama Siddhartha seeking truth alone, and notes that many true believers have progressed without any sudden conversion. Most conversion phenomena are psychological, but when the mobilization of mind and the motivation of loyalty are complete, a truly spiritual experience can occur in which the indwelling spirit grasps the believer’s consecrated purpose. (100:5.3, 100:5.4)

But emotion alone is a false conversion; one must have faith as well as feeling. (100:5.5)

On mysticism the paper is cautious. Trancelike visionary states should never be cultivated as religious experience, and the very conditions that bring them on—fatigue, fasting, fear, wild dancing—reveal their danger. Much of what emerges from them comes from the subconscious mind. The better way is living faith, sincere worship, and unselfish prayer, and Jesus never resorted to such methods for communion with the Father. (100:5.7, 100:5.8, 100:5.10, 100:5.11)

The author also asks for consistency in how we picture the mind. If we accept a subconscious mind as a working hypothesis, we should also posit a corresponding superconscious level, the zone of contact with the Thought Adjuster. The danger in such speculation is that strange dreams and visions may be mistaken for divine communications. In the past, divine beings revealed themselves to God-knowing persons not because of their mystic trances but in spite of them. (100:5.6)

VI–VII. Marks of Religious Living; The Acme of Religious Living — The Balanced Personality

Religion is not a specific function of life but a mode of living. The outstanding mark of all religion is wholehearted devotion to supreme values, and a religion is genuine to the extent that the value it holds supreme is a real spiritual reality. The truly religious person is not socially isolated, keeps his sense of humor, and knows the peace that passes understanding. (100:6.1, 100:6.2, 100:6.5, 100:6.6)

Genuine religion takes nothing away from human existence, but it does add new meanings to all of life (100:6.5)

Evolutionary religion, being a genuine experience, also has its loyalty and grandeur. But revelatory religion adds a new quality, divine wisdom, and leads to a deeper consciousness of the Fatherhood of God and the brotherhood of man. The capacity to receive that wisdom is developed through experience in and with the human religions. (100:6.8, 100:6.9)

The final section finds the acme of religious living in the personality of Jesus. The average person cannot hope to reach his perfection, but anyone can develop a strong and unified personality along his lines. What was unique in the Master’s personality was not so much its perfection as its symmetry, its exquisitely balanced unification. (100:7.1)

He was not a man of sorrows but a soul of gladness, often saying “Be of good cheer.” He was indignant at sin but never angry at the sinner, never in a hurry, and respectful even of the faulty worship of his fellows. The most original of his teachings was the emphasis on love and mercy in place of fear and sacrifice. (100:7.5, 100:7.9, 100:7.13, 100:7.14, 100:7.16)

Jesus lived the truth as he taught it, free of hypocrisy and affectation. His heart embraced all mankind, and his invitation was always “Whosoever will, let him come.” As a man among men he trusted the Father as a little child trusts his parent, and apparent failure never shook him. (100:7.2, 100:7.6, 100:7.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 100 pictures religion not as a possession but as a growing life. We cannot cause the growth ourselves, but we can tend its soil, cultivate its habits, and refuse to run from its conflicts. At the end of that road stands the example of Jesus, who still unifies life, ennobles character, and simplifies experience today. (100:7.18)
