Introduction — A Mission in Twelve Groups

At Magadan Park nearly one hundred evangelists and disciples, including the women’s corps, were waiting to begin the Decapolis tour. On August 18 Jesus paired each apostle with one of the twelve evangelists and sent them out, with the other evangelists, in twelve groups. The tour was to last four weeks, and Jesus promised to visit them often during that time. (159:0.1, 159:0.2)

They labored in Gerasa, Gamala, Hippos, Gadara, Philadelphia, Scythopolis, and many other cities. The text states plainly that throughout this tour there were no miracles of healing or other extraordinary events. What the paper leaves us is not wonders but the teachings Jesus gave as he visited each group. (159:0.2)

I. The Sermon on Forgiveness — The Father Who Goes Out First

At Hippos Jesus taught forgiveness with the story of the shepherd who leaves the ninety and nine to search for the one sheep that has gone astray. He summed up in a single sentence the difference between the older religion and the gospel of the kingdom. (159:1.2)

In your religion God may receive repentant sinners; in the gospel of the kingdom the Father goes forth to find them even before they have seriously thought of repentance. (159:1.2)

Because the Father forgives us, we must learn to forgive one another. Jesus set out concrete steps to follow when a brother does wrong. The fellowship cannot judge souls or forgive sins, but it has been entrusted with maintaining temporal order on earth. (159:1.3)

Source 159:1.3
StepWhat to do
1Go to him and, with tact and patience, show him his fault between the two of you alone
2If he will not listen, go again, taking one or two mutual friends
3If he still refuses, tell the whole story to the congregation
4If he refuses to hear the brotherhood, let them take such action as they deem wise

When Peter asked whether forgiving seven times was enough, Jesus answered “seventy times and seven” and told the parable of the unforgiving steward: a man forgiven ten thousand talents threw into prison a fellow servant who owed him a hundred denarii. (159:1.4, 159:1.5)

Freely you have received the good things of the kingdom; therefore freely give to your fellows on earth. (159:1.5)

The text concludes that Jesus taught the dangers and unfairness of sitting in personal judgment on others. Legislative and judicial authority was vested in the group, not the individual, and even then was not to be exercised as personal authority, because an individual’s verdict is easily warped by prejudice or passion. For those of us who deal with conflict in a community today, this principle holds just as firmly. (159:1.6)

II. The Strange Preacher — Whoever Is Not Against Us

At Gamala John reported that he had forbidden a man who was teaching in Jesus’ name and casting out devils, because the man had never been with them. Jesus told him not to forbid such a one, and to rejoice that their teaching had begun to spread beyond the bounds of their personal influence. (159:2.1)

My son, in matters of this sort it would be better for you to reckon that he who is not against us is for us. (159:2.1)

John felt this contradicted Jesus’ earlier saying, “He who is not with me is against me.” The text explains that the earlier saying concerned an individual’s personal relation to the spiritual teachings of the kingdom, while this one concerned administrative jurisdiction among groups of believers. The man, whose name was Aden, ignored the prohibition, went on with his work, and raised up a considerable company of believers at Kanata. The lesson to rejoice in good work outside one’s own circle rather than to guard against it is all the more needed in a day of many denominations and organizations. (159:2.2, 159:2.4)

III. Instruction for Teachers and Believers — Respect the Personality

At Edrei Jesus taught the principles that should guide those who proclaim truth. The text presents them summarized and restated in modern phraseology. At their heart is respect for human personality and the conviction that spiritual victories are won only by spiritual power. (159:3.1, 159:3.2)

Source 159:3.2
AvoidDo instead
Coercing by overpowering argument, mental superiority, or shrewd eloquenceAppeal directly to the divine spirit dwelling within the mind
Appealing to fear, pity, or mere sentimentBe fair and restrained, respecting the personality of your pupils
Destroying self-respect; condemning only the wrongsGive generous recognition to what is praiseworthy
Sarcasm and cynicism toward the timidHelp them find work and escape idleness
Frightening people into the kingdomLead as a loving father does, without fear
False sympathy that feeds self-pityOffer sympathy to the brave

Jesus warned that strong feelings of emotion are not the same as the leadings of the divine spirit. Everyone who passes from the life of the flesh to the life of the spirit must cross a fringe of conflict, and all experience some uncertainty in that time of transition. Yet the gospel yoke is easy and the burden of truth is light. (159:3.6, 159:3.7)

The gospel yoke is easy and the burden of truth is light. (159:3.7)

The teaching on self-respect is finely balanced. Too much self-respect ends in pride, and the loss of it often ends in paralysis of the will. So the gospel restores self-respect to those who have lost it and restrains it in those who have it. This principle—build up a person’s dignity first when you hope to help him change—applies equally at home and in the classroom. (159:3.3)

Faith is to religion what sails are to a ship; it is an addition of power, not an added burden of life. (159:3.8)

He told them not to portray him as a man of sorrows. Those who accept this teaching are filled with joy, and the happiness of those who are certain about God keeps growing. He also said that faith does not make us immune to calamity but makes us unafraid when it comes. For those who mistake faith for a guarantee against trouble, this distinction is essential. (159:3.10, 159:3.13)

Increasing happiness is always the experience of all who are certain about God. (159:3.10)

Jesus asked them to make known that he is tender of his children’s feelings and patient with their frailties, but ruthless with sin and intolerant of iniquity. Gentleness and firmness are not at odds. This balance—generous toward people, uncompromising toward evil—is the pattern a teacher should follow. He added that a faith assured of the divine presence expands the mind, reinforces the personality, and enhances the power to love and be loved. (159:3.9, 159:3.12)

I do not promise to deliver you from the waters of adversity, but I do promise to go with you through all of them. (159:3.13)

IV. The Talk with Nathaniel — How to See the Scriptures

At Abila Nathaniel, troubled that Jesus seemed to detract from the authority of the Scriptures, took him aside to ask. Jesus asked him not yet to share the answer with his brethren, and said that he did not regard the Scriptures as the rabbis did. They are the work of men and reflect the views and enlightenment of the times in which they arose. (159:4.1, 159:4.2, 159:4.3)

Source 159:4.3

Most deplorableThe idea of absolute perfection and infallibility, and the misinterpretation of the scribes
nothing which human nature has touched can be regarded as infallible. (159:4.8)

Jesus said that the authority of truth is the very spirit dwelling in its living manifestations, not the dead words of men of another generation. He made no written record of the gospel lest, after he was gone, his followers divide into contending groups over differing interpretations. Nathaniel was enlightened but also shocked, and he told no one of this talk until after the ascension. To honor scripture without making an idol of it remains a task for every believer. (159:4.7, 159:4.11)

V. The Positive Nature of Jesus’ Religion — Overcome Evil with Good

At Philadelphia James asked how to choose the better passages of Scripture, and Jesus pointed to teachings about a clean heart, the Lord as shepherd, love of neighbor, the promise to fear not, and nations learning war no more. He took the best of the Jewish religion and set it in the gospel, making the fatherhood of God the cornerstone and the practice of the brotherhood of man imperative. (159:5.1, 159:5.7)

Jesus called for positive action instead of negative compliance. Turning the other cheek did not mean standing there dumb and passive, but actively doing the best thing possible to lead an erring brother into better ways. He taught three ways of contending with evil. (159:5.8, 159:5.9, 159:5.11)

Source 159:5.14
MethodCharacter
Return evil for evilPositive but unrighteous

the truly good is invariably more powerful than the most malignant evil. (159:5.10)

If forced to carry a stranger’s pack, do not sit and sigh while berating him under your breath; if nothing better comes to mind, carry it a second mile. Jesus turned the negative golden rule into a positive admonition, and through everyday words like salt, leaven, and little children he brought the philosophy of religion down from heaven to earth. His call to take the initiative and turn a situation toward good, rather than merely endure harm, offers a third way between passive resignation and retaliation. (159:5.15, 159:5.16, 159:5.17)

VI. The Return to Magadan — The Last Rest

The four-week mission was moderately successful, and hundreds were received into the kingdom. Above all, the apostles and evangelists gained the valuable experience of carrying on the work without Jesus’ immediate presence. On September 16 everyone gathered at Magadan, and David’s messengers brought reports from all over Palestine. (159:6.1, 159:6.2)

The text singles out the value of the messenger corps. They kept the believers in touch with each other and with Jesus, and in these dark days they collected funds to support the families of the apostles and evangelists. David maintained an overnight relay service between Jerusalem and Bethsaida. The week that followed was their last rest, for the Perean mission would become an unbroken campaign of preaching and teaching all the way to Jerusalem. That the labor of unnamed helpers behind the scenes sustains a whole community is just as true of our gatherings today. (159:6.3, 159:6.4, 159:6.5)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 159 contains not a single miracle. Instead it is full of mature teaching on forgiveness, tolerance, the attitude of a teacher, how to read scripture, and how to resist evil. Jesus’ religion was not a list of things not to do but an invitation to do good actively. (159:5.8)
