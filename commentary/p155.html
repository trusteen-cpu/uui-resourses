The Urantia Book · Paper 155

Fleeing Through Northern Galilee

Two discourses on religion given on the road of flight: the heart of this paper is the contrast between the religion of the mind, resting on tradition’s authority, and the religion of the spirit, grounded in personal experience.

Introduction — The Road of Flight Becomes a School

After leaving Capernaum, Jesus and the twenty-four landed near Kheresa and spent their first night in a park south of Bethsaida-Julias. That night Jesus discussed with them their projected tour through Batanea and northern Galilee to the Phoenician coast. This paper follows the first three weeks of that journey, centering on the two discourses on religion he gave near Luz. (155:0.1, 155:4.1)

Outwardly it was a journey of men being hunted, yet the text does not write this period as a record of failure. They made few converts, but the apostles and evangelists learned much from their daily conversations with the Master. Where outward results had disappeared, the essence of religion came up for question once again. (155:3.2)

I. Why Do the Heathen Rage? — A Kingdom of Love

Jesus quoted the psalm in which the rulers take counsel against the Lord and his anointed, and said that its words were being fulfilled before their eyes. But the rest of the Psalmist’s picture—the wrathful Son—would not be fulfilled, for the Psalmist had held mistaken ideas about the Son of Man and his mission. (155:1.1, 155:1.2)

My kingdom is founded on love, proclaimed in mercy, and established by unselfish service. (155:1.2)

Jesus even pointed out something the disciples could learn from their enemies. Their outlook was narrow, but for that very reason they concentrated all their energies on their goal. The disciples, by contrast, were caught up in regretting the past, whining over the present, and vainly hoping for the future. (155:1.3)

Why do the heathen rage? Because they know not the truth. Why do you languish in futile yearning? Because you obey not the truth. (155:1.3)

He also warned against narrowness and overspecialization. The Greeks with science but no religion and the Jews with religion but no science are both left with fragments of truth. His saying that people will seek us out if we live a truth-coordinated life means that we should first test the success of our witness in our own lives. (155:1.4, 155:1.5)

The measure wherewith truth seekers are drawn to you represents the measure of your truth endowment, your righteousness. (155:1.5)

II–III. Chorazin and Caesarea-Philippi — A Baptism of Adversity

Peter and the twelve evangelists spent two weeks in Chorazin, while Jesus and the eleven apostles spent two weeks at Caesarea-Philippi. No city in all Galilee yielded so few souls as Chorazin, and for the evangelists those two weeks were the hardest and most unproductive time of their careers. Yet that very lack made each of them take stock more honestly of his own soul’s progress. (155:2.1, 155:2.2)

Source 155:2.3

What the apostles learned at Caesarea prepared them for the discourses later in the paper. When truth is crystallized into a creed, it becomes a boundary line of self-righteousness instead of a signpost, and it fossilizes. Many souls are best led to love the unseen God by first learning to love the brethren they can see. And a faith resting only on material longings will crumble as knowledge of nature grows, whereas a spiritual religion can never be disturbed by the progress of physical science. (155:3.3, 155:3.4, 155:3.6)

true religion was man’s heartfelt loyalty to his highest and truest convictions. (155:3.5)
His whole earth life was consistently devoted to the mission of thawing out the frozen forms of religion into the liquid liberties of enlightened sonship. (155:3.8)

IV–V. On the Way to Phoenicia; The Discourse on True Religion

As they stopped for lunch in the shade of a rock ledge near Luz, Peter asked why they were fleeing instead of confronting their enemies, and Thomas asked what the real difference was between their religion and that of the Jerusalem leaders. Jesus said it would help them all more if he answered Thomas’s question. The discourse that followed is given in summary and restated in modern phraseology. (155:4.2, 155:5.1)

Source 155:5.2
Three forms of religious devotionCharacterBasis
Primitive religionFear of mysterious energies and worship of superior forces — the religion of the physical nature, the religion of fearA seminatural and instinctive urge
The religion of civilizationAdvancing religious concepts and practices — the religion of the mind, intellectual theologyThe authority of established religious tradition
True religion — the religion of revelationRevelation of supernatural values, a glimpse of the Father’s goodness and beauty — the religion of the spiritDemonstrated in human experience

Jesus refused to belittle the primitive forms, though he deplored how much of them persisted even in the religions of the more intelligent races. The religion of authority offers a ready refuge to a soul harassed by fear and uncertainty, and asks in return only passive intellectual assent. But those who cast their lot in that way give up the supreme adventure of human existence: man seeking God, for himself and as himself, and finding him. (155:5.6, 155:5.9, 155:5.10)

The religion of the spirit means effort, struggle, conflict, faith, determination, love, loyalty, and progress. (155:5.11)

Jesus described the religion of the Jerusalem leaders as an established system of intellectual beliefs built from many doctrines—a religion of authority. The new religion they were about to proclaim would appeal instead to the Father’s spirit dwelling in the mind of man, and would draw its authority from the fruits that appear in the personal experience of those who accept its truths. The question of where religious authority comes from runs through the whole discourse. (155:5.12)

Calling each of the twenty-four by name, Jesus asked which of them would choose the easy path of a fossilized religion over the way of the spirit with its persecutions. All of them rose to answer, but he raised his hand and stopped them. Because this was one of the few emotional appeals he ever made, he told them to find an unemotional answer before the Father. (155:5.13, 155:5.14)

Go now apart by yourselves, each man alone with the Father (155:5.14)

When they reassembled, Jesus only told them to pray that their emotions would be transformed into higher loyalties and experiences of the spirit. As they walked on, the talk among them ripened, and at about three o’clock Peter begged him to say more. His way of giving the emotional moment time to mature, rather than turning it at once into a decision, was itself a lesson. (155:5.15, 155:5.16)

VI. The Second Discourse on Religion — Find God for Yourself

At Peter’s request Jesus taught again in the shade of a hillside. The Father did not stop speaking words of truth when the old prophets ended their utterances, he said; so do not call divine what is wholly human, and do not fail to discern truth that comes from outside the traditional channels. He urged them not merely to quote the prophets but to become living prophets themselves. (155:6.2, 155:6.7)

aspire to become living prophets of the Most High and spiritual heroes of the coming kingdom. (155:6.7)

He called them to be born again, born of the spirit. That means coming out of the darkness of authority and the lethargy of tradition to discover God for oneself, in oneself, as a fact of one’s own experience. To move from a racial faith inherited to a personal faith achieved by actual experience—that is the progress this passage describes. (155:6.3, 155:6.4)

Jesus rebuked the false teachers who drag hungry souls back into the distant past and leave them there. Such people are frightened by every new discovery and discomfited by every new revelation. The prophet who knew perfect peace with his mind stayed on God was not someone who talked about God but someone who had found him. (155:6.6)

Yet Jesus warned them not to look down on Israel. From Moses to Amos and Hosea, their persistent search had prepared the way for the greater revelation of the Father. To choose the religion of the spirit is not to despise tradition but to go beyond it. (155:6.10)

There is only one adventure more thrilling than discovering God’s will, and that is honestly trying to do it. That will can be done in any occupation, and all things are sacred in the lives of those who are spirit led. Jesus commended not the mental immaturity of a child but the spiritual simplicity of an easy-believing, fully trusting little one. (155:6.11, 155:6.12)

Some callings are not holy and others secular. (155:6.11)
It is not so important that you should know about the fact of God as that you should increasingly grow in the ability to feel the presence of God. (155:6.12)

Jesus told them not to try to prove to others that they had found God; such proof cannot be produced consciously. There are, however, two positive demonstrations that a person knows God. (155:6.14)

Source 155:6.15

Now, mistake not, my Father will ever respond to the faintest flicker of faith. (155:6.17)

The Father honors even the superstitious emotions of primitive man and the feeble attempts of fearful souls whose faith never goes beyond intellectual assent to authority. But those called out of darkness into light are expected to believe with a whole heart—body, mind, and spirit. Religion must not be a theological shelter to flee into, but an experience that testifies that God has found us and lifted us up. (155:6.17, 155:6.18)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 155 offers one of the clearest statements in The Urantia Book of what religion is. Jesus calls us to choose the adventure that experience demands over the security that authority provides, yet he asks us to make that choice not in the heat of emotion but in quiet decision before the Father. His teaching that we should seek unity of spirit feeling rather than uniformity of belief is especially practical for readers today who live among many different convictions. (155:5.14, 155:6.9)
