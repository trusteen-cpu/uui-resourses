Introduction — A Thursday Morning Farewell

Jesus planned this Thursday as his last free day on earth as a divine Son incarnated in the flesh. After breakfast he led the apostles and a few loyal disciples to a secluded spot above the camp and taught them many new truths. This forenoon talk was his farewell address to the whole camp group, both Jews and gentiles. (178:0.1)

Only Judas was missing. Some of the apostles supposed the Master had sent him into the city on an errand, probably to arrange the Passover. He did not return until midafternoon. The paper falls into three parts: in the morning, the discourse on citizenship; in the afternoon, the preparation of the supper and David Zebedee’s quiet activity; and toward evening, the last words on the way down into the city. (178:0.1)

I. Sonship and Citizenship (1) — Living in Two Kingdoms at Once

For almost two hours Jesus talked with about fifty of his trusted followers and answered a score of questions about the relation of the kingdom of heaven to the kingdoms of this world. The narrator summarizes the discourse in modern language. Its core is this: the kingdoms of this world are material and may need physical force, but the kingdom of heaven is a spiritual brotherhood of spirit-born sons and may be promulgated only by the power of the spirit. (178:1.1, 178:1.2)

Jesus said there is nothing incompatible between the two requirements. Conflict arises only if a Caesar usurps the prerogatives of God and demands supreme worship. Even then, the believer should worship only God while seeking to enlighten such misguided rulers. Sonship in the kingdom should instead make one a better citizen, and the believer, besides his duty to man and his duty to God, voluntarily assumes a third obligation: service to the brotherhood of believers. (178:1.3, 178:1.4, 178:1.5)

The fruits of the spirit, your sincere and loving service, are the mighty social lever to uplift the races of darkness, and this Spirit of Truth will become your power-multiplying fulcrum. (178:1.6)

He urged them to show wisdom and sagacity in dealing with unbelieving rulers: to iron out minor disagreements with discretion and, in everything short of their spiritual allegiance to the rulers of the universe, to live peaceably with all men. He also foretold that while rulers acted as religious dictators, believers could expect persecution, yet the light they bore and the very manner of their suffering would eventually bring about the gradual divorcement of politics and religion. (178:1.7, 178:1.9)

Be you always as wise as serpents but as harmless as doves. (178:1.7)
Learn to be faithful to the kingdom even in times of peace and prosperity. (178:1.10)

This warning is surprisingly sharp. More dangerous than times of persecution are the times when most men speak well of believers and many in high places nominally accept the gospel. Believers are to keep watch on their own, so that the angels need not lead their ease-drifting souls into troublous ways. For today’s reader, the passage raises the question whether comfort is dulling one’s faith. (178:1.10)

I. Sonship and Citizenship (2) — Preaching the Gospel and Serving

Jesus told them never to let anything divert their devotion to the one duty of preaching the gospel. Loving spiritual ministry, enlightening intellectual communion, and uplifting social service should overflow to all mankind, but none of these, nor all of them together, may take the place of proclaiming the gospel. Such ministries are the social by-products of the more sublime transformation wrought in the believer’s heart. (178:1.11)

Whatsoever you would that men should do to you, do even so to them. (178:1.12)

He told them to persuade, never to compel, and never to promote truth through the power of civil government or secular law. A believer in public service must not be a poorer civil servant than an unbeliever; if he is, he should ask whether the roots of truth in his heart have died. Jesus then set side by side what believers must not be and what they should be. (178:1.12, 178:1.13)

Underneath this teaching lies the consciousness of sonship with God. Jesus said that this consciousness should quicken the entire life service of every man, woman, and child. Faith is not meant to make people neglect their work and public duties; it should be a mighty stimulus that awakens the powers lying within the personality. Since faithfulness at work is itself a fruit of faith, this teaching applies directly to any ordinary working person. (178:1.13)

After the leaven and the mustard seed, Jesus compared the gospel to the seed of the living being, which remains the same seed while it unfolds in new manifestations from generation to generation, adapting to the needs of each age. He did not want the gospel to become merely a sacred memory or tradition. He also told the apostles not to attack the old ways, but skillfully to put the leaven of new truth in the midst of the old beliefs. (178:1.15, 178:1.16)

Jesus recalled that he and the apostles had never directly attacked the persons or the authority of those who sit in Moses’ seat. They had only offered new light, and they clashed with the leaders only when those leaders blocked the preaching of the gospel. Even now, it was the leaders who were the attackers. Yet when the willful unbeliever attacks, they were to defend vigorously the truth that had saved them. Here gentleness and courage go together in balance. (178:1.16)

From generation to generation this gospel must show increasing vitality and exhibit greater depth of spiritual power. (178:1.15)
Show yourselves to be loyal citizens, upright artisans, praiseworthy neighbors, devoted kinsmen, understanding parents, and sincere believers in the brotherhood of the Father’s kingdom. (178:1.17)

II. After the Noontide Meal — An Upper Room Quietly Prepared

Not many took in the forenoon address; the Greeks understood it best. Even the apostles could not reconcile the Master’s references to future political kingdoms and successive generations of believers with his impending death. By noon everyone had heard of Lazarus’s hasty flight from Bethany. (178:2.1, 178:2.2)

Through his secret agents, David Zebedee knew all about the plot, including Judas’s part in it. When he began to ask the Master whether he knew, Jesus raised his hand and stopped him: he knew all about it. David was to tell no one and was not to doubt that the will of God would prevail in the end. When word came asking whether Abner of Philadelphia should come to Jerusalem, Jesus sent back the message to go on with his work. (178:2.3, 178:2.4)

If I depart from you in the flesh, it is only that I may return in the spirit. (178:2.4)

When Philip asked about the Passover, Jesus called Peter and John. As Judas drew near to overhear, David engaged him in conversation and kept him occupied. Jesus told the three to follow a man bearing a water pitcher whom they would meet at the city gate. All this had been arranged with John Mark the day before in the hills, because if Judas learned the place in advance, the supper might be disturbed. That same day Judas turned over all the apostolic money to David. (178:2.5, 178:2.6, 178:2.9, 178:2.10)

III. On the Way to the Supper — Looking Down on the City

About half past four Jesus set out with the twelve. This was the last journey he ever made with all twelve of them. When they reached the place where he had spoken the evening before about the destruction of Jerusalem, they unconsciously paused and looked down on the city. Since Jesus did not wish to pass through the city until after sunset, he spoke his last words to them there. (178:2.12, 178:3.1)

As the Father sent me into this world, so am I about to send you forth to represent me and finish the work I have begun. (178:3.2)

These words carry the weight of the past years. The apostles had been witnesses of the experience of being laborers together with God, and Jesus had long warned them that he must leave. He had set them apart in the hills of Capernaum precisely so that they could carry on the work after he was gone. Now they had to make ready to share with others the experience they had had with him. (178:3.2)

I need no defense by the hand of man; the armies of heaven are even now near at hand (178:3.3)

He told them not to expose themselves needlessly to peril by any foolish attempt to defend him. Just as Lazarus had been sent away to safety, the apostles must live to witness to the gospel. And when they saw the city destroyed, they were not to forget that they had already entered upon the eternal life of endless service. In the Father’s universe there are many abodes, and when they had finished their work on earth, they too would come to the Master’s joy. (178:3.3, 178:3.4)

As they entered the city through the narrow streets in the gathering darkness, no one in the crowds recognized that the Son of God was passing by. Only three of the apostles knew where they were going, and none of them knew that one of their own number had already entered into a conspiracy to betray the Master. John Mark followed them to the gate, then ran ahead by another street to welcome them at his home. (178:3.5, 178:3.6)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 178 shows the teaching of Jesus on the eve of his death reaching beyond personal salvation to the life of the believer in society and the state. Jesus did not tell believers to turn their backs on the world, nor to spread their faith by borrowing worldly power. Instead he showed how a person living by the spirit becomes the best citizen, neighbor, and parent. At the same time, with danger approaching, he calmly prepared the supper and trusted the Father’s will. (178:1.8, 178:2.3)
