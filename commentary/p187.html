Introduction — A Life Laid Down Freely

When the two brigands had been made ready, twelve soldiers under a centurion set out for the place of execution. This centurion was the very captain who had arrested Jesus in Gethsemane the night before. The two brigands were scourged according to custom, but the captain judged that Jesus had already suffered enough and ordered no further flogging. Because the two brigands were associates of Barabbas, the text observes that Jesus was crucified in Barabbas’s place. (187:0.1, 187:0.2)

The narrator first makes clear that this death was a matter of Jesus’ own free will. In foretelling it, he had said that no one would take his life from him; he would lay it down of himself. (187:0.3)

No one takes my life away from me—I lay it down of myself. (187:0.3)

Just before nine in the morning the procession left the governor’s headquarters. Among the crowd of some two hundred were a few secret sympathizers, but most were enemies or onlookers. Very few of the leaders went out to watch the execution; most of them were in the temple, discussing what to do about his followers. (187:0.4)

I. On the Way to Golgotha — The Daughters of Jerusalem and Simon of Cyrene

As was the custom, Jesus carried only the crossbeam on his shoulders. The captain walked ahead carrying the boards that named each man’s crime; on Jesus’ board Pilate had written in Latin, Greek, and Aramaic, “Jesus of Nazareth—the King of the Jews.” The authorities begged him to change it, but Pilate refused: “What I have written, I have written.” (187:1.1, 187:1.2, 187:1.3)

The text also records that crucifixion was not a Jewish mode of punishment. The Greeks and Romans had learned it from the Phoenicians, and it was never used on Roman citizens, only on slaves and subject peoples. The procession went by the most direct route out through the Damascus gate to Golgotha. (187:1.4, 187:1.5)

In the narrow streets, tenderhearted women who had known Jesus’ comfort and love wept and lamented aloud. Since it was against the law to show sympathy for one condemned, they were brave indeed. Jesus was grateful for their sympathy, but he did not want them to draw the anger of the authorities, and he thought less of himself than of the troubles about to fall on Jerusalem. (187:1.6, 187:1.7)

Daughters of Jerusalem, weep not for me, but rather weep for yourselves and for your children. (187:1.6)

Jesus had neither eaten, drunk, nor slept since the Last Supper, and soon after passing the gate he fell under the weight of the crossbeam. The captain halted the soldiers and made Simon of Cyrene, who happened to be passing, carry the beam. Simon stayed through all those hours, and after the resurrection he became a valiant believer and led his family into the kingdom. Yet he never knew that the man whose burden he bore was the Jewish tutor who had once befriended his injured son. (187:1.8, 187:1.9, 187:1.10)

This record of a burden taken up by chance that changed a man and his whole family has a quiet power. Simon’s two sons, Alexander and Rufus, became effective teachers of the new gospel in Africa. A small kindness Jesus showed long before and the crossbeam Simon carried that day were joined, though neither man knew it. (187:1.10)

II. The Crucifixion — “Father, Forgive Them”

The soldiers bound Jesus’ arms to the crossbeam and nailed his hands and feet. The cross was not high; his feet were only about three feet from the ground, so he could see and hear every mocking word and face. There was considerable feeling against crucifixion in Jerusalem, and a society of Jewish women always sent someone with drugged wine to lessen the victim’s suffering. Jesus tasted it, but refused to drink. (187:2.1, 187:2.3)

The Master chose to retain his human consciousness until the very end. (187:2.3)

While the two brigands cursed their executioners, the only words Jesus spoke as he was nailed to the crossbeam were a plea for those who were nailing him. The text explains that a lifetime of unselfish service was the mainspring of that plea. (187:2.4)

The ideas, motives, and longings of a lifetime are openly revealed in a crisis. (187:2.4)

The soldiers divided his clothes as was customary and cast lots for the seamless robe. The text says this was just as well: had his followers obtained his garments, they would have been tempted toward the worship of relics. Jesus wanted to leave behind nothing material, only the memory of a life dedicated to doing the Father’s will. (187:2.8, 187:2.9)

III. Those Who Saw the Crucifixion — Mockery and Silence

The cross was raised about half past nine, and before eleven more than a thousand people had gathered. The unseen hosts of a universe watched in silence as the Creator underwent the most dishonorable of deaths at the hands of his creatures. Near the cross stood Mary his mother, Ruth, Jude, John, Salome, Mary Magdalene, and a group of earnest women believers. (187:3.1, 187:3.2)

Passers-by and some of the rulers jeered that he had saved others but could not save himself. When Jesus made no reply, most of them went away, and the soldiers drank their sour wine and offered him mock toasts. When Jesus said, “I thirst,” the captain soaked a sponge in wine and moistened his lips. (187:3.3, 187:3.4, 187:3.5)

He had lived as a man, and he would die as a man—doing the Father’s will. (187:3.6)

IV. The Thief on the Cross — Faith at the Last Moment

When one brigand railed at Jesus, the other thief, who had many times heard the Master teach, rebuked him. Jesus turned and smiled at him approvingly, and the man fanned the flickering flame of his faith and asked to be remembered when Jesus came into his kingdom. (187:4.1)

Verily, verily, I say to you today, you shall sometime be with me in Paradise. (187:4.1)

This young man had been led into a life of violence by those who praised robbery as a patriotic protest against injustice, and he had looked on Barabbas as a hero. Now he saw a true hero on the cross beside him. The text adds that anyone in the jeering crowd who had appealed to Jesus in faith would have been received with the same love. The door of salvation does not close even at the last moment. (187:4.2, 187:4.5, 187:4.6)

And if any other person among the jeering crowd had experienced the birth of faith within his soul and had appealed to the mercy of Jesus, he would have been received with the same loving consideration that was displayed toward the believing brigand. (187:4.6)

About noon, when John came back with Jesus’ mother and the women believers, Jesus entrusted his mother and John to each other and asked them to leave the scene. Mary spent the rest of her life in John’s home at Bethsaida. Even in his extreme suffering, Jesus did not forget to care for his family. (187:4.7)

V. Last Hour on the Cross — “It Is Finished”

After noon a sandstorm blowing in from the Arabian desert darkened the sky. Shortly after one o’clock Jesus began to lose human consciousness, and his human mind repeated passages from the Psalms. The words people heard — “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” — were the opening line of the twenty-second Psalm, which he was reciting. (187:5.1, 187:5.2)

This passage corrects an old misunderstanding. That cry was not the despair of one abandoned. Jesus never for a moment doubted that he had lived according to the Father’s will and was now laying down his life according to that will. As his consciousness faded, what held his mind were the scriptures he had learned since childhood. (187:5.2)

He did not feel that the Father had forsaken him (187:5.2)

Seeing how Jesus died, the Roman centurion said that this was indeed a righteous man, and from that time he began to believe in Jesus. The text sums it up: Jesus died as royally as he had lived. He had provided for the safety of his apostles, restrained Peter’s violence, arranged for John to be near him, and at the last handed over his spirit to the Father. (187:5.5, 187:5.6)

Jesus died royally—as he had lived. (187:5.6)

Because it was the day of preparation, the authorities asked Pilate to have the victims’ legs broken so the bodies could be taken down. Finding Jesus already dead, the soldiers pierced his side with a spear to make sure. A death that often took days came in less than five and a half hours, because of overwhelming emotional agony and spiritual anguish. (187:5.7, 187:5.8)

VI. After the Crucifixion — The Close of a Day of Sorrow

About half past three David Zebedee sent out the last messengers announcing the Master’s death. John sent the women to the home of Elijah Mark and stayed at Golgotha until Joseph and Nicodemus came with the order to take the body. So ended a tragic day in which a vast universe shuddered at the indifference and perversity of mortals. (187:6.1, 187:6.2, 187:6.3)

Fewer than thirty people were present when the Master died: thirteen Roman soldiers and about fifteen believers, all of them women except Jude and John. In an hour when so many had scattered, the faithfulness of those who stayed to the end remains a shaft of light in this sorrowful record. (187:5.1, 187:5.4)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 187 focuses less on the description of suffering than on the character revealed in suffering. Jesus refused the wine that would dull his consciousness, forgave his executioners, received the repentant brigand, and cared for his mother. What came to light in that crisis were the motives and the love of a whole lifetime. (187:2.4, 187:5.6)
