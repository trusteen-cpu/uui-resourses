Introduction — A Gospel Reflecting His Teachings More Than His Personality

From February 11 to 20, Jesus and the twelve toured the cities and villages of northern Perea where Abner’s associates and the women’s corps were at work. They found the messengers succeeding, and Jesus repeatedly called the apostles’ attention to the fact that the gospel of the kingdom could spread without miracles and wonders. (166:0.1)

The text says that this three-month mission succeeded with little help from the twelve, and that from this time on the gospel reflected his teachings more than his personality. But his followers did not keep to this direction for long. After his death and resurrection they built the early church around miraculous concepts and glorified memories of his personality. (166:0.2)

the gospel from this time on reflected, not so much Jesus’ personality, as his teachings. (166:0.2)

I. The Pharisees at Ragaba — The Outside and the Inside of the Cup

On the Sabbath of February 18, Nathaniel, a wealthy Pharisee of Ragaba, invited Jesus as guest of honor to a breakfast together with some twenty fellow Pharisees who had been following the tour. Jesus sat down at once without washing his hands at the basin. He washed only for cleanliness and greatly disliked purely ceremonial acts. The host was shocked, and those across the table sneered and twisted their mouths. (166:1.1, 166:1.2, 166:1.3)

Jesus observed that they had invited him in order to display their ceremonial devotion to their own righteousness, and then he rebuked a religion whose outside and inside did not match. He first acknowledged that many Pharisees were his friends and even his disciples, but he made it plain that God looks not at outward piety but at the inner motives of the soul. (166:1.3, 166:1.4)

How carefully you cleanse the outside of the cups and the platters while the spiritual-food vessels are filthy and polluted! (166:1.4)

When a lawyer complained that these words reproached his class too, Jesus rebuked the lawyers who load heavy burdens on people without lifting a finger and take away the key of knowledge. Yet he said that the doors of the kingdom of heaven are open to all who have faith and will not be shut by the prejudice and arrogance of false teachers. He left the house without eating. (166:1.5, 166:1.6)

But you cannot thus shut up the doors of the kingdom of heaven (166:1.5)

Some of the Pharisees who heard him believed and entered the kingdom, but more of them hardened their resolve to catch him in words they could use against him. The same words became a door to some and a stumbling block to others. (166:1.6)

II. The Ten Lepers — A Stranger’s Gratitude

Near Amathus, Jesus met ten lepers. Nine were Jews and one a Samaritan; their common suffering had carried them past religious prejudice. Jesus happened to be explaining why the gentiles and less orthodox Jews of Perea were more ready to believe the gospel, yet the apostles were still reluctant to feel kindly toward Samaritans. (166:2.1, 166:2.2)

Simon Zelotes suggested they pass by, since a Samaritan was among them. Jesus answered that the Samaritan might prove more grateful than the Jews, and asked whether they should sit in judgment on their fellows. Then he told the ten to go and show themselves to the priests, and on the way they were all made whole. (166:2.3, 166:2.4)

Should we sit in judgment on our fellow men? (166:2.3)
Where, then, are the other nine, the Jews? Only one, this alien, has returned to give glory to God. (166:2.5)

As the Samaritan left, all the apostles looked at Jesus except Simon Zelotes, who hung his head. No one spoke, and Jesus said nothing; there was no need. The text adds that only four of the ten actually had leprosy, the other six having a skin disease, and that the Samaritan was a true leper. (166:2.6, 166:2.7)

Jesus explained the event as the difference between the children of the house and the stranger. The children take their blessings for granted and neglect to give thanks, while the stranger, filled with wonder at the gift, cannot help being grateful. The insight that gratitude dulls the longer grace is received speaks with special sharpness to those who live amid long habits of faith. (166:2.8)

III. The Sermon at Gerasa — The Narrow Door Is Wide Enough for All

Asked whether few or many would be saved, Jesus first reviewed the ideas then current: the teaching that only the children of Abraham would be saved, the notion that just as few of those who left Egypt reached the promised land, so only a few would enter the kingdom, and the proverb of the narrow door and the broad way. He granted that the proverb held truth, but he gave it a new interpretation. (166:3.1, 166:3.2, 166:3.3)

Even if the door to the way of life is narrow, it is wide enough to admit all who sincerely seek to enter, for I am that door. (166:3.3)

The danger lies not in the size of the door but in delay. For those who pursue immature pleasures and selfishness, refusing to enter the spiritual experience, and then knock later to be counted among the great when the glory is revealed, the door is shut. But all who sincerely seek will surely find eternal salvation, and people will come from every direction to possess the kingdom by living faith. (166:3.4, 166:3.5)

Source 166:3.7

The narrator calls this a truly new and strange interpretation of the old proverb about the straight and narrow way. The apostles were slowly grasping the meaning of the early declaration that one must be born again, yet they still did not fully understand the teaching that spiritual power must be used to overcome the world’s obstacles. The door stands open, but walking through it takes will and spiritual decision. (166:3.6, 166:3.7, 166:3.8)

I am the door, I am the new and living way, and whosoever wills may enter to embark upon the endless truth-search for eternal life. (166:3.7)

IV. Teaching about Accidents — Health Is Not the Smile of Heaven

At lunch on the way to Philadelphia, Thomas asked whether spirit beings take part in strange events of the material world, and whether angels can prevent accidents. Jesus asked whether he had not noticed that he had consistently refused to use heavenly powers for his personal sustenance. Then he declared it a superstition to regard prosperity as a sign of divine approval and adversity as a sign of God’s displeasure. (166:4.1, 166:4.2, 166:4.3)

He pointed to the Galileans killed by Pilate and the eighteen killed when the tower of Siloam fell. They were not greater sinners than others; they were innocent victims of the accidents of time. Then he divided the events of life into three kinds. (166:4.4, 166:4.5)

Source 166:4.6
Kind of eventThe text’s explanation
1. Ordinary happeningsPart of living on earth with one’s fellows
2. Accidents of nature and human misfortunesNot prearranged or brought about by spiritual forces
3. The results of effort that follows natural lawFollow the laws of fruitfulness and you reap a harvest — the parable of the fig tree
health is not the smile of heaven, neither is affliction the frown of God. (166:4.10)

To illustrate the third kind, Jesus told the parable of the fig tree. A tree that had borne no fruit for three seasons was about to be cut down, but the gardener asked for one more year to dig around it and fertilize it. When they followed the laws of fruitfulness, the tree yielded an abundant harvest. The result came not from a miracle but from work that respected the law. (166:4.9)

The Father bestows material things without discrimination, but spiritual gifts are limited only by the capacity of the one receiving them — by faith and by willingness to abide by the Father’s will. The apostles did not fully grasp this teaching. The text remarks that one hour of teaching cannot wholly change the beliefs of a lifetime, which is why Jesus repeated these things many times. (166:4.11, 166:4.12)

Although the Father is no respecter of persons, in the bestowal of spiritual gifts he is limited by man’s faith and by his willingness always to abide by the Father’s will. (166:4.11)

This teaching gives a twofold comfort to those who suffer. It frees them from the guilt of thinking misfortune is God’s punishment, and it restores the value of faithful effort that respects natural law. God’s care shows itself less in preventing accidents than in giving spiritual gifts to the human soul. (166:4.4, 166:4.11)

V. The Congregation at Philadelphia — Abner’s Solitary Faithfulness

Of all the cities of Perea, Philadelphia saw the largest number enter the kingdom. Its synagogue had never been under the supervision of the Sanhedrin and so was never closed, and Abner taught there three times a day. This synagogue later became a church, the headquarters for missions to the east, and for centuries remained a center of Christian learning. (166:5.1, 166:5.2)

After Jesus’ death the church at Philadelphia came into serious conflict with the Jerusalem church under James. The text explains that because of this discord, the gospel records preserve nothing about Abner and his work. Abner broke with Peter and James over administrative questions and with Paul over differences of philosophy and theology. (166:5.3, 166:5.4)

Source 166:5.4
With whom he brokeWhy

What he nonetheless supportedPaul’s mission to the gentiles; he sided with Paul in his dispute with the Jerusalem church

He lived an isolated life, yet during his later years and for some time after, the believers at Philadelphia held more strictly than any other group on earth to the religion of Jesus as he lived and taught it. Abner remained a faithful believer in and teacher of the gospel of the kingdom until he died in A.D. 74 at the age of eighty-nine. (166:5.5, 166:5.6, 166:5.7)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

What runs through Paper 166 is the distinction between appearance and reality. Washing the hands is not purity of soul, descent is no qualification for salvation, prosperity is not God’s approval, and illness is not God’s frown. Jesus stripped away these illusions one by one and led people to the realities of faith, gratitude, and devotion to the Father’s will. (166:1.4, 166:3.3, 166:4.10)
