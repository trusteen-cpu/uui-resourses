Introduction — One More Opportunity

While the camp at Pella was being set up, Jesus went up secretly to the feast of dedication, taking only Nathaniel and Thomas with him. Not until they had crossed the Jordan did the two apostles realize where he was going, and they used every argument to dissuade him, pointing to the folly and danger of placing himself in the hands of the Sanhedrin. His reply never changed. (164:0.1)

I would give these teachers in Israel another opportunity to see the light, before my hour comes. (164:0.1)

If the visit at the feast of tabernacles had been for pilgrims from all over the empire, this visit had a single purpose: to give the Sanhedrin and the Jewish leaders another chance to see the light. Though he expected most of them would not accept the gospel, he gave every one of them one more opportunity to choose. This paper shows a love that does not close the door even on those it expects to refuse. (164:2.1, 164:2.3)

I. Story of the Good Samaritan — Who Proved to Be the Neighbor?

One evening while they were staying at Jericho, a lawyer asked how to inherit eternal life. Jesus had him state the law’s two commandments of love himself, and answered that if he truly did these things, he would attain eternal life. Then the lawyer asked who his neighbor was. It was a trap meant to draw from Jesus a statement contrary to Jewish law, which limited one’s neighbor to one’s own people. (164:1.1, 164:1.2)

Instead of falling into the trap, Jesus told a story any Jericho audience would fully appreciate. A priest and a Levite passed by a man left half dead by robbers, while a Samaritan bound up his wounds, brought him to an inn, and even paid for his care. (164:1.3)

Source 164:1.3
Who came byWhat he didMeaning in the story
The priestSaw his plight and passed by on the other side of the roadA religious office does not make one a neighbor
The LeviteSaw the man and passed by on the other sideKnowing the law is not the same as mercy
The SamaritanMoved with compassion, bound up his wounds and cared for him at the innThe despised one proved to be the true neighbor

The lawyer, unwilling even to say the word “Samaritan,” answered, “He who showed mercy on him.” So he himself gave the answer that, spoken by Jesus, would have furnished grounds for a charge of heresy. The text judges the story a beautiful admonition to Jesus’ followers and a stunning rebuke to the Jews for their attitude toward the Samaritans. (164:1.3, 164:1.4)

Go and do likewise. (164:1.3)

This short story moved the definition of neighbor from the boundaries of blood and religion to the realm of deeds. The text adds that ever since, it has fostered brotherly love among all who have believed Jesus’ gospel. While the questioner was weighing who qualified as a neighbor, the story asked the listener to become one. (164:1.4)

II. At Jerusalem — The Gathering at Nicodemus’s House

The chief event of this visit took place on Friday night at the home of Nicodemus. About twenty-five Jewish leaders who believed in Jesus gathered there, fourteen of them present or recent members of the Sanhedrin. Eber, Matadormus, and Joseph of Arimathea were among them. (164:2.1)

Before this learned audience Jesus displayed, for the first time since his teaching in Alexandria and Rome, his broad learning and insight into human affairs. They proposed ways to win over the rest of the Sanhedrin, but Jesus only listened in silence. To the suggestion that they stand together before the seventy, he replied that this would only bring wrath on their heads, and told them each to work as the spirit led. (164:2.2, 164:2.3, 164:2.4)

When the meeting ended, all present sensed something mysterious in the Master’s personality, were charmed by his gracious manner, and came to love him. Yet as Jesus went to the Mount of Olives for the night, he had not yet decided how to bring his work once more to the Sanhedrin’s attention. The way came the next morning, near the temple, in an unexpected form. (164:2.3)

III. Healing the Blind Beggar — Not Because of Sin

On the Sabbath morning near the temple, Jesus saw Josiah, a beggar blind from birth, and at that moment the way to bring his mission once more before the leaders came to him. Nathaniel asked whose sin had caused this man’s blindness. At the time, many notions were current that saw a birth defect as the result of sin. (164:3.1, 164:3.2)

Source 164:3.3

A contradictory practiceThough blindness was thought a result of sin, giving alms to the blind was considered noble
Neither did this man sin nor his parents that the works of God might be manifest in him. (164:3.7)

Jesus made clear that the blindness had come about in the natural course of events. He repeatedly warned his apostles to seek the true causes of all phenomena and to avoid the tendency to attach spiritual causes to ordinary physical events. This refusal to brand a suffering person with the stigma of sin is an important correction for today’s reader as well. (164:3.6, 164:3.7)

He made clay with spittle, put it on Josiah’s eyes, and told him to wash in the pool of Siloam. Josiah had not asked to be healed and his faith was feeble, but having overheard the conversation and taking this man to be a great teacher or prophet, he did as he was told. The text gives three reasons for this use of material means. (164:3.8, 164:3.11, 164:3.12)

Source 164:3.13
ReasonExplanation
FirstIt was not a response to individual faith but a deed of Jesus’ own choosing, arranged so the man would receive lasting benefit
SecondTo encourage a man who had not asked and whose faith was weak — the ceremony held just enough to prompt him to act
ThirdTo teach his followers not to despise or neglect material means in healing the sick, nor to regard miracles as the only method of cure
He wanted to teach them that they must cease to regard miracles as the only method of curing human diseases. (164:3.15)

Having always been a beggar, Josiah went back to his usual place of alms-seeking once the first excitement of his new sight had passed. When neighbors whispered whether this was really the beggar, he answered that he was, but he did not know where the man who healed him was. He did not yet know the meaning of much that he saw. He had received grace but did not yet understand what it meant. (164:3.9, 164:3.10)

I am he. (164:3.9)

The chief purpose of this deed was an open challenge to the Sanhedrin and the religious leaders, a public declaration of the break with the Pharisees. So in the afternoon Jesus sent the two apostles to Josiah to stir up a controversy deliberately, so that the Pharisees could not fail to take notice of the miracle. (164:3.16)

IV. Josiah Before the Sanhedrin — A Growing Testimony

The Sanhedrin convened its council in violation of its own rule against meeting on the Sabbath. Jesus had hoped to stand before them over a Sabbath healing in order to expose this very point, but they were afraid to summon him and called only Josiah. The meeting soon fell into division. To the claim that one who does not keep the Sabbath cannot be from God, younger members who secretly believed responded with pointed questions. (164:4.1, 164:4.3, 164:4.4)

His parents, when summoned, feared excommunication; they admitted only that their son had been born blind and told the council to ask him, since he was of age. Excommunication meant losing every right in Jewish society except that of buying the necessities of life. Called back again, Josiah would not bow to pressure to declare Jesus a sinner. (164:4.6, 164:4.7, 164:4.8)

Whether this man is a sinner, I know not; but one thing I do know—that, whereas I was blind, now I see. (164:4.9)

When the same questions were repeated, Josiah asked whether they too wanted to become his disciples, and at last, standing on a stool, he cried out that if this man were not from God, he could not have done this. The leaders cast him out of the synagogue. The text says that most of his wise and courageous testimony grew up in his mind while the unfair trial went on. (164:4.10, 164:4.11, 164:4.12)

V. Teaching in Solomon’s Porch — My Sheep Hear My Voice

While the Sanhedrin met, Jesus taught a midwinter crowd for more than two hours nearby in Solomon’s Porch. He had hoped to be summoned so that he could proclaim the freedom and joy of divine sonship, but they were afraid even to call him as a witness. When challenged to say plainly whether he was the Messiah, he answered that the works he did in his Father’s name bore witness for him. (164:5.1, 164:5.2)

The teacher of truth attracts only those who hunger for the truth and who thirst for righteousness. (164:5.2)

When he said that he and the Father were one, some ran to pick up stones. He asked for which of his good works they meant to stone him, and told them that if they could not believe him, they should at least believe the works. Then he declared that as the Father dwelt in him, so he would dwell in all who believed the gospel. (164:5.2, 164:5.3)

the Father is in me and I in the Father, and that, as the Father dwells in me, so will I dwell in every one who believes this gospel. (164:5.3)

Only when Jesus heard that Josiah had been cast out of the synagogue did he go to find him and ask whether he believed in the Son of God. Josiah fell down, confessed his belief, and worshiped, and he was greatly encouraged when told to come with them to the camp at Pella. He spent the rest of his life as a preacher of the gospel of the kingdom. (164:5.4, 164:5.5, 164:5.6)

This simple-minded man of Jerusalem had indeed been cast out of a Jewish synagogue, but behold the Creator of a universe leading him forth to become associated with the spiritual nobility of that day and generation. (164:5.5)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

In Paper 164 Jesus gives the leaders another chance though he knows they will refuse, and he chooses a man from the lowest place in society as his means. A lawyer who tried to narrow the circle of neighbors ended up giving the wider answer himself, and a blind beggar spoke the truth more plainly before the court than the scholars did. The paper shows that faith does not arrive complete from the start; it grows as it is tested. (164:2.3, 164:4.12)
