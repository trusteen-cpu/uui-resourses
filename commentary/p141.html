Introduction — Tears in a Boat

On January 19, A.D. 27, Jesus and the twelve prepared to leave their headquarters at Bethsaida. The apostles’ families and the disciples gathered to see them off, but just before departure Jesus could not be found. Andrew went looking and found him sitting in a boat down the beach, weeping. None of the apostles had ever seen him weep. (141:0.1, 141:0.2)

I am saddened only because none of my father Joseph’s family have remembered to come over to bid us Godspeed. (141:0.2)

His family stayed away out of pride, disappointment, misunderstanding, and petty resentment over hurt feelings. It is significant that the first day of the public work begins with this human sorrow. Even under the weight of his mission, Jesus did not take his family’s love lightly. The paper then follows the journey of nearly three months down the Jordan valley to Jerusalem. (141:0.2)

I. Leaving Galilee — Taking Over John’s Work

As Jesus’ fame spread, he headed south toward Judea, away from Herod’s attention. More than a hundred believers wanted to follow, but he begged them not to come along. The party stayed more than two weeks at the place on the Jordan near Pella where John had preached and Jesus had been baptized, and hundreds gathered there from many regions. (141:1.1, 141:1.2)

Jesus did no public preaching; Andrew divided the crowd and assigned the preaching to the apostles. Many of those who came were John’s disciples, and for them it was a constant stumbling block that Jesus did nothing to free John from prison. The apostles never belittled John’s preaching, and night after night Andrew taught his associates how to get along smoothly with John’s followers. (141:1.3, 141:1.4, 141:1.5)

During this first year of Jesus’ public ministry more than three fourths of his followers had previously followed John and had received his baptism. (141:1.5)

II. God’s Law and the Father’s Will — From Subjects to Sons

On the night before leaving Pella, Jesus gave new instruction about the kingdom. The apostles had pictured the kingdom of heaven as a glorified Jewish rule with the Messiah on David’s throne, promulgating the laws of the world by miraculous power. Jesus declared that the kingdom is the realization and acknowledgment of God’s rule within human hearts. Its King is the Father, who rules his children’s hearts through the spirit that lives within the human soul. (141:2.1)

when the Father’s will is your law, you are hardly in the kingdom. But when the Father’s will becomes truly your will, then are you in very truth in the kingdom (141:2.2)

Of the apostles, only James Zebedee may have grasped the full meaning of this announcement. Yet the words sank deep into their hearts and later brought joy to their ministry. The teaching puts into the language of faith something we know from life: the same task feels entirely different to one who does it from duty and to one who does it because he wants to. (141:2.3)

III. The Sojourn at Amathus — A Strong and Manly Jesus

During almost three weeks near Amathus the apostles preached twice a day, and Jesus preached only on Sabbath afternoons. Since the Wednesday holiday could no longer be kept, Andrew gave two apostles a day of rest in turn. The apostles’ roles also sorted themselves out naturally. (141:3.1, 141:3.2)

Friction between John’s disciples and the newer disciples never ceased, yet Jesus never once advised how to settle it. A host, he said, does not take part in the family troubles of his guests, and a wise parent does not take sides in the petty quarrels of his children. Leaving them to solve their own problems was how he trained the apostles. (141:3.3)

Here the narrator corrects a common misunderstanding of Jesus’ personality. He was an intellectual force and a spiritual fortress, a man of dignified manhood. Had he been the weak figure that artists often paint, the temple merchants would hardly have fled before him. (141:3.5, 141:3.6)

He not only meant well, but he went about actually doing good. (141:3.6)

The words “resist not evil” did not mean condoning sin. They taught forgiveness: not to retaliate against mistreatment of one’s own person or injury to one’s dignity. And though the Master’s yoke is easy, he never imposes it; each person must take it up by free will. (141:3.7, 141:3.8)

IV. Teaching about the Father — A Father, Not a Bookkeeper

Again and again Jesus impressed on the apostles that God is a Father, not a great bookkeeper recording his children’s sins to use against them at the judgment. The Jews had thought of God as Father of the nation, but never before had large numbers of people thought of him as the loving Father of the individual. He also tried to free them from the idea that animal sacrifices were a duty. (141:4.1, 141:4.3)

God is your Father, and religion—my gospel—is nothing more nor less than the believing recognition of the truth that you are his son. (141:4.2)

From this time Jesus taught more fully the mission of comforting the afflicted and ministering to the sick. He had them see a person as a whole of body, mind, and spirit, and he taught them to distinguish three kinds of affliction they would meet. The text notes that the Master understood the difference between spirit possession and insanity, but the apostles did not. (141:4.4, 141:4.8)

V. Spiritual Unity — The Same Spirit, Not the Same Opinions

When James asked how they might learn to see alike and so enjoy more harmony, Jesus was stirred in spirit as he answered. He did not want social harmony to be bought at the price of free personality and spiritual originality. (141:5.1)

You do not have to see alike or feel alike or even think alike in order spiritually to be alike. (141:5.1)

Spiritual unity comes from the shared awareness that each person is indwelt by the Father’s spirit gift, and that this spirit increasingly rules within. It can be enjoyed amid the greatest diversity of thought, temperament, and conduct. The text spells out its two meanings: a common motive for service, the desire above all to do the Father’s will; and a common goal of existence, to find the Father and become like him. (141:5.2, 141:5.3)

Jesus did not want believers dogmatized and standardized, not even according to the interpretations of good men, and he warned repeatedly against controlling believers by creeds and traditions. His teaching that people of differing views can serve the same Father applies just as well to communities of faith today. (141:5.4)

VI. Last Week at Amathus — Put Something In, Don’t Take Something Out

Simon Zelotes tried to teach Teherma, a Persian from Damascus, but regarded him as a “fire worshiper” and failed to persuade him. Yet Teherma listened readily to Jesus. When Simon asked why, Jesus answered him. (141:6.1, 141:6.2)

How often have I told you to labor only to put something into these hungry souls? (141:6.2)

Lead people into the kingdom, and its living truths will soon drive out serious error. Jesus himself had come not to take away what people had received from their forefathers, but to show them more fully what they had seen in part. That night he taught the proper order: do not try to show people the beauties of the temple before you have taken them into it, and introduce them to God before discussing doctrine. Simon did as he was told, and Teherma entered the kingdom. (141:6.2, 141:6.3, 141:6.4)

It is not your kingdom; you are only ambassadors. (141:6.4)

VII. At Bethany Beyond Jordan — A Teacher, Not a Preacher

On February 26 the party moved to Bethany in Perea, where John had first proclaimed the kingdom, and stayed four weeks. In the second week Jesus rested three days in the hills with Peter, James, and John and taught them new truths. A teacher’s only business is to reveal God to the individual as his Father and to lead that person to become son-conscious. Jesus left behind no books, laws, or organizations. (141:7.1, 141:7.2, 141:7.4)

He emphasized two truths above all: salvation by faith alone, and the liberty that comes from knowing the truth. He meant to show not an example for a few but a standard of living for all the worlds of his universe. The text calls him a teacher, not a preacher. As an orator Peter was more effective, but Jesus spoke directly to people’s souls through his unique personality. (141:7.6, 141:7.9, 141:7.10)

VIII–IX. Working in Jericho; Departing for Jerusalem

While they stayed at Bethany, Andrew sent the apostles in pairs to Jericho. They visited every house in the city to comfort the afflicted, and they found that the good news of the kingdom brought great comfort to the sick. Jericho was where the charge to preach the glad tidings and minister to the afflicted was first fully carried out. They also spent three days with a delegation from Mesopotamia. (141:8.1, 141:8.2, 141:8.3)

On the last Monday of March the party took the road up to Jerusalem. Jesus and the twelve rested five days at the home of Lazarus in Bethany, where Martha and Mary were glad to receive them. On Sunday morning, April 6, Jesus and all twelve apostles entered Jerusalem together for the first time. (141:9.1, 141:9.2, 141:9.3)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 141 portrays the first steps of the public work quietly. Rather than giving orders, Jesus let the apostles solve their own problems, and rather than imposing uniform thinking, he made them one in the same spirit. The faith he taught was not the religion of subjects obeying a law but the religion of sons who make the Father’s will their own. (141:2.2, 141:5.3)
