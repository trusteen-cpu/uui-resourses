Introduction — At the Foot of Mount Hermon

Near sundown on August 12, A.D. 29, Jesus and the apostles reached the foot of Mount Hermon, close to the place where the lad Tiglath had once waited while the Master went up the mountain alone and technically terminated the Lucifer rebellion. They stayed there two days in spiritual preparation for what was to follow. (158:0.1)

Jesus wished all his apostles could share the experience on the mountain, but none of them had reached the spiritual level that would justify exposure to the visitation of celestial beings. So he took only Peter, James, and John, who usually accompanied him on such special vigils. This paper portrays, within a single week, glory on the mountain, failure below it, and the first clear foretelling of his death. (158:0.2)

I. The Transfiguration — The Choice to Decline Universe Authority

On Monday, August 15, six days after Peter’s confession, Jesus climbed the mountain with the three apostles. The text notes the significance that this event took place in the lands of the gentiles, on a gentile mountain. As a boy he had climbed the hill near his home and dreamed of the battles of empires; now he climbed a mountain to receive the endowment for the closing scenes of his bestowal. (158:1.1, 158:1.2, 158:1.4)

That day Jesus could have relinquished the struggle and returned to his rule of the universe. But he chose not only to meet the requirements of the Eternal Son but also to fulfill the last and full measure of his Paradise Father’s present will. The three apostles watched him decline to be invested with full universe authority, and saw him left alone when the celestial messengers departed. (158:1.4)

At about three in the afternoon Jesus went apart for a long conference with Gabriel and the Father Melchizedek, returning about six o’clock to reassure his anxious apostles. That night the three, who had fallen asleep, were awakened by a crackling sound and saw Jesus talking with two brilliant beings clothed in light. Peter mistakenly took them for Moses and Elijah; in reality they were Gabriel and the Father Melchizedek. (158:1.6, 158:1.8)

I now declare that the Son of Man has chosen to go through his full life in your midst and as one of you. (158:1.6)

In his confusion Peter proposed building three tents. Then a silvery cloud overshadowed them, and they heard the same voice that had spoken at the baptism. Jesus touched them as they lay on their faces and told them not to be afraid. (158:1.9, 158:1.10)

This is my beloved Son; give heed to him. (158:1.10)

II. Coming down the Mountain — Elijah Has Already Come

Halfway down the mountain Jesus told them to tell no one what they had seen until the Son of Man had risen from the dead. Having just seen his glory, the apostles were shocked to hear of death and resurrection. To steer away from the subject, Peter asked about the scribes’ saying that Elijah must come first, and Jesus answered that Elijah had already come. The three realized that he meant John the Baptist. (158:2.1, 158:2.2)

He enjoined silence so as not to foster their mistaken idea of a wonder-working Messiah. Jesus told them that since they would not receive him as the Son of Man, he had consented to be received according to their settled determination. But he warned them that this choice would carry a cost. (158:2.3, 158:2.4)

but the training which I have given you should suffice to bring you triumphantly through even these sorrows of your own choosing. (158:2.4)

The text makes clear that the three were not chosen because they were better prepared or more fit. None of the twelve was spiritually qualified; these three were simply the ones assigned to accompany him in times of solitary communion. That privilege is not proof of superiority is a lesson worth keeping in any community today. (158:2.5)

III. Meaning of the Transfiguration — Three Testimonies

What the three apostles saw was only a fleeting glimpse of a celestial pageant that took place that day on Mount Hermon. The text explains the transfiguration as the occasion of divine acknowledgment of the fullness of the bestowal. (158:3.1)

Jesus noted that the Father had not indicated the bestowal was finished, and he decided to pursue the mortal bestowal to its natural end. That was the meaning of the transfiguration for him. For the three apostles it marked the Master’s entrance upon the final phase of his earth career. It is striking how the same event carries different meanings depending on where one stands. (158:3.5)

IV–V. The Epileptic Boy; Jesus Heals the Boy

Below the mountain a very different scene was unfolding. James of Safed had spent nearly two weeks searching for Jesus with his only son, about fourteen years old, who was epileptic and possessed by a rebellious midwayer. When he arrived, the nine apostles were arguing about who should be greatest in the coming kingdom. (158:4.2, 158:4.3, 158:4.4)

Simon Zelotes and Judas Iscariot stepped forward, claiming that the keys of the kingdom had been given to them, and tried to cast out the demon, but the boy only had a more violent fit while the scribes mocked. Andrew tried again after prayer and also failed, and frankly confessed defeat. Meanwhile the three coming down the mountain were ecstatic, and the nine below lay sleepless in humiliation. (158:4.6, 158:4.7, 158:4.8)

When Jesus returned, the father knelt before him. Jesus rebuked them, saying that the works of faith do not come forth at the bidding of doubting unbelief, and he told the father to question not the Father’s power of love but only the sincerity and reach of his own faith. (158:5.1, 158:5.2)

Lord, I believe. I pray you help my unbelief. (158:5.2)

Jesus raised the boy in accordance with the Father’s will and in honor of living faith. It was a true healing of a double affliction, physical and spiritual. The text adds that the twelve always alternated between exaltation and humiliation. The father’s confession, mingling faith and doubt, comforts us that an honestly seeking faith is accepted even without perfect certainty. (158:5.3, 158:5.4, 158:5.5)

VI. In Celsus’ Garden — The Causes of Failure

That evening Thomas asked Jesus to teach them about their defeat. Jesus pointed out that those who stayed behind, though instructed to pray, had failed to exercise their faith and had fallen back into the old tendency of seeking preferred places in the kingdom. (158:6.1, 158:6.2)

Spiritual greatness consists in an understanding love that is Godlike and not in an enjoyment of the exercise of material power for the exaltation of self. (158:6.3)

Jesus said that spiritual work requires spiritual power, and that even where the potential is present, nothing can be done without the third and essential factor: the personal experience of living faith. He then told them again that the Son of Man would be put to death but would rise again. The apostles retired sorrowful, and remembered his words only after the resurrection. (158:6.4, 158:6.5, 158:6.6)

When can you be depended upon to adhere to the higher and spiritual realities of the kingdom regardless of the outward appearance of all material manifestations? (158:6.4)

This question is put to everyone who wants visible results to confirm faith. A faith that holds to spiritual realities even when prayer is not answered at once, or when good work ends in apparent failure—that is the maturity Jesus hoped for in his apostles. (158:6.4)

VII. Peter’s Protest — When Love Becomes a Stumbling Block

The next day Jesus avoided his critics by taking only the apostles along the Damascus road. When Andrew asked him to speak plainly rather than in parables, Jesus told them directly that he must be rejected in Jerusalem, be killed, and rise again. Peter impulsively laid his hand on the Master’s shoulder and declared that these things would never happen to him. (158:7.1, 158:7.2, 158:7.3)

Peter spoke out of love. But Jesus’ human nature recognized in that well-meant affection a subtle temptation to change his course of following the Father’s will to the end. So, with a rare flash of the eye, he rebuked him at once. (158:7.4, 158:7.8)

Mind not the ways of men but rather the will of God. (158:7.4)

Jesus went on to tell them to disregard themselves, take up their responsibilities daily, and follow him. He was not sending them alone into the conflict but leading them, and asked only that they follow bravely. Even in their shock, none of them was minded to forsake him. The scene shows that the good intentions of those closest to us can at times become the greatest temptation, and that discernment is needed to recognize it. (158:7.5, 158:7.6)

The apostles only vaguely grasped that he was speaking of his death; what he said about rising again failed to register at all. Yet as the days passed, Peter, James, and John, recalling the mount of transfiguration, came to understand some of these matters more fully. The experience on the mountain became a key to hard sayings later on. A deep experience need not be fully understood at the time; with time it can become a light on other stages of life. (158:7.7)

VIII. At Peter’s House — Like a Little Child

When they reached Capernaum, Jesus asked what they had been discussing all afternoon, and the apostles held their peace. They had been continuing the argument about rank begun at Mount Hermon. Jesus set one of Peter’s little children in their midst. (158:8.1)

Whosoever shall humble himself and become as this little one, the same shall become greatest in the kingdom of heaven. (158:8.1)

Jesus told the apostles that if they would be first in the kingdom, they should seek to minister these good truths to their brethren in the flesh. The teaching that the way to greatness is service echoes what they had heard in Celsus’ garden; the apostles who argued over rank heard the same lesson twice within a few days. (158:8.1, 158:6.3)

But most of all, see that you despise not one of these little ones (158:8.1)

He charged them not to cause the little ones to stumble, to sacrifice the cherished idols that hinder the progress of the kingdom, and never to despise even one of these little ones. To men contending for greatness, the answer given was not exaltation but humility. (158:8.1)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 158 sets the mountain of glory beside the valley of failure. Jesus declined universe authority and chose to live as one among men, while the apostles at his side went through rivalry, failure, and shock again and again. Yet Jesus was always patient with their human shortcomings and firm only when something threatened the doing of the Father’s will. (158:1.4, 158:7.8)
