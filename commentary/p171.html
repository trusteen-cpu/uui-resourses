Introduction — Seats on the Right Hand and the Left

The day after the sermon on the kingdom of heaven, Jesus announced that he would leave for the Passover at Jerusalem, visiting the cities of southern Perea on the way. His followers took this to mean that he was at last going up to inaugurate the temporal kingdom of Jewish supremacy. Whatever he said about the nonmaterial character of the kingdom, the hope of a nationalistic government would not be erased. (171:0.1, 171:0.2)

That Sunday afternoon Salome, the mother of James and John, came with her two sons and asked that in the kingdom one of them might sit on Jesus’ right hand and the other on his left. Jesus would make no promise in advance; instead he asked whether they could drink the cup he was about to drink. Without a moment’s thought the two answered that they could. When the other apostles heard of it, they were highly indignant. (171:0.4, 171:0.5, 171:0.6)

Whosoever would be great among you, let him first become your servant. (171:0.6)

That evening, thanks to Peter’s efforts, the two brothers apologized to the ten and were restored to their good graces. The narrator notes that they little realized that within a month their teacher would hang on a cross with a dying thief on one side and another transgressor on the other. This brief scene sharply exposes the gap between a heart that seeks honor and a Master who speaks of service. (171:0.6, 171:0.7)

I. The Departure from Pella — A Camp Breaks Up

On the forenoon of Monday, March 13, Jesus and the twelve took final leave of the Pella encampment. About a thousand disciples followed, most of them convinced that he was now going to Jerusalem to lay claim to David’s throne. Two days later David Zebedee, on Andrew’s instructions, closed the visitors’ camp, where almost four thousand were staying, sold its equipment, and turned the money over to Judas Iscariot. (171:1.1, 171:1.3, 171:1.4)

David disliked the task but carried it out, and he was present in Jerusalem through the tragic last week. Later he went to Philadelphia, where he spent the rest of his life with Abner and Lazarus as financial overseer of the kingdom’s work there. Andrew’s order to end the messenger service was taken by everyone as a sign that the kingdom would soon be set up—a reminder that when expectations harden, the same event can be read in exactly the opposite way. (171:1.4, 171:1.5)

Along with David’s later story, the narrator gives a glimpse of a parting of ways far in the future. After the destruction of Jerusalem, two centers carried Jesus’ teachings in different directions. (171:1.5, 171:1.6)

II. On Counting the Cost — Sit Down and Reckon Before You Follow

At the Bethany ford of the Jordan the disciples realized that Jesus was not going straight to Jerusalem, and they hesitated. Then Jesus climbed onto a huge stone and gave the discourse known as “Counting the Cost.” From now on, he told them, following him would no longer mean listening to teaching and watching his works; it would mean bearing witness to the gospel in the face of bitter persecution and crushing disappointment. (171:2.1, 171:2.4)

If you are not willing to pay the full price, you can hardly be my disciple. (171:2.3)

After these words about half the crowd left him and went directly up to Jerusalem. Even those who stayed still believed that after a brief time of trial the kingdom would be established much as they had always hoped. The call to count the cost remains an honest question for believers today: before emotional enthusiasm, look first at your motives. (171:1.2, 171:2.6)

III–IV. The Perean Tour and Livias — Swords and Warnings

For more than two weeks Jesus and the twelve went about the towns of southern Perea where the seventy were working. At Heshbon he met Abner, and this was the last time Abner saw him in the flesh. Jesus advised the messengers to go on with their work regardless of what was about to happen at Jerusalem. Day by day the number of followers dwindled, until fewer than two hundred remained. (171:3.1, 171:3.2, 171:3.3)

and I pray the Father to grant you wisdom that you may love and understand your brethren. (171:3.2)

The apostles knew the Sanhedrin had condemned him to death, yet they were less afraid than before. Having seen Lazarus raised, they had concluded that Jesus might, in an emergency, assert his divine power and put his enemies to shame. At Livias the two Simons secretly handed out more than a hundred swords. Jesus knew of it but never said so; early the next morning he gathered the apostles and once more told them plainly of his death and of his rising on the third day. (171:3.4, 171:4.1, 171:4.2)

I would now warn you not to put your trust in the uncertainties of the flesh nor in the frailties of man’s defense (171:4.2)

Most of the apostles and many of the inner disciples, believing him to be “the resurrection and the life,” did not think it possible that he could die. When faith and expectation become entangled, even the clearest warning is bent to fit the hearer’s hopes. The fact that the Jews knew little or nothing of a suffering Messiah deepened the misunderstanding. (171:3.5, 171:4.9)

The apostles could not take his words literally. The “third day” was a common expression for “soon,” so they took his “rising” to mean the rising of the kingdom. When friendly Pharisees warned that Herod sought to kill him, Jesus told them to tell that fox the course he would take, and he lamented over Jerusalem. The narrator adds that what sustained the Master in this trying period was the memory of the transfiguration. (171:4.3, 171:4.6, 171:4.8, 171:4.9)

V–VI. Two Men at Jericho — Bartimeus and Zaccheus

Late on Thursday afternoon, March 30, near the gate of Jericho, Bartimeus, a beggar blind from his youth, heard that Jesus was passing by and began to cry out. The more people rebuked him, the louder he cried. Jesus stood still, had him brought over, asked what he wanted, and, seeing his faith, restored his sight. (171:5.1, 171:5.2, 171:5.3)

What stands out in the story is Jesus’ question. He did not guess at the man’s need and act first; he asked, “What do you want me to do for you?” He had put the same question to Salome, but one asked for honor and the other for sight. Bartimeus, his sight restored, stayed near Jesus glorifying God until the Master left the next day. (171:0.4, 171:5.3)

That same evening Zaccheus, the chief publican, being short of stature, climbed a sycamore tree to see Jesus. Jesus stopped beneath it and told him he would stay at his house that night. When a Pharisee grumbled that he was lodging with a sinner, Zaccheus stood on a stool and publicly promised to give half his goods to the poor and to restore fourfold whatever he had wrongfully exacted. (171:6.1, 171:6.2)

Today has salvation come to this home, and you have become indeed a son of Abraham. (171:6.3)

VII. “As Jesus Passed By” — Gracious Goodness

Here the narrator pauses the story to portray Jesus’ character. He spread good cheer wherever he went and was full of grace and truth. His compassion was boundless, yet he seldom indulged in pity and never increased the self-pity of those who suffered. People felt not so much that they were confessing to him as that they were conferring with him. (171:7.1, 171:7.3, 171:7.5)

Goodness is universally attractive only when it is gracious. Goodness is effective only when it is attractive. (171:7.2)

He never sought to direct or manage people, yet he did not hesitate to be severe when the occasion demanded it. Great things happened not only because people had faith in him but also because he had so much faith in them. Most of the truly important things he said or did seemed to happen casually, “as he passed by.” (171:7.6, 171:7.7, 171:7.8, 171:7.9)

When he smiled on a man, that mortal experienced increased capacity for solving his manifold problems. (171:7.6)
And it behooves the Master’s followers in all ages to learn to minister as “they pass by”—to do unselfish good as they go about their daily duties. (171:7.10)

VIII. Parable of the Pounds — Ability and Faithfulness

Halfway up the road to Bethany, Jesus told the apostles the parable of the pounds. It drew on the actual history of Archelaus and his failed attempt to gain the rule of Judea. Just as that prince was rejected by his citizens, Jesus said, the Son of Man would be rejected in his spiritual rule. He made the twelve his special stewards and charged them to trade diligently with what had been entrusted to them. (171:8.2, 171:8.3, 171:8.4)

The servant who gained ten pounds was given ten cities; the one who gained five, five cities. The servant who feared his master and kept the pound wrapped in a napkin lost even that. When the apostles asked how this parable differed from the parable of the talents, Jesus only told them each to find the true meaning for themselves. In later years Nathaniel summed up the meaning of the two parables in four conclusions. (171:8.6, 171:8.7, 171:8.8, 171:8.9)

After lunch Jesus pointed westward and said, “Come, my brethren, let us go on into Jerusalem, there to receive that which awaits us.” So the Master’s last journey to Jerusalem in the likeness of mortal flesh was resumed. (171:8.14, 171:8.15)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 171 is a paper of the road to Jerusalem. The number of followers kept shrinking, and even those who stayed expected a kingdom of glory. Along that road Jesus taught service, warned his hearers to count the cost, and did not pass by the blind man or the publican at the roadside. The paper shows a life that, even while moving toward a great mission, cares for the one person in front of it. (171:7.9)
