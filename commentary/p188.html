Introduction — A Chapter Little Known

The day and a half during which Jesus’ body lay in Joseph’s tomb is a chapter of Michael’s earth life that is almost unknown. The narrator says at the outset that the burial and the resurrection can be recorded, but what actually happened during the roughly thirty-six hours from three o’clock Friday afternoon to three o’clock Sunday morning cannot be told with certainty. This honesty — saying plainly what is not known — sets the tone of the whole paper. (188:0.1)

The authorities intended, as was customary, to throw Jesus’ body into the open burial pits of Gehenna. But Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus went to Pilate and asked for permission to give the body a proper burial. Joseph had brought a large sum of money to pay for the privilege, but Pilate would take nothing and signed the order at once. (188:0.2, 188:0.3)

I. The Burial of Jesus — The Courage of Two Former Sanhedrists

At Golgotha, representatives of the Sanhedrin were standing by to make sure the body went to the criminal pits. When Joseph presented the order, they raised a tumult and tried to seize the body by force. The centurion had four soldiers stand over the body with drawn swords until order was restored; then he read Pilate’s permit aloud and gave the body to Joseph. (188:1.1)

This body is yours to do with as you see fit. I and my soldiers will stand by to see that no man interferes. (188:1.1)

A crucified person could not be buried in a Jewish cemetery, so the two men decided to lay Jesus in Joseph’s new family tomb, hewn out of solid rock. Joseph sincerely believed in the resurrection, while Nicodemus was doubtful. But these two former Sanhedrists, who had kept their faith more or less hidden, from this time on became the most outspoken disciples in Jerusalem. (188:1.2)

The men hurried away as the Sabbath approached, but Mary Magdalene, Mary the wife of Clopas, Martha (Jesus’ mother’s sister), and Rebecca of Sepphoris stayed near the tomb until it grew dark. The text adds that, apart from David Zebedee and Joseph, hardly any of the disciples really believed that Jesus would rise on the third day. (188:1.6, 188:1.7, 188:1.8)

II. Safeguarding the Tomb — The Side That Remembered the Promise

His followers had forgotten the promise of the third day, but his enemies had not. Gathering at the home of Caiaphas around midnight Friday, the leaders feared that the disciples might steal the body and proclaim a resurrection. The next morning a committee went to Pilate to ask for a guard. (188:2.1, 188:2.2)

Pilate gave them ten soldiers, and they added ten temple guards, so that twenty men watched the tomb. Another stone was rolled before the tomb, and Pilate’s seal was set upon it. There is an irony here: the very thoroughness of their effort to defeat the promise would make the events that followed all the more clearly attested. (188:2.3)

III. During the Sabbath Day — Thirty-Six Hours in the Tomb

Throughout the Sabbath the disciples and apostles stayed in hiding, while nearly a million and a half pilgrims in Jerusalem talked about the crucifixion. These pilgrims would soon carry the news of the resurrection back to their homes. On Saturday night John Mark quietly gathered the eleven apostles in the upper room of the Last Supper. (188:3.1, 188:3.2)

Meanwhile the scattered believers passed the Sabbath where they were. Mary returned with Ruth and Jude to the family at Bethany; David, at the home of Nicodemus, prepared to gather his messengers on Sunday morning; the women of Galilee stayed at Joseph’s home and prepared spices. Even in their grief, each quietly went on doing what he or she could. (188:3.3)

The narrator treats Jesus’ condition in the tomb with great care. Jesus said, “Father, into your hands I commend my spirit,” yet since his Thought Adjuster had long since been personalized, the narrator admits not fully understanding what those words meant. He can only suggest that what Jesus entrusted to the Father was a spiritual reality analogous to the soul of faith-growing mortals. (188:3.4)

But this is merely our opinion—we do not really know what Jesus commended to his Father. (188:3.4)

The narrator says all this suggests that not all of Jesus’ personality was asleep, yet admits that its interpretation lies beyond his ability. (188:3.10, 188:3.11, 188:3.13, 188:3.16)

IV. Meaning of the Death on the Cross — Not Atonement or Ransom

This section is the heart of the paper. The text states plainly that Jesus did not die to atone for the racial guilt of mankind, to appease an angry God, or to pay a ransom to Satan for man. At the same time it warns that in avoiding this error we must not lose sight of the true meaning of the death on the cross. (188:4.1, 188:4.2, 188:4.3)

Moses taught the dignity and justice of a Creator God; but Jesus portrayed the love and mercy of a heavenly Father. (188:4.4)

The text says that before Jesus came one might perhaps have been justified in believing in such a stern God, but not since he lived and died among us. As revelation grows, our understanding of God must grow too. Its account of the nature of sin, in particular, lifts the vague sense of guilt so many people carry. (188:4.4, 188:4.5)

The animal nature—the tendency toward evil-doing—may be hereditary, but sin is not transmitted from parent to child. (188:4.5)

What, then, is Jesus? The text says that while it may not be proper to call him a sacrificer or a ransomer, it is altogether proper to call him a savior. He lived and died not for this world alone but for a whole universe, and he forever made clear the survival of personality after death. (188:4.6, 188:4.7)

The great thing about the death of Jesus, as it is related to the enrichment of human experience and the enlargement of the way of salvation, is not the fact of his death but rather the superb manner and the matchless spirit in which he met death. (188:4.12)

Salvation is not a philosophic transaction but a reality. It rests on two realities, the fatherhood of God and the brotherhood of man, which can be grasped by faith and made part of one’s personal experience. The true believer is less concerned about future punishment than about present separation from God. (188:4.10, 188:4.13)

V. Lessons from the Cross — The Sign of Love Overcoming Hate

The cross shows the supreme devotion of the true shepherd, even for unworthy members of his flock. It places the relationship between God and man forever on a family basis: God is the Father, and man is his son. (188:5.1)

Love is truly contagious and eternally creative. (188:5.2)

The text says that the forgiveness of love transcends the forgiveness of mercy. Mercy sets guilt to one side, but love destroys sin and all the weakness that follows from it. Jesus’ forgiveness is not condonation that makes light of wrong; it is salvation from condemnation, which makes the wrong right. (188:5.2, 188:5.3)

Jesus’ suffering was not confined to the cross. For more than twenty-five years he had carried the cross of a real and strenuous mortal existence. His prayer, “Father, forgive them,” made the cross an eternal symbol of love triumphing over hate and of truth over evil. That loving devotion passed into his disciples: the first teacher to lay down his life for the gospel prayed, as he was stoned, that this sin not be laid to their charge. (188:5.4, 188:5.6)

Jesus had such a love that he was willing to lay down his life for his enemies, a love greater than any which had hitherto been known on earth. (188:5.7)

It is said that no one has greater love than to lay down his life for his friends, but Jesus’ love reached even to his enemies. The meaning of the cross, then, lies not in what the Father required but in what the Son willingly gave. (188:5.7, 188:5.11)

There was nothing in the cross which the Father required—only that which Jesus so willingly gave, and which he refused to avoid. (188:5.11)

The text urges us not to view the cross through the eyes of primitive man, assuming a God who is a relentless enforcer of law. Rather, it is the climax of the unfolding of the Father’s divine love. It also gives the comfort that no one need ever fear that the Creator does not know our suffering. (188:5.11, 188:5.12, 188:5.13)

No man can ever fear that the Creator does not know the nature or extent of his temporal afflictions. (188:5.12)

The narrator sums up once more: the death on the cross was not meant to reconcile man to God. It was meant to stimulate man’s realization of the Father’s eternal love and the Son’s unending mercy, and to broadcast these truths to a whole universe. The record that creatures on countless worlds, ready to give up the struggle of faith, looked at Jesus on the cross and rose again shows that this love is still a power that lifts the weary. (188:5.5, 188:5.13)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Along with the quiet faithfulness at the tomb, Paper 188 asks us to change the way we see the cross. It is not an offering to appease an angry God; it is the sign of a good person giving himself to the wicked and saving them by love. To look upon it stirs in us the desire to go and do likewise. (188:5.9)
