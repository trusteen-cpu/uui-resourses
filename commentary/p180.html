Introduction — Why He Had Them Sit Down Again

After the supper ended with the singing of the Psalm, the apostles thought they would return straight to the camp. But Jesus had them sit down again. He reminded them that when he had once sent them out without purse or wallet, they had lacked nothing; now, in troublous times when they could no longer depend on the good will of the multitudes, they were to make provision for their own support. (180:0.1, 180:0.2)

Then he said that the time had come for the Son of Man to be glorified. Soon they would seek him and not find him, but when they had finished their work on earth, they would come to him. This paper presents in six sections the farewell discourse that began this way. Its content is what the one who was leaving wanted, first and most deeply, to leave with those who would remain. (180:0.3)

I. The New Commandment — Beyond Loving the Neighbor

Jesus now gave the new commandment he had foreshadowed in the parable of the foot washing. The apostles knew well the commandment to love their neighbor as themselves. But the Master was not wholly satisfied with that. He changed the measure of love from “yourself” to “as I have loved you.” (180:1.1)

That you love one another even as I have loved you. And by this will all men know that you are my disciples if you thus love one another. (180:1.1)

Jesus said this commandment was not a new burden but a new joy. Greater love has no man than to lay down his life for his friends, and the apostles were no longer servants but friends. The narrator adds an important comment: what Jesus demands is loyalty, not sacrifice, and the service of a friend for a friend can never be called a sacrifice. (180:1.2, 180:1.3, 180:1.6)

The Master had taught the apostles that they were sons of God, then called them brethren, and now, before he left, he called them friends. This change of names traces the deepening of a relationship. Today’s reader, too, may find that when faith is seen not as a list of duties but as a friendship, the very same service turns from a burden into a joy. (180:1.6)

II. The Vine and the Branches — The True Meaning of Prayer

Jesus compared himself to the true vine, the Father to the husbandman, and the apostles to the branches. Just as a branch separated from the vine withers, so they could not yield the fruits of loving service unless they abode in him. The vine is pruned only to increase its fruitfulness. The apostles recalled the large emblem of the grape and vine at the entrance to Herod’s temple and the old teaching that the Messiah would be “a stem arising out of the vine” of David’s ancestors. (180:2.1, 180:2.3)

Herein is the Father glorified: that the vine has many living branches, and that every branch bears much fruit. (180:2.1)

The narrator points out that misunderstanding of what was said here about prayer later caused great sorrow. Believers came to regard prayer in Jesus’ name as a sort of supreme magic for getting anything they asked, and for centuries honest souls have wrecked their faith on this stumbling block. Prayer is not a process of getting your way but a program of taking God’s way. When one’s will is truly aligned with his, one can ask anything conceived by that will-union. (180:2.4)

When the branch of the vine is alive, there can be only one answer to all its petitions: increased grape bearing. (180:2.5)

This parable corrects our expectations of prayer. Even when a living branch asks for selfish ease, the divine answer is more and more bearing of the fruits of the spirit. The Father’s hand of discipline, too, is laid on in love for the sake of fruit, and a wise husbandman cuts away only the dead branches. When an answer to prayer does not take the shape we wanted, the reader may consider that it could be an answer aimed at more fruit. (180:2.5, 180:2.6)

III. Enmity of the World — The Way, the Truth, and the Life

Jesus told them not to be discouraged if the world hated them; it had hated him first. They were in the world, but their lives were not to be worldlike; they were chosen out of the world to represent the spirit of another world to that very world. Those who had known the light and rejected it had no excuse, for the light that would save if accepted can only condemn if knowingly rejected. (180:3.1, 180:3.2)

Yet he promised not to leave them alone. In the Father’s universe there are many tarrying-places, and he would go before them and send for them. When Thomas said they did not know the way, Jesus answered that he was the way; when Philip asked to be shown the Father, Jesus answered that he who had seen him had seen the Father. (180:3.3, 180:3.4, 180:3.6, 180:3.8)

Thomas, I am the way, the truth, and the life. No man goes to the Father except through me. (180:3.7)

IV. The Promised Helper — Leaving in Order to Be Nearer

Jesus promised that after he went to the Father and received the final sovereignty of his domain, he would pour out the Spirit of Truth upon all flesh. Now he could be with them only in person, in one place; then he would be with all who desired his presence, wherever they were, and with each of them at the same time. That is why he said it was better for him to go away. (180:4.1)

Do you not discern that it is better for me to go away; that I leave you in the flesh so that I may the better and the more fully be with you in the spirit? (180:4.1)

In this new day the apostles would be indwelt by the Son as well as by the Father. The Spirit of Truth would guide and comfort them and bring to their remembrance all that the Master had taught. When Judas Alpheus, who seldom asked anything, made bold to put a question, Jesus smiled and answered that this spirit would live in the heart of each one and so draw all the children of light together. Judas did not fully understand, but he grasped the promise of the new teacher. (180:4.2, 180:4.3, 180:4.4, 180:4.5, 180:4.6)

V. The Spirit of Truth — Living Truth and the Golden Rule

In this section the narrator explains the nature of the Spirit of Truth. It is not the letter or law of truth but the conviction of truth, the consciousness and assurance of true meanings on real spirit levels. Truth can be known and lived, but it cannot be imprisoned in formulas or creeds. When humans undertake to formulate divine truth, it speedily dies. (180:5.1, 180:5.2)

Static truth is dead truth, and only dead truth can be held as a theory. (180:5.2)

The text takes the golden rule as its example. Read literally, it could lead someone to reason that because he wants others to speak their full minds to him, he should speak his full mind to them, a course that might bring no end of sorrow. People take the golden rule variously: as an intellectual affirmation, an emotional gratification, a social yardstick, or a moral injunction. But for the believer in the kingdom, it becomes a call so to relate oneself to others that they receive the highest possible good from that contact. (180:5.5, 180:5.6, 180:5.7)

Love works the same way. The text says that unselfish love must undergo a constant and living readaptation of relationships under the leading of the Spirit of Truth. Our concept of the highest good of the one we love keeps changing and enlarging, and love must take into account both the present evil environment and the eternal goal of divine destiny. Love is not to follow a fixed pattern set once and for all; as the other person grows, the shape of our love must grow too. (180:5.10)

The same principle applies to the teaching of nonresistance to evil. It is a spiritual pronouncement that combines nonresistance of all selfish reaction to the universe with the active pursuit of true spirit values. Neither the golden rule nor nonresistance can be rightly understood as dogma or precept; they can be comprehended only by living them in the living interpretation of the Spirit of Truth. (180:5.9, 180:5.11)

The old religion taught self-sacrifice; the new religion teaches only self-forgetfulness, enhanced self-realization in conjoined social service and universe comprehension. (180:5.12)

VI. The Necessity for Leaving — Sorrow Turned to Joy

Jesus warned that the hour would come when those who killed them would think they were doing a service to God. He told them in advance so that, when the time came, they might be strengthened by knowing that he had known everything and that his spirit was with them. Surprised that none of them asked why he was leaving, he told them plainly, as one friend to another, that his going was for their good. (180:6.1, 180:6.2)

I have yet much to say to you, but you cannot stand any more just now. (180:6.3)

Jesus said that the Spirit of Truth would not speak of himself but would declare what the Father had revealed to the Son. This spirit comes forth from the Son, reveals the Son’s truth, and glorifies the Son as the Son glorified the Father. Indwelling the believer, the spirit illuminates the difference between sin and righteousness and enables one to judge wisely in one’s own heart. The heart of this promise is that an inner discernment grows in place of external rules. (180:6.2, 180:6.4)

He explained the meaning of the parting with the figure of a woman in travail. Her anguish is forgotten in the joy of the child’s birth. The apostles would grieve at first, but when they saw him again, their sorrow would be turned into a rejoicing no one could take from them. He had spoken to them in parables because they were children in the spirit; when they were perfected in spirit growth, they would see the Father himself. Yet the eleven still could not get away from their idea of a material kingdom to be set up on earth. (180:6.7, 180:6.8, 180:6.9)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 180 shows what the last words of one who is leaving ought to be. Jesus left not rules but a measure of love; he promised not an institution but the living Spirit of Truth. He did not deny the sorrow of parting, yet he showed that it was the beginning of a deeper companionship. This paper moves faith from duty to friendship, and from form to living experience. (180:1.6, 180:5.2)
