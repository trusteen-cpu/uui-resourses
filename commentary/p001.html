Introduction — Why Begin with the Father?

If the Foreword lays out a map of terms, Paper 1 introduces the One at the center of that map. Before it describes the structure of the universe or the orders of celestial beings, The Urantia Book speaks of the Universal Father, because all things and beings come from him and the journey of every ascending creature leads back to him. The paper asks us to think of God first as a creator, then as a controller, and lastly as an infinite upholder. (1:0.1)

the God of all creation, the First Source and Center of all things and beings (1:0.1)

The God this paper portrays is no distant principle. The myriads of planetary systems were made to be inhabited by creatures who could know God, receive his affection, and love him in return, and the will creatures of universe upon universe have already set out on the long journey to attain the Father. So the opening of Paper 1 turns at once into a call addressed to us: be perfect, even as the Father in heaven is perfect. (1:0.2, 1:0.3)

This is the true meaning of that divine command, “Be you perfect, even as I am perfect,” which ever urges mortal man onward and beckons him inward (1:0.6)

The perfection meant here is not perfection in the infinite sense. Mortals, starting out where they are and moving in their own way, can still reach the divine goal that God has set for them. This perfection is less a flawless state than a direction—the direction of endless growth. (1:0.5)

I. The Father’s Name — What Matters More Than a Name

God is called by many names throughout the universes: the First Source, the Universe Center, the Father of Fathers, the Paradise Father, and others. Yet the paper makes a striking claim: God has never revealed himself by name, only by nature. He makes himself known not through a title but through what he is. (1:1.1, 1:1.5)

What, then, really matters? The paper’s answer is plain. Whatever name we use, the important thing is to know him and to long to become like him. (1:1.6)

The name he is given is of little importance; the significant thing is that you should know him and aspire to be like him. (1:1.6)

Nor does God impose formal worship or slavish service on anyone. Each person must, in his or her own heart, recognize, love, and freely worship him. This is why the paper calls the affectionate dedication of the human will to doing the Father’s will man’s choicest gift to God. Of all that we have, the only thing God does not already possess is our free choice. (1:1.2)

II. The Reality of God — How Do We Know Him?

God is primal reality in the spirit world, the source of truth in the mind spheres, and he overshadows all throughout the material realms. The paper states firmly that God is neither manlike nor machinelike. He is universal spirit, eternal truth, infinite reality, and father personality. (1:2.1)

How, then, do we know that God exists? Never by scientific experiment or by logical deduction. God can be realized only in the realms of human experience. The paper says that the presence of the Thought Adjuster dwelling within us is disclosed by three kinds of experience. (1:2.7, 1:2.3)

The surest evidence a believer can offer others, then, is not an argument but a life. The person who knows God carries in his or her own experience the only positive proof of the living God that one human being can give to another. (1:2.8)

III. God is a Universal Spirit

“God is spirit.” This classic confession stands at the center of the third section. To be created “in his image” does not mean that God has a body like ours; it means that Mystery Monitors sent from his presence dwell within us. Spirit is invisible to human eyes, yet it is entirely real. (1:3.1)

The section also explains, briefly, how survival works. A mind linked only to matter cannot survive death. But when a God-conscious mind becomes more and more spirit taught and spirit led, its spiritual potential is transformed into the morontia reality of the immortal soul. A mind that remains bound to matter to the end, by contrast, comes at last to personality extinction. (1:3.7)

This passage has very practical meaning for the life of faith. Survival does not depend on how much we know or how faithfully we keep religious rites. It depends on whether the mind accepts the leading of the spirit in the small choices we make each day. The Urantia Book therefore treats survival not as a question for some distant future but as one bound up with today’s decisions. Each time we choose honesty, choose kindness, choose trust over fear, the soul within us grows a little more. (1:3.7)

At the close of the section the Divine Counselor adds his own testimony. As one who came forth from the Eternal and has returned again and again to the presence of the Universal Father, he affirms that two great declarations are true. (1:3.8)

I know the truth of the great declarations: “God is spirit” and “God is love,” and these two attributes are most completely revealed to the universe in the Eternal Son. (1:3.8)

IV. The Mystery of God — God Within Us

Because God’s perfection is infinite, he remains forever a mystery. Yet the greatest mystery the paper names is not the stars or the vastness of the universe. It is the fact that divinity dwells within the minds of mortals. (1:4.1)

The physical bodies of mortals are “the temples of God.” (1:4.2)

The fragment of God within us is not yet ours by right of possession, but it is designed to become one with us if we survive. When our course on earth is run, the dust returns to the earth from which it came, and the spirit returns to God who gave it. (1:4.3)

God, moreover, is no respecter of persons. The divine presence any creature enjoys is not limited by favoritism on God’s part, but only by that creature’s capacity to receive and discern spiritual realities. If we want to meet more of God, it is not God who must change; we must widen our capacity to receive him. (1:4.6)

V–VI. Personality of the Universal Father; Personality in the Universe

We must not let God’s infinity obscure his personality. The paper says that the Universal Father is the acme of divine personality, the origin and destiny of all personality throughout creation. God is both infinite and personal; he is an infinite personality. (1:5.1)

Personality, in the supreme sense, is the revelation of God to the universe of universes. (1:5.13)

This personal God takes part in our lives at the deepest level. His spirit is a real part of us, so that when we are afflicted he is afflicted, and when we triumph he triumphs in and with us. God is not an indifferent observer; he is a Father who shares what we go through. (1:5.16)

The sixth section sets out how differently people understand God. The same God appears as a cause, an idea, or a living person, depending on where one stands. (1:6.2)

The section also tells us who has shown God’s personality most clearly. Jesus of Nazareth attained the full realization of the potential of spirit personality in human experience, and his life of doing the Father’s will became man’s most real and ideal revelation of the personality of God. If we want to know what God is like, we can look at the life of Jesus. (1:6.8)

VII. Spiritual Value of the Personality Concept

The final section explains why the idea of a personal God matters so much. The concept of a personal God makes fellowship possible, encourages intelligent worship, and nurtures a refreshing trust. Impersonal things can interact, but they cannot have fellowship. (1:7.1)

For this reason the paper corrects a familiar image from mysticism. We do not become one with God the way a drop of water is lost in the ocean. We are united with him through personal communion and wholehearted conformity to his will, gradually taking on more and more of the divine nature. (1:7.2)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 1 is less a body of information about God than an invitation into a relationship with him. Its message can be gathered into three points. God is a person whom we can know and love. He already lives within us as spirit. And the one true gift we can offer him is our free choice to do his will.

After Paper 1, the papers that follow unfold in turn the nature of God (Paper 2), his attributes (Paper 3), and his relation to the universe and to the individual (Papers 4 and 5). If we read them with the picture from this first paper in mind—a Father who can be known and loved—we will sense that even the harder concepts ahead all point toward this one Person.
