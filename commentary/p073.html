Introduction — A Home Prepared Before They Came

Paper 73 begins the story of Adam and Eve not with their arrival but with the home made ready to receive them. The Caligastia downfall brought cultural decadence and spiritual poverty, yet organic evolution went forward regardless. Almost forty thousand years ago the Life Carriers saw that the biologic progress of the Urantia races was nearing its apex, and together with the Melchizedek receivers they petitioned the Most Highs of Edentia to send a Material Son and Daughter. (73:0.1, 73:0.2)

Tabamantia, sovereign supervisor of the series of experimental worlds, came to inspect the planet and duly recommended the request; in a little less than one hundred years Adam and Eve arrived. The paper is presented by Solonia, the seraphic “voice in the Garden.” It shows how, on a world retarded by rebellion and resting under spiritual isolation, a small company of loyal people prepared the way for one who had not yet come. (73:0.3, 73:7.5)

I. The Nodites and the Amadonites — Two Surviving Streams of Culture

On a normal planet the arrival of the Material Son heralds a great age of invention, material progress, and intellectual enlightenment—but not so on Urantia. Ten thousand years after the rebellion practically all the gains of the Prince’s administration had been effaced, and the tribes languished in savagery and moral stagnation. The traditions of Dalamatia and the culture of the Planetary Prince persisted among only two groups. (73:1.1, 73:1.2)

After the destruction of Dalamatia the followers of Nod divided into three groups, settling near the headwaters of the Persian Gulf, in the highlands of Elam, and on the northeastern Syrian shores of the Mediterranean. They mated freely with the Sangik races and left an able progeny, and some of them, near Lake Van and the southern Caspian region, mingled with the Amadonites and were numbered among the “mighty men of old.” (73:1.5, 73:1.6)

A traditional enmity ran between the two groups, surfacing whenever they tried a common enterprise, and later in the affairs of Eden it was exceedingly difficult for them to work together in peace. Even so, before Adam and Eve came these were the most advanced peoples on earth. That even the keepers of culture could not be reconciled with one another shows that progress and harmony do not necessarily arrive together. (73:1.4, 73:1.7)

Prior to the arrival of Adam and Eve these groups—Nodites and Amadonites—were the most advanced and cultured races on earth. (73:1.7)

II. Planning for the Garden — A Preparation Begun in Faith

Van knew that the Material Sons of Jerusem always lived in simple but charming garden homes. So, eighty-three years before the arrival of Adam and Eve, he proposed to his associates that they proclaim the advent and prepare a garden home for the newcomers. From the highland headquarters and sixty-one scattered settlements came more than three thousand workers, and Van divided them into one hundred companies, each with its captain. (73:2.2, 73:2.3, 73:2.4)

in solemn assembly, dedicated themselves to this mission of preparing for the promised—at least expected—Son. (73:2.3)

There was opposition. Caligastia and Daligastia, though deprived of much of their power for evil, did everything possible to frustrate the work. But the almost ten thousand loyal midway creatures labored tirelessly and largely offset their machinations. Beside the visible workers, unseen helpers were working too. (73:2.5)

III. The Garden Site — A Mediterranean Peninsula, Almost an Island

After nearly three years of searching, the committee on location reported on three possible sites: an island in the Persian Gulf, the river location later occupied as the second garden, and a long, narrow peninsula projecting westward from the eastern shores of the Mediterranean. The committee almost unanimously chose the third, and two years were spent moving the world’s cultural headquarters, including the tree of life, to the new site. (73:3.1, 73:3.2)

Encircled by mountains and set like an island in an inland sea, the peninsula enjoyed a healthful climate and an even temperature. It seldom rained in Eden itself, but each night a mist rose from the irrigation channels to refresh the vegetation. The great river of the Garden flowed east, fed by four tributaries rising in the coastal hills—the “four heads” of the river that went out of Eden. The mountains held precious stones and metals, yet these received little attention; the dominant idea was to glorify horticulture and exalt agriculture. (73:3.3, 73:3.4, 73:3.5)

Eden was the one bright spot on Urantia (73:3.6)

IV–V. Establishing the Garden; The Garden Home — A Garden, and Only a Garden

Van gave the whole peninsula to the Garden and assigned pasturage and animal husbandry to the adjoining mainland. No animals were ever slaughtered within the Garden’s precincts. The first task was a brick wall across the neck of the peninsula; a smaller wall outside it enclosed wild beasts as an added defense. Every worker was a volunteer—no hirelings were ever used—and they supported themselves by cultivating the Garden and tending their herds. (73:4.1, 73:4.2, 73:4.3, 73:4.4)

Van’s instructions were that Eden was to be a garden, and only a garden. (73:4.1)

The most human moment comes when Van suggests training the younger generation in case the expected Son and Daughter are delayed. To many this sounded like an admission of lack of faith; it caused great disappointment and many desertions. Yet Van went forward with his plan of preparedness, filling the vacant places with younger volunteers. True faith not only holds fast to its hope; it also has the faithfulness to prepare for that hope’s delay. (73:4.5)

When Adam came the Garden was only one-fourth finished, yet it had thousands of miles of irrigation ditches, more than twelve thousand miles of paved paths and roads, and just over five thousand brick buildings. Its sanitary arrangements were far ahead of anything yet attempted on Urantia, and Amadon’s inspectors made daily rounds in search of possible causes of sickness. Most of the Garden was deliberately left in a more or less natural state, since it was thought best to finish it according to Adam’s own ideas. (73:5.2, 73:5.3, 73:5.4, 73:5.6)

By Adam’s arrival most of the plants of that part of the world were growing in Eden, and many fruits, cereals, and nuts had already been greatly improved. Many modern vegetables and cereals were first cultivated here. Adam and Eve were well pleased with the general plan of Eden, though they made many changes in the furnishings of their own dwelling. (73:5.5, 73:5.7)

Never before this time nor after has Urantia harbored such a beautiful and replete exhibition of horticulture and agriculture. (73:5.8)

VI. The Tree of Life — Not a Myth but a Gift of Edentia

In the center of the Garden temple Van planted the long-guarded tree of life. The text draws a clear distinction: the “tree of the knowledge of good and evil” may be a figure of speech, but the tree of life was real. It was a shrub of Edentia, sent to Urantia by the Melchizedeks when Caligastia was commissioned as Planetary Prince. (73:6.1, 73:6.3)

The “tree of the knowledge of good and evil” may be a figure of speech, a symbolic designation covering a multitude of human experiences, but the “tree of life” was not a myth (73:6.3)

The tree stored space-energies antidotal to the age-producing elements of animal existence, but it was wholly useless to ordinary evolutionary beings. Its fruit served the materialized members of the Prince’s staff and the modified Andonites who possessed the complement of life, and Adam and Eve too ate of it periodically to maintain their dual form of physical life. So when the Nodites later invaded Eden and ate freely of the fruit for years with no effect, they became enraged, and in one of their internal wars the temple and the tree were both destroyed by fire. (73:6.4, 73:6.6, 73:6.7)

Adam, Eve, their children, and their children’s children all perished in the course of time and so came under the ascension scheme of the local universe, in which mansion world resurrection follows material death. The loss of the tree of life was not an ending; it meant that they entered the same path of ascension that every mortal walks. (73:6.8)

VII. The Fate of Eden — A Garden Reclaimed by Nature

After Adam left, the first garden was occupied in turn by Nodites, Cutites, and Suntites, and was overrun for almost four thousand years. Then, with the violent activity of the surrounding volcanoes and the submergence of the Sicilian land bridge to Africa, the eastern floor of the Mediterranean sank and carried the whole peninsula beneath the waters. The sinking was not sudden; it took several hundred years. (73:7.1)

We cannot regard this disappearance of the Garden as being in any way a result of the miscarriage of the divine plans or as a result of the mistakes of Adam and Eve. (73:7.2)

The Garden was never meant to be the permanent home of the Adamites. The Melchizedeks counseled Adam not to begin racial uplift until his family numbered half a million, and then to establish racial and continental headquarters under his sons and daughters. The Adamites were destined to go out as emissaries of new life, giving themselves unselfishly to the needy races of earth. (73:7.3, 73:7.4)

They were to become emissaries of a new life to all the world (73:7.3)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

The heroes of Paper 73 are not Adam and Eve but the people who waited for them. Van, Amadon, and thousands of volunteers worked for more than eighty years with no fixed date, and when some deserted they trained the next generation and carried on. The Garden they built was finally submerged, yet the text calls this a natural occurrence, not a failure. The worth of preparation lies not in its results lasting forever but in faithfully answering the call of one’s own time. (73:2.2, 73:4.5, 73:7.2)

Paper 74 turns to Adam and Eve, now arrived, and their first days in Eden. Remembering the labor of preparation seen in this paper helps us weigh more fully the expectations and misunderstandings they met, and the weight of the plan they had come to carry out.
