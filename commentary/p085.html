Introduction — Primitive Religion Born of Fear and Illusion

After three papers on marriage and the home, Paper 85 turns to the earliest roots of religion. Its author, a Brilliant Evening Star of Nebadon, says that primitive religion was a natural evolutionary development with a biologic origin, apart from moral associations and spiritual influences. Animals have fears too, but they have no religion because they have no illusions. (85:0.1, 85:7.4)

Man creates his primitive religions out of his fears and by means of his illusions. (85:0.1)

The primitive manifestations of worship appeared long before any complex ideas of life here and hereafter. The objects of worship were things in nature that were near at hand or impressive. Man feared every manifestation of power he could not understand, and storms, floods, earthquakes, and volcanoes made a deep impression on his expanding mind. Only when religion rose above nature worship did it gain roots of spiritual origin. (85:0.2, 85:0.3, 85:0.4)

The inexplicable things of life are still termed "acts of God" and "mysterious dispensations of Providence." (85:0.4)

I–II. Worship of Stones and Hills; Worship of Plants and Trees

The first object evolving man worshiped was the stone. To someone who did not understand erosion, a stone that suddenly turned up in a field was a mystery, and a meteor streaking across the sky looked like the passage of a spirit. Jacob anointing a stone and Rachel hiding sacred stones belong to the long continuation of this awe. Stone worship was followed by hill worship: mountains became the dwelling places of good spirits and gods, and caves the entrances to the world of evil spirits. (85:1.1, 85:1.2, 85:1.5)

Such fetishes are many times mere symbols of the real object of worship. (85:1.3)

This sentence is also a caution against looking down on primitive people too easily. Simple mortals did not always identify the stone itself with Deity. Plants were at first feared and then worshiped because of the intoxicating liquors made from them, for intoxication was believed to make a person divine. Tree cults are among the oldest religious groups, and all early marriages were solemnized under trees. (85:1.3, 85:2.1, 85:2.3)

The nature of the spirit dwelling in a tree differed from people to people. The Finns believed most trees were occupied by kind spirits, the Swiss distrusted trees as harboring crafty spirits, and the peoples of India and eastern Russia regarded tree spirits as cruel. Except in China, there was once a universal cult of the tree of life. That people could imagine such different characters for the same object shows that the content of worship came more from the human mind than from the object. (85:2.4)

The author adds that even amid this jumble of nature worship, the earliest worship, activated by the adjutant mind-spirit, was already functioning long before man’s religious nature could respond fully to spiritual influences. However superstitious the surface, a stimulus from above was flowing beneath it. (85:2.6)

III. The Worship of Animals — Symbol and Idol

Primitive man felt a peculiar kinship with the higher animals and stood in awe of their strength and cunning. Every animal was at some time worshiped by some race, and half-human, half-animal beings such as centaurs and mermaids were worshiped as well. The Hebrews worshiped serpents down to the days of Hezekiah, the Chinese worship of the dragon is a relic of the serpent cult, and the wisdom of the serpent became the symbol of Greek medicine. (85:3.1, 85:3.2, 85:3.3)

The worship of insects and other animals was also promoted by a misapplication of the golden rule to every form of life. Animal symbols often point to forgotten gods or vanished cults. It was in this era too that the lamb became the sacrificial animal and the dove the symbol of peace and love. (85:3.4)

symbolism must not be confused with direct idolatry wherein the material object is directly and actually worshiped. (85:3.5)

The author holds that a symbol may be good or bad depending on whether it displaces the original idea of worship. When a symbol such as a candle becomes a window that leads the mind to God, it helps; when it becomes an end in itself, it slides into idolatry. Believers today can examine their own symbols by this standard. (85:3.5)

IV. Worship of the Elements — Water and Wind, Thunder and Fire

Mankind has worshiped earth, air, water, and fire. Bubbling springs and rushing streams strongly suggested spiritual vitality, and sometimes a drowning person was not rescued for fear of offending the river god. Thunder was taken for the voice of an angry god, and fire worship reached its height in Persia. Belief in nature spirits was strong enough to survive into Hebrew theology in Moses’ day as angels of fire, water, and air. (85:4.1, 85:4.2, 85:4.3, 85:4.4)

That the same phenomenon could be a blessing in one region and a curse in another shows that the objects of primitive religion were determined less by their own nature than by people’s living conditions and fears. The author also explains why magic survived so long. (85:4.2)

A devotee of magic will vividly remember one positive chance result in the practice of his magic formulas, while he nonchalantly forgets a score of negative results, out-and-out failures. (85:4.4)

This observation still holds today. We remember the hunch that came true and quickly forget the ones that missed. Superstition grows on this selective memory even more than on ignorance. Honest faith begins with a mind that does not forget its failures.

V. Worship of the Heavenly Bodies — From Moon to Sun

The worship of stones, trees, and animals passed through reverence for the elements to the deification of sun, moon, and stars. Moon worship reached its height in the hunting era, and sun worship became the chief rite of the agricultural age. Sun worship first took root in India and lasted longest there; in Persia it gave rise to the Mithraic cult, and later civilizations named the first day of the week after the sun. In India and elsewhere the stars were thought of as the glorified souls of great men who had left the life of the flesh, and the Chaldean star cultists regarded themselves as children of the sky father and the earth mother. (85:5.1, 85:5.2)

The sun-god was regarded as the mystic father of virgin-born sons of destiny. These babies were believed to be set adrift on some sacred river, miraculously rescued, and to grow up to become deliverers of their people. The author records plainly that such savior traditions grew out of the worship of the heavenly bodies. (85:5.3)

VI. Worship of Man — Gods in Human Form

Having worshiped everything on earth and in the sky, man at last worshiped himself. The savage made no clear distinction between beasts, men, and gods. Extraordinary persons were regarded as superhuman, those with mental disorders as indwelt by a god, and priests, kings, and prophets were worshiped too. Dead chiefs were deified, and in later times distinguished souls were sainted. (85:6.1, 85:6.2, 85:6.3)

The author says that unaided evolution never produced gods higher than the glorified spirits of deceased humans. Man worship reached its peak when rulers demanded veneration from their subjects and claimed descent from the gods. This is also an old warning that religion can become a tool of power. (85:6.3, 85:6.5)

The ghost gods that arose from human beings must be distinguished from the nature gods, nature spirits elevated to the status of deity. The nature cult and the later ghost cult developed side by side and influenced each other, and many religious systems embraced both concepts. Sometimes the two were intertwined in a single god, as in Thor, a ghost hero who was also master of the lightning. (85:6.4)

Evolutionary religion creates its gods in the image and likeness of mortal man; revelatory religion seeks to evolve and transform mortal man into the image and likeness of God. (85:6.3)

VII. The Adjutants of Worship and Wisdom

Nature worship seemed to arise spontaneously in the minds of primitive men, and in a sense it did. Yet all along the sixth adjutant, the spirit of worship, was constantly stimulating the human urge to worship. Animal fear may have triggered worship and its objects may have been natural things, but the worship urge itself had a definite origin. (85:7.1)

To the primitive mind there is little difference between fearing, shunning, honoring, and worshiping. (85:7.2)

When the worship urge is admonished and directed by wisdom—meditative and experiential thinking—it then begins to develop into the phenomenon of real religion. (85:7.3)

The author asks us to remember that feeling, not thinking, dominated all evolutionary development. The desire to worship was there from the beginning, but what it turned toward changed as wisdom grew. The maturing of worship, then, does not lie in discarding feeling but in letting feeling be guided by thought born of meditation and experience. When awe grows beyond fear into understanding of the Creator, primitive worship becomes real religion. (85:7.2, 85:7.3)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 85 is a catalog of the things mankind has worshiped, and at the same time a testimony that the urge to worship was planted in man from the beginning. Mingled with fear and illusion, that urge turned to stones, trees, and animals, but when wisdom guides it, it turns from nature to the Creator of nature. Relics that survive today, such as the Maypole and the Christmas tree, candles and tombstones, are footprints of that long journey. (85:1.4, 85:2.5)
