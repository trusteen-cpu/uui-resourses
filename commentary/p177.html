Introduction — One Day, Two Roads

When the work of teaching did not press them, Jesus and his apostles rested from their labors every Wednesday. On this Wednesday morning an ominous silence hung over the camp. Jesus told the apostles to rest, to think over everything that had happened since they came to Jerusalem, and to meditate on what lay just ahead. He added only one restriction: under no circumstances were they to go within the gates of the city. (177:0.1, 177:0.2)

Make sure that the truth abides in your lives, and that you daily grow in grace. (177:0.1)

This paper records two people who went in opposite directions on the same day. A boy went up into the hills with the Master and spent the day in fellowship with God; an apostle ignored the Master’s request, went down into the city, and agreed to hand him over. Between them, the remaining apostles endured a day without their Master and passed through the lowest point of their discouragement. This contrast is the key to reading the whole paper. (177:1.3, 177:4.1, 177:5.5)

I. One Day Alone with God — The Boy Who Would Not Let Go of the Basket

When Jesus made ready to go into the hills alone, David Zebedee wanted to send three armed men with him. Jesus declined the escort: no man would lay hands on him until the hour when he was ready to lay down his life in conformity to his Father’s will. Then John Mark came forward with a small basket of food and water. (177:0.3, 177:0.4)

As Jesus reached down to take the basket, the boy held on to it and said that if he came along to carry the lunch, the Master would be more free to worship. He even promised to be silent and ask no questions. His boldness astonished some who stood near, but Jesus saw the whole heart of the lad and took him along. (177:1.1, 177:1.2)

Since with all your heart you crave to go with me, it shall not be denied you. (177:1.2)
Forever this occasion exemplifies the willingness of the Creator to fellowship the creature. (177:1.3)

What stands out in this scene is not the boy’s qualifications but his desire. John Mark regretted that he had not been old enough to be one of the apostles. Yet he had followed the group almost constantly since their first preaching at the Jordan ford near Jericho, and that steady love led to this day’s companionship. It is a comfort to every reader that fellowship with God is open not according to rank or age but according to the longing of the heart. (177:1.4)

This day became known on high as “the day which a young man spent with God in the hills.” The text says that even a youth, if the desire of the heart is really supreme, can command the attention and enjoy the loving companionship of the God of a universe. Jesus told the lad not to become discouraged by impending events and assured him he would live to become a mighty messenger of the kingdom. John Mark never revealed what he heard that day, and in the hours that remained he never let the Master out of his sight for long. (177:1.3, 177:1.4, 177:1.5, 177:1.6)

II. Early Home Life — Love Ruled by Wisdom

Much of the day’s conversation was spent comparing the childhood of the two. When John asked how he could know he would become a mighty messenger of the kingdom, Jesus pointed to his home as the ground on which his faith and love could be relied upon: parents who bore each other a sincere affection, and parents who possessed wisdom as well as love. (177:2.1, 177:2.2)

Jesus offered John’s friend Amos as a contrast. Amos’s parents loved their son so much that they refused to let him come along. Amos believed the gospel just as much as John, yet Jesus said he could not fully depend on him, because his early home life was not such as would produce a wholly dependable person. John, by contrast, had spent his first eight years in a home where love prevailed and wisdom reigned, and so he had the kind of loyalty that would go through to the end. (177:2.2, 177:2.4)

When you get married and have children of your own to rear, make sure that your love is admonished by wisdom and guided by intelligence. (177:2.3)

Jesus explained that a child depends almost entirely on the home for his early concepts of everything intellectual, social, moral, and even spiritual. The child gets his first impressions of the universe from the mother’s care and his first ideas of the heavenly Father from the earthly father. The narrator adds that the gospel, founded as it is on the father-child relationship, can hardly enjoy world-wide acceptance until home life embraces more of love and more of wisdom. Modern homes have gained freedom, he notes, but that liberty is not restrained by love, motivated by loyalty, or directed by the intelligent discipline of wisdom. (177:2.5, 177:2.6, 177:2.7)

Such a home life enhances religion, and genuine religion always glorifies the home. (177:2.6)

III. The Day at Camp — The Empty Place

The apostles walked about visiting the disciples’ tents, but by afternoon they were anxious for the Master’s safety and felt inexpressibly lonely without him. No one said so openly, yet every one of them except Judas wished himself in John Mark’s place. Nathaniel spoke to a few of them on “Supreme Desire” and confessed that their trouble was that they were halfhearted. His remark that only the boy who would not let go of the basket went with the Master sums up the lesson of this paper: the strength of desire determines the companionship. (177:3.1, 177:3.2)

What is wrong with most of us is that we are only halfhearted. (177:3.2)

Meanwhile David Zebedee, convinced that the rulers were going to kill Jesus, had sent for his mother and asked her to bring Mary and every member of Jesus’ family to Jerusalem. Because he had done this on his own initiative, he told no one. More than twenty of the Greeks who had met Jesus at the home of Joseph of Arimathea also came to the camp. Jesus visited with them that evening, and had it not been for the disturbance it would have caused his apostles, he would have ordained them as he had the seventy. In the city, the chief priests wondered why Jesus did not return to the temple, while they prepared for the Passover and perfected their plans to destroy him. (177:3.3, 177:3.4, 177:3.5, 177:3.6, 177:3.7)

IV. Judas and the Chief Priests — Not Money, but a Wounded Self

Shortly after Jesus and John Mark left, Judas ignored the Master’s request and hurried to the home of Caiaphas. An informal meeting of the Sanhedrin was being held there to decide on the charges against Jesus and the procedure for securing Roman confirmation of his death sentence. The day before, Judas had told relatives and Sadducean friends that Jesus was a well-meaning dreamer but not the deliverer of Israel, and they had flattered him with the promise of high honors. (177:4.1, 177:4.2)

The text examines Judas’s motives layer by layer and states plainly that he did not go to Caiaphas for money. Introduced by his cousin, Judas asked the high priest what he would give for this service, and he failed to notice the disdain on Caiaphas’s face. They agreed to arrest Jesus on Thursday night, after the pilgrims had retired. (177:4.4, 177:4.6, 177:4.7, 177:4.8)

the other apostles likewise craved this same worldly honor in their minds, but with their hearts they loved Jesus and were doing their best to learn to love the truths which he taught them. (177:4.10)

This passage weighs heavily precisely because it does not paint Judas as a monster. The early ambition was shared by all the apostles. The difference lay in whether that ambition was overcome by love in the heart or fed by disappointment and resentment. Once Judas separated himself from the support of his brethren and exposed himself to the insinuations of his enemies, the long-accumulated wounds were ready to engulf him. Here the reader may take warning about how small, unresolved hurts can grow. (177:4.5, 177:4.11)

Judas returned to camp intoxicated with thoughts of grandeur such as he had not had for many a day. He rejoiced that he had been shrewd enough to trade the glory he failed to win in the new kingdom for honor and reward in the old order. The text judges that, in its last motive of conscious intention, his betrayal was the cowardly act of a selfish deserter who thought only of his own safety and glory. Jesus loved and trusted Judas just as he did the other apostles, but Judas failed to return that trust with love. The chief priests now had a way to take Jesus without arresting him in public. (177:4.9, 177:4.10, 177:4.12)

And every mortal man knows full well how love, even when once genuine, can, through disappointment, jealousy, and long-continued resentment, be eventually turned into actual hate. (177:4.11)

V. The Last Social Hour — Beware the Support of the Multitude

Wednesday evening was a social hour, but the apostles could not be cheered. Jesus asked carefully about each of their families, and David Zebedee looked down at his feet, afraid to answer. On this occasion Jesus warned them not to be deceived by the crowds in the temple who seemed to believe their teaching. Those who know the gospel only in the mind and have not experienced it in the heart cannot be depended on when real trouble comes. (177:5.1, 177:5.2)

Watch, therefore, and pray that on the morrow you may be strengthened for the days that are just ahead. (177:5.2)

The text records this Wednesday evening as the low-tide mark of the apostles’ spiritual status up to the hour of the Master’s death. Even his good cheer frightened them. Just before midnight, knowing this was the last night he would sleep through with his chosen family, Jesus sent them to rest and called the coming day one more day to do the Father’s will. (177:5.4, 177:5.5, 177:5.6)

Conclusion — Living This Paper

Paper 177 shows how far human choices can diverge within a single quiet day. Among those who followed the same Master, a boy stood beside him out of longing, and an apostle left him out of resentment. The teaching on the home reminds us how early the roots of such a parting are laid. Readers can examine both the warmth of their own desire and any resentment stored in their hearts. (177:2.5, 177:4.10)
